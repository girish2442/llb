Official unit scope: Offences against State (ss.147–158); election, coin, currency notes and government stamps (ss.168–188); public tranquillity—unlawful assembly, rioting, affray, promoting enmity, prejudicial assertions (ss.189–197); public justice—false/fabricated evidence, destruction of evidence, harbouring offender (ss.227–238); public nuisance (ss.270–273); mischief/criminal trespass (ss.324–334); forgery and property marks (ss.335–344).

Statutory map: BNS ss.147–158, 168–188, 189–197, 227–238, 270–273, 324–344.

Study each topic through definition → ingredients → exceptions/defences → punishment → example → case principle. Check the exact current wording in the Bare Act before reproducing a section verbatim.

Offences against the State

Sections 147–158 include waging/attempting/abetting war against Government of India, conspiracy, collecting arms, concealing design, assaulting high constitutional authorities and acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity. Section 152 addresses acts endangering sovereignty, unity and integrity of India. Distinguish violent war/insurrection offences from speech-related conduct; read the exact statutory language and constitutional protections under Article 19.

Simple example: Example: an organised armed attack aimed at overthrowing the government is qualitatively different from peaceful criticism.

Election offences

Sections 168–174 include candidate/public servant election-related offences, bribery, undue influence, personation and false statements, subject to the definitions and exceptions. The remaining syllabus range ss.175–188 covers coin, currency notes, bank-notes and government stamps. Identify the protected electoral process or instrument and the precise prohibited conduct.

Simple example: Example: offering a voter money to influence their vote may amount to electoral bribery.

Public tranquillity

Sections 189–197 cover unlawful assembly, common object liability, rioting, liability of members, affray, promoting enmity between groups and assertions prejudicial to national integration. Unlawful assembly requires five or more persons and a statutorily prohibited common object; rioting requires force/violence by an unlawful assembly or member in prosecution of common object.

Simple example: Distinguish unlawful assembly from mere gathering; common object and statutory purpose matter.

Public justice and evidence

Sections 227–238 cover giving/fabricating false evidence, punishment for false evidence, threats to induce false evidence, screening offender, destruction of evidence, false information and harbouring offender. Fabrication involves creating circumstances/false entries intended to cause an authority to form an erroneous opinion on a material point.

Simple example: Example: knowingly creating a false document for use in court may amount to fabrication/forgery depending on ingredients.

Public nuisance

Sections 270–273 address public nuisance and related consequences/continuance. Public nuisance affects the public or a class of people, not merely one private individual. Compare with private nuisance, which is a tort concerning interference with use/enjoyment of land.

Simple example: Example: blocking a public street in a way that affects the community may constitute public nuisance.

Mischief and criminal trespass

Sections 324–334 address mischief and forms of aggravated mischief; criminal trespass and house-trespass/breaking are within the range. Mischief requires intention or knowledge of causing wrongful loss/damage and destruction/change diminishing value or utility. Criminal trespass requires entry upon property in another’s possession with intent to commit an offence or intimidate, insult or annoy, or unlawful remaining with such intent.

Simple example: Ownership is not always decisive: possession is central to criminal trespass.

Forgery and property marks

Sections 335–344 address making a false document/electronic record, forgery, aggravated forgery and property marks. Forgery generally requires making a false document/record with one of the specified fraudulent/dishonest intents. Distinguish a false statement from making a false document.

Simple example: A forged signature placed on a deed with intent to cause it to be believed genuine may satisfy the definition.

Exam writing checklist

  1. Start with the legal concept and correct BNS section.
  2. Set out each ingredient separately.
  3. Explain exceptions, provisos and statutory explanations.
  4. Apply the facts or give a short illustration.
  5. State punishment only after checking the exact subsection.
  6. Use a case for its principle and identify if it was decided under IPC.
