Nature and conception of crime

A crime is conduct prohibited by law and made punishable by the State. It is a public wrong: prosecution is ordinarily brought in the name of the State. Distinguish crime from tort (private civil remedy), breach of contract (failure to perform a consensual obligation), and moral wrong (social disapproval without necessarily being unlawful). The same act may create both criminal and civil liability.

Simple example: Example: A deliberately assaults B. A may face prosecution and also pay civil compensation.

Actus reus, mens rea and causation

Actus reus is the external element: conduct, legally relevant omission, circumstance or consequence. Mens rea is the mental element required by the offence—intention, knowledge, dishonesty, fraudulence or negligence as specified. The prosecution must prove every ingredient beyond reasonable doubt. Causation connects conduct to prohibited consequence. Not every offence requires the same mental state.

Simple example: Example: causing death by a deliberate knife attack differs from an accidental death during a lawful activity.

Maxims and stages

Actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea; ignorantia facti excusat, ignorantia juris non excusat; volunti non fit injuria is not a universal criminal defence. Stages: intention, preparation, attempt, commission. Mere intention is generally not punishable; preparation is usually not punishable unless specifically criminalised; attempt is punishable under s.62 where applicable.

Simple example: Example: buying a tool may be preparation; using it to break a lock and enter may cross into attempt or completed offence depending on facts.

Parties and participation

Principal offender; abettor (ss.45–60 in Unit II); conspirator (s.61); person acting with common intention or common object where relevant provisions apply; aiders and participants. Liability depends on the statutory mode of participation and proof of intention/knowledge. Do not treat presence alone as automatic guilt.

Simple example: Example: a lookout who intentionally facilitates a planned robbery may incur liability if the legal ingredients are proved.

BNS scope and definitions

BNS is Act 45 of 2023, in force from 1 July 2024. Section 1 deals with title, commencement and application; s.2 definitions; s.3 general explanations. It replaces IPC for offences committed after commencement, subject to constitutional non-retroactivity and repeal/savings rules. Section 358 repeals IPC while preserving prior liabilities/proceedings as provided.

Simple example: Temporal rule: identify date of conduct first; do not apply BNS retrospectively to an earlier offence where it would impose greater liability.

Punishments and sentencing

Section 4 lists death, imprisonment for life, imprisonment (rigorous/simple), forfeiture of property, fine and community service. Sections 5–13 address commutation, fractions, rigorous/simple form, fine and default, limits for multiple offences, uncertain conviction among offences, solitary confinement and enhanced punishment after previous conviction. Solitary confinement is regulated, not an unlimited free-standing punishment. Sentencing must follow the offence provision, statutory limits and constitutional safeguards.

Simple example: Life imprisonment ordinarily means the remainder of natural life unless lawfully remitted/commuted; it does not automatically mean 14 years.

General exceptions

Sections 14–44 include mistake of fact in specified good-faith circumstances; judicial acts and court orders; accident; necessity; infancy; unsoundness of mind; involuntary intoxication; consent-related exceptions; good-faith acts; compulsion; slight harm; and private defence. Each exception has conditions and exclusions. The accused bears the applicable evidential burden; prosecution retains the ultimate burden to prove guilt.

Simple example: Example: private defence can justify necessary defensive force, but not revenge after danger ends.

Exam writing checklist

  1. Start with the legal concept and correct BNS section.
  2. Set out each ingredient separately.
  3. Explain exceptions, provisos and statutory explanations.
  4. Apply the facts or give a short illustration.
  5. State punishment only after checking the exact subsection.
  6. Use a case for its principle and identify if it was decided under IPC.
