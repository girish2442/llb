Q01UNIT I · Introduction & Formation
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Define proposal/offer. Explain the essentials of a valid proposal and circumstances in which it lapses.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024 (proposal); Jan/May 2025 re-exam (proposal and lapse); Jan/Feb 2026 papers (offer).

Study model answer

Meaning — Section 2(a), Indian Contract Act, 1872: When one person signifies to another his willingness to do or abstain from doing something, with a view to obtaining the other’s assent, he makes a proposal.

Essentials of a valid offer

  1. Clear intention to create legal relations: a social invitation is normally not an offer.
  2. Definite and certain terms: essential terms must not be vague (Section 29).
  3. Communicated to the offeree: an unknown offer cannot be accepted.
  4. Made with a view to obtaining assent: it must show willingness to be bound on acceptance.
  5. May be express or implied (Section 9), and may be made to a specific person or the public generally.
  6. Must be distinguished from an invitation to offer: advertisements of goods, catalogues, display of goods and auction notices usually invite offers, depending on wording and context.

When an offer lapses or ends

  • On expiry of the fixed time, or after a reasonable time if none is fixed.
  • By communication of revocation before acceptance becomes complete against the proposer (Sections 5–6).
  • By rejection or a counter-offer.
  • On failure of a condition precedent.
  • On death or insanity of proposer, if the fact comes to the acceptor’s knowledge before acceptance.
  • By a prescribed mode not followed where the proposer insists on it and does not waive the defect.

Cases: Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt — a person cannot accept an offer without knowledge of it. Harvey v. Facey — merely stating the lowest price is not necessarily an offer. Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. — an advertisement can be a unilateral offer when it shows clear commitment.

Conclusion: A valid offer must be definite, communicated and intended to obtain assent. It remains open only until accepted or lawfully terminated.

Q02UNIT I · Introduction & Formation
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain the rules and essentials of valid acceptance, including communication and revocation.

Past papers: June/July 2025; offer/acceptance also appears in Mar/Apr 2024 and other sessions.

Study model answer

Section 2(b): A proposal, when the person to whom it is made signifies his assent, is accepted and becomes a promise.

  1. Absolute and unqualified: acceptance must match the offer; a material change is a counter-offer (Section 7).
  2. By the proper person: the offeree or authorised agent must accept.
  3. In the prescribed or usual/reasonable manner: if the proposer prescribes a mode, it should be followed (Section 7).
  4. Within time: acceptance must be made before expiry or within a reasonable time.
  5. While offer is open: acceptance after rejection, revocation or lapse is ineffective.
  6. Knowledge of offer: the offeree must know of the offer; see Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt.
  7. May be express or implied: conduct can amount to acceptance (Section 8), such as performing conditions of a public reward offer.
  8. Silence ordinarily is not acceptance: an offeror cannot impose acceptance merely by saying silence will count.

Communication — Section 4: Communication of acceptance is complete against the proposer when it is put into a course of transmission to him so as to be out of the acceptor’s power, and against the acceptor when it comes to the proposer’s knowledge. For instantaneous communications such as telephone, acceptance generally takes effect when received/heard.

Revocation — Sections 5–6: an offer may be revoked before acceptance is complete against the proposer; an acceptance may be revoked before it is complete against the acceptor. Revocation can occur by notice, lapse of time, failure of a condition, or death/insanity known before acceptance.

Case: Felthouse v. Bindley — silence cannot ordinarily be forced into acceptance. Entores Ltd. v. Miles Far East Corp. — instantaneous acceptance is generally effective on receipt.

Conclusion: Acceptance must be clear, timely, communicated in the required way and correspond exactly with the offer.

Distinguish an offer from an invitation to offer (invitation to treat). Explain with examples.

Past papers: June/July 2025 short note; invitation to offer also listed in May 2025 re-examination.

An offer shows willingness to be legally bound as soon as the other party accepts. An invitation to offer invites the other party to make an offer; the inviter may accept or reject it.

Q04UNIT I · Introduction & Formation
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

“An agreement without consideration is void.” Discuss the rule and its exceptions.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; May 2025 re-examination; Jan/Feb 2026.

Study model answer

Section 2(d): Consideration is an act, abstinence or promise done at the desire of the promisor by the promisee or any other person. Section 25 states that an agreement without consideration is void, subject to exceptions.

Why consideration matters: it distinguishes a bargain from a gratuitous promise. It need not be adequate, but it must be real and lawful.

Exceptions under Section 25

  1. Natural love and affection: a written and registered agreement between near relatives, made on account of natural love and affection.
  2. Past voluntary service: a promise to compensate wholly or partly a person who has already voluntarily done something for the promisor, or something the promisor was legally compellable to do.
  3. Time-barred debt: a written and signed promise to pay wholly or partly a debt barred by limitation.
  4. Completed gifts: the rule does not invalidate a gift actually made.
  5. Agency: no consideration is necessary to create an agency (Section 185).
  6. Remission/waiver: under Section 63, a promisee may remit or dispense with performance without fresh consideration.
  7. Charitable subscription: enforceability may arise where the promisee incurs liability on the faith of the promise; facts must establish a binding basis.

Cases: Kedarnath Bhattacharji v. Gorie Mohamed — subscription was enforceable where liability was incurred on its faith. Abdul Aziz v. Masum Ali — a bare promise to donate, with no liability incurred, was not enforceable.

Conclusion: Consideration is the general rule, but the Act recognises specific exceptions. The exception’s statutory conditions must be proved.

Q05UNIT I · Introduction & Formation
IMPORTANT10 marks

Explain the essential elements of a valid contract under Section 10.

Core syllabus question; supports all units.

Study model answer

Under Section 10, Indian Contract Act, 1872, agreements are contracts if made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for lawful consideration and lawful object, and are not expressly declared void.

  1. Offer and acceptance: a lawful proposal must be accepted validly.
  2. Intention to create legal relations: parties must intend legal consequences.
  3. Lawful consideration: Section 25 generally requires consideration, subject to exceptions.
  4. Capacity: parties must satisfy Section 11 (majority, sound mind and not disqualified by law).
  5. Free consent: consent must not be caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or relevant mistake (Sections 13–22).
  6. Lawful object and consideration: Section 23 excludes unlawful purposes.
  7. Not expressly void: for example, certain restraints, wagering agreements and uncertain agreements.
  8. Certainty: terms must be certain or capable of being made certain (Section 29).
  9. Possibility: an agreement to do an impossible act is void (Section 56).
  10. Required formalities: where law requires writing, registration, stamping or other formalities, they must be met.

Conclusion: A contract is more than a promise; it is an agreement satisfying the statutory requirements for legal enforceability.

Q06UNIT I · Introduction & Formation
IMPORTANT6 marks

Explain general offers and the legal effect of performing the conditions of a reward advertisement.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024 reward problem; related offer/acceptance questions recur.

Study model answer

A general offer is made to the public or a class of persons. It can be accepted by anyone who knows of it and performs the stated conditions. Acceptance may be by conduct; separate advance notice is not always required for a unilateral reward offer where performance itself is the invited mode.

Case: Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. — a clear advertisement promising a reward on stated conditions was treated as an offer to the public, and performance constituted acceptance.

Problem: If A advertises a reward for finding a missing dog and B, knowing of the advertisement, finds and returns the dog, B can generally claim the reward. A cannot defeat the claim merely by arguing that B did not separately communicate acceptance before performing, if the offer invited performance as acceptance.

Limit: a person who performs without knowledge of the offer ordinarily cannot claim under it (Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt).

Problem: A advertises ₹5,000 for anyone who finds a missing dog. B reads the advertisement, finds the dog and brings it to A. A refuses to pay because B did not separately communicate acceptance. Advise.

Past paper: Mar/Apr 2024, Unit I.

Study model answer

Issue: Whether B accepted the general offer and can claim the reward.

Rule: A general offer may be accepted by performing its conditions. Under Sections 2(a), 2(b) and 8, acceptance may be by conduct. Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. supports acceptance through performance of a unilateral offer. The offeree must know of the offer; Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt.

Application: B knew of the advertisement and found and returned the dog in accordance with its terms. If the advertisement was sufficiently definite and invited performance as acceptance, no separate prior notice was required unless the offer specified it.

Conclusion: B can generally recover the ₹5,000 reward, subject to proof that he knew of and fulfilled the stated conditions.

Q07UNIT II · Consideration, Capacity & Free Consent
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain the legal position and effects of a minor’s agreement.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; May 2025 re-examination; Jan/Feb 2026.

Study model answer

Section 11: a person is competent to contract if of majority age under applicable law, of sound mind and not disqualified by law. A minor is not competent to contract.

Rule — Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose: a minor’s agreement is void ab initio, not merely voidable. Key effects:

  1. No personal contractual liability: the minor cannot ordinarily be sued to enforce the agreement or recover contractual damages.
  2. No ratification on attaining majority: a void agreement cannot be ratified as such; a fresh contract supported by fresh consideration may be made after majority.
  3. Restitution is limited: Section 33 of the Specific Relief Act may permit restoration of benefits in specified circumstances, but not a disguised personal money decree against the minor. Equitable relief depends on statute and facts.
  4. Necessaries — Section 68: a supplier of necessaries suited to the minor’s condition in life may be reimbursed from the minor’s property, not personally from the minor.
  5. Beneficial arrangements: a minor may take benefits, such as under a beneficial contract or scholarship, subject to applicable law.
  6. No estoppel against the minor: a minor’s false statement about age does not ordinarily make a void contract enforceable by estoppel.
  7. Guardian’s contracts: may bind the minor’s estate if the guardian acts within authority and for the minor’s benefit, subject to applicable law.

Conclusion: The law protects minors from contractual exploitation. A claimant should consider Section 68 for necessaries or statutory restitution, not assume ordinary contractual recovery.

Q08UNIT II · Consideration, Capacity & Free Consent
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain the doctrine of privity of contract and its exceptions.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; June/July 2025; repeated across papers.

Study model answer

Rule: ordinarily, only a party to a contract may sue to enforce it. A person who is not a party cannot normally claim merely because the contract benefits them. Indian law permits consideration to move from the promisee or any other person (Section 2(d)), but this does not generally abolish privity.

Case: M.C. Chacko v. State Bank of Travancore — the Supreme Court affirmed the general rule of privity, subject to recognised exceptions.

Important exceptions/recognised routes

  1. Trust or charge: a beneficiary may enforce rights created for them under a trust or charge.
  2. Family settlement/marriage arrangement: a beneficiary under a family arrangement may, in appropriate circumstances, enforce it.
  3. Acknowledgment or estoppel: where a contracting party acknowledges an obligation to the third party or is estopped by conduct, enforcement may be possible on the facts.
  4. Agency: a principal may sue or be sued on a contract made by an authorised agent.
  5. Assignment: contractual rights may be assigned where law and the contract permit.
  6. Covenants attached to property: certain obligations may be enforceable by persons who acquire the relevant property interest.
  7. Statutory rights: legislation may expressly confer a right on a third party.

Distinction: privity of contract asks who may sue; privity of consideration asks from whom consideration may move. Indian law is flexible on the latter but generally retains the former.

Conclusion: A third-party beneficiary should identify a recognised exception or statutory basis rather than rely only on the fact that the contract benefits them.

Q09UNIT II · Consideration, Capacity & Free Consent
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Define fraud and distinguish fraud from misrepresentation.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; June/July 2025.

Study model answer

Fraud — Section 17: includes knowingly making a false suggestion, active concealment, a promise made without intention to perform, any other act fitted to deceive, and an act or omission specially declared fraudulent by law, subject to statutory qualifications.

Misrepresentation — Section 18: includes an unwarranted positive assertion believed true, a non-fraudulent breach of duty that gains an advantage by misleading another, and innocently causing a party to make a mistake as to the substance of the subject matter.

FraudMisrepresentation
Usually involves knowledge of falsity, lack of belief in truth or reckless disregard, or intention to deceive.False statement is made without fraudulent intent, often honestly believed true.
May support rescission and damages where legally available.May make the contract voidable under Section 19, but damages are not automatically available merely for innocent misrepresentation.
Silence is generally not fraud unless there is a duty to speak or silence is equivalent to speech.May arise from an innocent but misleading assertion or qualifying conduct.

Effect — Section 19: consent caused by fraud or misrepresentation generally makes the contract voidable at the option of the affected party, subject to exceptions. If the party had means of discovering the truth with ordinary diligence, the statutory exception for certain misrepresentation/fraudulent silence may apply.

Conclusion: The central distinction is dishonest intent. Identify the false statement, the maker’s state of mind, inducement and the remedy sought.

Q10UNIT II · Consideration, Capacity & Free Consent
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain free consent and the factors that vitiate it.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; free consent and undue influence in Jan/Feb 2026.

Study model answer

Sections 13–14: consent means agreeing upon the same thing in the same sense (consensus ad idem). Consent is free when it is not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake, subject to the Act’s detailed provisions.

  1. Coercion — Section 15: committing or threatening an act forbidden by the IPC/Bharatiya criminal law as applicable, or unlawfully detaining/threatening to detain property, to cause a person to enter an agreement.
  2. Undue influence — Section 16: one party is in a position to dominate another’s will and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage. Relationships of authority, fiduciary confidence, or dependence may be relevant; the burden may shift where the transaction appears unconscionable.
  3. Fraud — Section 17: intentional deception or other conduct covered by the section.
  4. Misrepresentation — Section 18: misleading statement/conduct without fraud as defined.
  5. Mistake — Sections 20–22: bilateral mistake of essential fact can make an agreement void; mistake of Indian law generally does not excuse; unilateral mistake of fact ordinarily does not make a contract void, subject to limited exceptions.

Effects: coercion, fraud, misrepresentation and undue influence generally make a contract voidable at the option of the affected party (Sections 19 and 19A). Bilateral mistake of essential fact makes the agreement void under Section 20.

Conclusion: Explain the relevant factor, prove that it caused consent, and state whether the result is void, voidable or otherwise affected.

Exam writing tip: Start with the definition/section, use numbered points, add at least one relevant case where appropriate, apply the rule to facts, and finish with a short conclusion.
Q11UNIT II · Consideration, Capacity & Free Consent
IMPORTANT10 marks

Explain capacity to contract and soundness of mind.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024.

Study model answer

Section 11 recognises capacity where a person has attained majority, is of sound mind and is not disqualified by law.

Section 12 — sound mind: a person is of sound mind for contracting if, at the time of making the contract, they are capable of understanding it and forming a rational judgment about its effect on their interests. A person usually of unsound mind may contract during a lucid interval; a person usually sound may not contract while temporarily incapable (for example, severe intoxication), if the statutory test is not met.

Minor: agreement is generally void ab initio; see Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose.

Persons disqualified by law: particular statutes may limit capacity, for example in cases involving certain legal status or corporate authority. The precise statute and facts must be identified.

Problem tip: A person who sells an article at a low price is not automatically of unsound mind. The question is capacity to understand and judge the transaction at the relevant time; price disparity alone is insufficient.

Conclusion: Capacity is assessed at contract formation and depends on the statutory test, not merely age, unusual behaviour or an unfavourable bargain.

Q12UNIT II · Consideration, Capacity & Free Consent
IMPORTANT6 marks

Can a supplier recover the price of necessaries supplied to a minor or a person incapable of contracting?

Past papers: Jan/Feb 2026 necessaries problem.

Study model answer

Section 68: if a person incapable of entering a contract, or anyone whom they are legally bound to support, is supplied with necessaries suited to their condition in life, the supplier is entitled to reimbursement from that person’s property.

Requirements: (1) recipient is incapable of contracting or is a person they must support; (2) goods/services are necessaries, not luxuries; (3) suitable to the recipient’s condition in life; (4) not already adequately supplied; (5) claim is against property, not personal contractual liability.

Application: food and essential supplies provided to a minor’s dependants may qualify if the minor is legally bound to support them and the goods were genuinely necessary. A spouse’s liability cannot be assumed without establishing the legal duty and facts.

Conclusion: Section 68 creates a quasi-contractual reimbursement right from property, not an ordinary contract debt.

Q13UNIT II · Consideration, Capacity & Free Consent
IMPORTANT6 marks

Discuss undue influence where one party is in a position to dominate another’s will.

Past-paper style: teacher/student or dependent relationship problem in Jan/Feb 2026.

Study model answer

Section 16: undue influence exists where the parties’ relationship enables one to dominate the will of the other and that position is used to obtain an unfair advantage.

Relevant relationships may include real or apparent authority, fiduciary relations, or a contract with a person whose mental capacity is affected by age, illness or distress. A relationship alone is not always enough; misuse and unfair advantage must be assessed.

Under Section 16(3), where a party is in a position to dominate the other’s will and the transaction appears unconscionable, the burden may shift to the stronger party to show the contract was not induced by undue influence.

Remedy — Section 19A: the court may set aside the contract absolutely or on terms it considers just.

Application tip: a teacher/student relationship does not automatically invalidate a sale. Consider dominance, dependence, the transaction’s fairness, and evidence of pressure or exploitation.

Problem: A and B agree before marriage to live separately after marriage. Is the agreement enforceable?

Past paper: May 2025 re-examination, Unit II.

Study model answer

Issue: Whether the agreement is a valid contract or void as opposed to public policy.

Rule: Sections 10 and 23 require lawful object and consideration. Agreements contrary to public policy or prohibited by law are unenforceable. Marriage-related agreements are assessed by their purpose, terms and applicable personal/statutory law; not every agreement concerning spouses is automatically void.

Q14UNIT III · Void Agreements, Contingent Contracts & Discharge
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Define contingent contract and explain the rules governing its enforcement.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; repeated syllabus core.

Study model answer

Section 31: a contingent contract is a contract to do or not to do something if some event collateral to the contract does or does not happen.

Rules under Sections 32–36

  1. Event happening — Section 32: enforceable only when the uncertain event happens; becomes void if the event becomes impossible.
  2. Event not happening — Section 33: enforceable when the happening of the event becomes impossible.
  3. Conduct of a living person — Section 34: where the event depends on how a person acts at an unspecified time, it may become impossible when that person does something making the event impossible under the section.
  4. Specified time — Section 35: if event must happen within a fixed time, the contract becomes void when time expires without it happening, or earlier when it becomes impossible; a contract based on event not happening within time becomes enforceable when time expires or the event becomes impossible sooner.
  5. Impossible event — Section 36: an agreement contingent on an impossible event is void, whether or not parties know of the impossibility.

Example: A promises to pay B ₹10,000 if a particular ship returns. The promise is contingent; enforcement depends on the statutory event rule and facts.

Distinction from wagering: a contingent contract has a genuine underlying transaction and the event is collateral; a wager depends on winning/losing on an uncertain event and is generally void under Section 30.

Conclusion: Identify the collateral event, then apply the correct section based on whether it must happen, must not happen, or must occur within a fixed period.

Exam writing tip: Start with the definition/section, use numbered points, add at least one relevant case where appropriate, apply the rule to facts, and finish with a short conclusion.
Q15UNIT III · Void Agreements, Contingent Contracts & Discharge
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain the doctrine of frustration/impossibility of performance with decided cases.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024 (80/100); May 2025 re-exam; repeated in other papers.

Study model answer

Section 56: an agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void. A contract becomes void when, after it is made, an act becomes impossible or unlawful because of an event the promisor could not prevent, subject to the statutory rule and judicial interpretation.

Grounds may include: destruction of the subject matter; death or incapacity in a contract of personal service; change in law making performance unlawful; or an event that fundamentally destroys the basis of the contract. Mere hardship, increased expense or commercial inconvenience is ordinarily insufficient.

Leading cases:

  • Taylor v. Caldwell — destruction of the music hall frustrated the contract where its continued existence was fundamental.
  • Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur & Co. — Section 56 is the governing Indian rule; impossibility is not confined to literal physical impossibility but must be assessed in context.
  • Alopi Parshad & Sons Ltd. v. Union of India — increased cost or commercial hardship alone does not excuse performance.
  • Energy Watchdog v. CERC — a force majeure clause is construed under the contract; Section 56 applies where the contract does not govern the event, and price rise alone did not frustrate performance.

Effect: the contract becomes void when performance becomes impossible/unlawful. Section 65 may require restoration of advantages received under an agreement discovered void or a contract that becomes void, subject to its terms and the facts.

Distinguish: first examine any force majeure clause; do not invoke frustration where the contract itself allocates the risk.

Conclusion: Frustration is narrow and fact-sensitive; the event must fundamentally affect performance, not merely make it less profitable.

Exam writing tip: Start with the definition/section, use numbered points, add at least one relevant case where appropriate, apply the rule to facts, and finish with a short conclusion.
Q16UNIT III · Void Agreements, Contingent Contracts & Discharge
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain the different modes of discharge of a contract.

Past papers: May 2025 re-examination; core recurring question.

Study model answer

Discharge means termination of contractual obligations.

  1. By performance: actual performance or valid tender/offer of performance (Sections 37–38). The promisee must accept a proper tender.
  2. By mutual agreement: novation, rescission or alteration (Section 62); remission, waiver or extension by promisee (Section 63).
  3. By impossibility/frustration: Section 56, where performance becomes impossible or unlawful after formation.
  4. By lapse of time: limitation law bars the remedy after the prescribed period; it does not necessarily extinguish the underlying obligation in every context.
  5. By operation of law: for example, death in a personal-service contract, insolvency consequences, or merger where legally applicable.
  6. By breach: actual breach at due date or anticipatory breach before due date (Section 39).
  7. By rescission of voidable contract: the entitled party may rescind where consent was vitiated, subject to the Act.

Conclusion: In an exam answer, define discharge, explain each mode, cite the relevant sections and give a short example.

Q17UNIT III · Void Agreements, Contingent Contracts & Discharge
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain appropriation of payments between debtor and creditor.

Past papers: June/July 2025; appropriation of payments listed in May 2025 re-exam.

Study model answer

Sections 59–61 apply when a debtor owes several distinct debts to the same creditor and makes a payment insufficient to discharge all of them.

  1. Debtor indicates the debt — Section 59: if the debtor expressly or by circumstances indicates which debt is to be paid, the creditor must apply it accordingly if the payment is accepted.
  2. Creditor appropriates — Section 60: if the debtor gives no indication, the creditor may apply the payment to any lawful debt actually due and payable, including a time-barred debt, subject to the section.
  3. Neither appropriates — Section 61: payment is applied in order of time of the debts; debts of equal standing are discharged proportionately.

Example: A owes B ₹10,000 due in January and ₹20,000 due in March. If A expressly says a ₹5,000 payment is for the January debt, B must apply it accordingly. If A says nothing, Section 60 governs; if neither makes an appropriation, Section 61 applies.

Conclusion: Always identify the debtor’s direction first, then creditor’s choice, and finally the statutory default.

Q18UNIT III · Void Agreements, Contingent Contracts & Discharge
IMPORTANT10 marks

“An agreement without consideration is void.” Explain consideration, its rules and lawful/unlawful consideration.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; linked to Unit I consideration question.

Study model answer

Section 2(d): consideration is an act, abstinence or promise at the desire of the promisor by the promisee or any other person. Consideration may be past, present or future in Indian law, provided it satisfies the section.

Rules: it must be at the desire of the promisor; may move from the promisee or another person; need not be adequate but must have some legal value; must be real and lawful; and must not be something the law regards as impossible or illusory.

Q21UNIT IV · Breach, Damages & Quasi-Contracts
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain remedies for breach of contract.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; May 2025 re-exam; repeated in multiple sessions.

Study model answer

When a party fails to perform a contractual obligation, the innocent party may seek one or more remedies, subject to the contract and law.

  1. Damages — Sections 73–75: compensation for loss naturally arising in the usual course or which the parties knew was likely when contracting; remote and indirect loss is excluded. Section 74 addresses stipulated sums/penalties, allowing reasonable compensation not exceeding the named amount. Section 75 permits compensation to a party rightfully rescinding.
  2. Specific performance: a decree requiring performance of the contractual obligation under the Specific Relief Act, 1963, as amended in 2018, subject to Sections 11(2), 14 and 16 and other statutory limits.
  3. Injunction: court order restraining a threatened or continuing breach, where the Specific Relief Act requirements are met.
  4. Rescission: termination/setting aside in legally recognised circumstances.
  5. Restitution/quantum meruit: recovery of the reasonable value of benefits/services in recognised situations, including where a contract is discharged or a non-gratuitous benefit is accepted (Sections 65, 70 and 75 as relevant).
  6. Declaratory relief: declaration of legal status/right where the Specific Relief Act permits.

Cases: Hadley v. Baxendale — remoteness test. Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Das and Kailash Nath Associates v. DDA — reasonable compensation under Section 74; stipulated sum is not automatically recoverable without legal basis.

Conclusion: Identify the breach, prove legally recoverable loss, mitigate loss, and choose the remedy suited to the facts.

Q22UNIT IV · Breach, Damages & Quasi-Contracts
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain the principles governing measurement of damages, including Hadley v. Baxendale.

Past papers: March/April 2024 short note; May 2025 re-exam general/special damages; June/July 2025 measurement.

Study model answer

Section 73: compensation is available for loss that naturally arose in the usual course from the breach or that the parties knew, when contracting, was likely to result. Remote and indirect loss is not recoverable. The injured party must take reasonable steps to mitigate loss.

Hadley v. Baxendale (1854): damages are recoverable if (1) they arise naturally according to the usual course of things, or (2) they were within the parties’ reasonable contemplation because special circumstances were communicated at contract formation.

Types: ordinary/general damages (natural loss); special damages (special loss due to communicated circumstances); nominal damages (where a right is breached but substantial loss is not proved); exemplary damages only in exceptional legally recognised contexts, not the ordinary contract rule; liquidated damages/penalty under Section 74, subject to reasonable compensation.

Other principles: causation; foreseeability/remoteness; certainty of proof; mitigation; no double recovery; compensation rather than punishment. Section 73 also takes account of available means of remedying the inconvenience caused by non-performance.

Case: Victoria Laundry v. Newman Industries illustrates the distinction between ordinary lost profits and unusually profitable contracts not known to the defendant.

Conclusion: State the two limbs of Hadley, apply them to the facts, and explain which loss was foreseeable and proved.

Write a short note on anticipatory breach of contract.

Section 39: if a party refuses to perform, or disables itself from performing, its promise in its entirety, the promisee may put an end to the contract unless the promisee has signified, by words or conduct, acquiescence in its continuance.

Q24UNIT IV · Breach, Damages & Quasi-Contracts
IMPORTANT10 marks

Explain quasi-contracts and the circumstances in which quasi-contractual liability arises.

Past papers: June/July 2025; related questions in syllabus and 2026 paper.

Study model answer

Quasi-contract is not a contract based on actual agreement. It is an obligation imposed by law to prevent unjust enrichment.

  1. Section 68 — necessaries: supplier reimbursed from property of person incapable of contracting or a person they must support.
  2. Section 69 — payment by interested person: a person interested in paying money which another is legally bound to pay, and who pays it, may recover from the other.
  3. Section 70 — non-gratuitous act: a person lawfully does something for another or delivers something, not intending it gratuitously, and the other enjoys the benefit; compensation or restoration is due.
  4. Section 71 — finder of goods: finder has responsibilities similar to a bailee.
  5. Section 72 — money/goods delivered by mistake or coercion: must be repaid or returned.

Cases: State of West Bengal v. B.K. Mondal & Sons — Section 70 compensation where government enjoyed non-gratuitous work. Sales Tax Officer v. Kanhaiya Lal Mukund Lal Saraf — recovery of money paid under mistake of law was recognised under Section 72.

Q25UNIT IV · Breach, Damages & Quasi-Contracts
IMPORTANT6 marks

Explain quantum meruit.

Past papers: March/April 2024 short-note option.

Study model answer

Quantum meruit means “as much as is deserved.” It permits reasonable payment for work or services where the law recognises a right to compensation, despite no agreed enforceable price for the completed benefit.

Common situations include: work done under a contract that is discovered void or becomes void (Section 65, where applicable); a party who rightfully rescinds after the other’s breach (Section 75); non-gratuitous acts whose benefit is enjoyed (Section 70); or divisible work accepted before wrongful prevention of completion, depending on facts and legal basis.

It does not automatically permit a party who has completely and wrongfully abandoned an entire indivisible contract to claim payment for partial work. The claimant must show a recognised legal basis and the value of the benefit.

Q26UNIT IV · Breach, Damages & Quasi-Contracts
IMPORTANT6 marks

Distinguish general, special, nominal and liquidated damages/penalty.

Past papers: May 2025 re-exam; June/July 2025 kinds of damages.

Study model answer
  • General/ordinary damages: loss naturally arising in the usual course from breach.
  • Special damages: additional loss caused by special circumstances, recoverable where those circumstances were communicated or within the parties’ contemplation.
  • Nominal damages: small sum recognising breach where substantial loss is not proved.
  • Liquidated damages: sum stipulated in the contract as an estimate of loss. Under Section 74, court awards reasonable compensation not exceeding the stipulated amount.

Q27UNIT V · Specific Relief Act, 1963
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain contracts which can be specifically enforced under the Specific Relief Act, 1963.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; May 2025 re-exam; Jan/Feb 2026.

Study model answer

The Specific Relief Act, 1963 was substantially amended in 2018. Under the amended Section 10, specific performance of a contract shall be enforced by the court subject to Section 11(2), Section 14 and Section 16. Avoid relying on older textbook statements that specific performance is always purely discretionary without checking the amended Act.

Key framework:

  • Section 10: specific performance is enforced subject to statutory exclusions and bars.
  • Section 11: contracts connected with trusts, subject to Section 11(2).
  • Section 14: contracts not specifically enforceable, including where substituted performance has been obtained, contracts involving continuous duties the court cannot supervise, contracts dependent on personal qualifications, and determinable contracts.
  • Section 16: personal bars to relief, including failure to prove performance or continuous readiness and willingness as required, and other statutory bars.
  • Section 20: substituted performance — after giving the required written notice and opportunity, the affected party may get performance through a third party or own agency and recover expenses as permitted; after obtaining substituted performance, specific performance of the same contract is barred under the Act.
  • Section 21: compensation may be awarded in addition to or in substitution for specific performance in circumstances provided by the Act.

Exam method: define specific performance; state amended Section 10; explain Sections 14 and 16; add one example and conclude that relief depends on the contract and statutory conditions.

Note: Check the current bare Act and your prescribed textbook for the precise wording of the relevant subsections before the exam.

Q28UNIT V · Specific Relief Act, 1963
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Define injunction and explain its types and when courts may refuse injunctions.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; May 2025 re-exam; June/July 2025; Jan/Feb 2026.

Study model answer

An injunction is a judicial order directing a person to do or refrain from doing a specified act.

Types under the Specific Relief Act, 1963 and CPC:

  1. Temporary/interlocutory injunction: operates for a limited period or until further order/trial, governed procedurally by Order XXXIX Rules 1–2 CPC.
  2. Perpetual injunction — Sections 37–38: granted by decree after hearing on merits, permanently restraining an act that infringes or threatens a right.
  3. Mandatory injunction — Section 39: compels performance of an act necessary to prevent or remedy a breach.
  4. Prohibitory injunction: restrains an act.
  5. Preventive relief: injunctions are a form of preventive relief under Section 36.

Temporary injunction test: prima facie case, balance of convenience, and irreparable injury. These are assessed together, not as automatic formulae.

Refusal — Section 41: statutory examples include restraining certain proceedings in which injunction is barred, where equally efficacious relief is available (except where appropriate), where claimant’s conduct disentitles relief, where no personal interest is shown, and other situations specified in the section. Section 42 deals with enforcing a negative agreement in qualifying cases.

Conclusion: Identify the right threatened, choose the proper type, satisfy equitable/statutory requirements and address Section 41 restrictions.

Q29UNIT V · Specific Relief Act, 1963
REPEATED / HIGH PRIORITY10 marks

Explain the provisions relating to recovery of specific immovable and movable property.

Past papers: Mar/Apr 2024; June/July 2025; May 2025 re-exam.

Study model answer

Specific Relief Act, 1963:

  • Section 5 — immovable property: a person entitled to possession of specific immovable property may recover it in the manner provided by the Code of Civil Procedure.
  • Section 6 — dispossession without consent and otherwise than due course of law: a person dispossessed may sue to recover possession notwithstanding title, subject to the six-month limitation and other statutory conditions. No suit under Section 6 lies against the Government, and no appeal/review is allowed as specified by the section; a separate title suit may remain available.
  • Section 7 — movable property: a person entitled to possession of specific movable property may recover it under the CPC framework.
