Unit I — Formation of Contract

Offer, acceptance, consideration, Section 10, tenders and e-contracts

QUESTION 1 · LONG ANSWERIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Define proposal/offer. Explain the rules of a valid offer and when an offer lapses.

Meaning and legal provision

Section 2(a) of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 states that when a person signifies to another their willingness to do or abstain from doing something, with a view to obtaining the other’s assent, they make a proposal.

Rules of a valid offer

  • Intention to create legal relations: the proposal must be intended to create legal obligations, not merely a social arrangement.
  • Clear and certain: terms must be definite or capable of being made certain (Section 29).
  • Communicated: an offer cannot be accepted unless the offeree knows of it. Lalman Shukla v. Gauri Dutt illustrates this rule.
  • May be express or implied: it may be made by words or conduct.
  • May be specific or general: a general offer can be accepted by anyone who knows of it and performs its conditions. Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. is the classic example.
  • Must not be merely an invitation to offer: advertisements of goods, displays in shops and catalogues are generally invitations to customers to make offers, depending on their wording and context.
  • Terms and conditions must be brought to notice: an offeror cannot ordinarily rely on undisclosed conditions.

When does an offer lapse?

  • When notice of revocation reaches the offeree before valid acceptance (Sections 5 and 6).
  • By expiry of the prescribed or reasonable time.
  • When a condition precedent is not fulfilled.
  • On death or insanity of the proposer, if the offeree learns of it before acceptance.
  • By rejection or a counter-offer.
  • By a material change in circumstances or destruction of the subject matter, where the law treats the offer as no longer open.

Conclusion

A valid offer must be clear, communicated and intended to obtain assent. Whether a statement is an offer or an invitation to offer depends on its wording and circumstances.

QUESTION 2 · LONG ANSWERPAST-PAPER THEME · verify against original papers

Explain the rules relating to valid acceptance. When is communication of acceptance complete?

Meaning

Under Section 2(b), a proposal is accepted when the person to whom it is made signifies assent. A proposal, when accepted, becomes a promise.

Rules of valid acceptance

  • Acceptance must be absolute and unqualified (Section 7). A material change is a counter-offer, not acceptance.
  • It must be communicated, expressly or by conduct, unless the offer dispenses with separate notification in a legally valid way.
  • It must be made by the person to whom the offer is addressed, or by an authorised person.
  • It must follow the prescribed mode; if no mode is prescribed, it must be in a usual and reasonable manner.
  • It must be made while the offer remains open and before valid revocation or lapse.
  • Silence alone ordinarily does not amount to acceptance.
  • Acceptance may be by conduct, including performance of the conditions of a proposal (Section 8).

Communication — Section 4

Communication of acceptance is complete against the proposer when it is put into a course of transmission to the proposer so as to be out of the acceptor’s power; against the acceptor, it is complete when it comes to the proposer’s knowledge. This traditional postal rule is subject to the nature of the communication and applicable law; instantaneous communications are generally assessed by receipt principles.

Revocation

Under Section 5, acceptance may be revoked by the acceptor before communication of acceptance is complete as against the acceptor, but not afterwards.

Case law

Felthouse v. Bindley illustrates that silence is not ordinarily acceptance. Bhagwandas Goverdhandas Kedia v. Girdharilal Parshottamdas is relevant to communication by telephone and the place of contract.

Conclusion

Acceptance must match the offer, be made in time and be communicated according to the applicable rule.

QUESTION 3 · LONG ANSWERPAST-PAPER THEME · verify against original papers

“An agreement without consideration is void.” Discuss the rule and its exceptions.

Meaning

Section 2(d) defines consideration as an act, abstinence or promise done at the desire of the promisor by the promisee or any other person. Under Section 25, an agreement made without consideration is generally void, subject to exceptions.

Why consideration matters

It is the price for which the promise of the other is bought. Consideration must be at the desire of the promisor, may move from the promisee or another person, may be past, present or future, and need not be adequate, though it must be real and lawful.

Exceptions under Section 25

  1. Natural love and affection: a written and registered agreement between near relations, made on account of natural love and affection.
  2. Past voluntary services: a promise to compensate wholly or partly a person who has already voluntarily done something for the promisor, or something the promisor was legally compellable to do.
  3. Time-barred debt: a written and signed promise to pay wholly or partly a debt barred by limitation.
  4. Completed gifts: the rule does not affect the validity, as between donor and donee, of a gift actually made.
  5. Agency: under Section 185, no consideration is necessary to create an agency.
  6. Remission/waiver: Section 63 permits a promisee to remit or dispense with performance, or accept any satisfaction considered fit, without fresh consideration.

Case law

Kedarnath Bhattacharji v. Gorie Mohamed is commonly cited where a subscription promise induced liability. Abdul Aziz v. Masum Ali illustrates that a bare promise without consideration or acted-upon liability may be unenforceable.

Conclusion

Consideration is the general rule for enforceability, but Section 25 and other statutory provisions recognise specific exceptions. State the exact exception and its conditions.

QUESTION 4 · LONG ANSWERIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain the essential elements of a valid contract under Section 10.

Legal rule

Section 10 provides that agreements are contracts if made by the free consent of parties competent to contract, for a lawful consideration and with a lawful object, and are not expressly declared void. Other laws may prescribe additional formalities for particular contracts.

Essential elements

  • Offer and acceptance: a lawful proposal must be accepted validly.
  • Intention to create legal relations: the arrangement must be legally enforceable.
  • Lawful consideration and object: Sections 23–25 apply.
  • Competent parties: parties must satisfy Section 11.
  • Free consent: consent must not be caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or relevant mistake (Sections 13–22).
  • Certainty: terms must not be uncertain or incapable of being made certain (Section 29).
  • Possibility of performance: an agreement to do an impossible act is void (Section 56).
  • Not expressly declared void: for example, agreements in restraint of marriage, subject to statutory qualifications, or wagering agreements in jurisdictions where Section 30 applies.
  • Required formalities: where law requires writing, witnesses, registration or electronic compliance, those requirements must be met.

Agreement and contract

Every contract is an agreement, but every agreement is not a contract. Only agreements enforceable by law are contracts under Section 2(h).

Conclusion

Section 10 gives the core test. A good answer explains each element and notes that special statutes can affect particular transactions.

QUESTION 5 · SHORT NOTEIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Distinguish an offer from an invitation to offer.

An offer shows willingness to be legally bound once the other party accepts it. An invitation to offer invites others to make offers, which the inviter may accept or reject.

  • Shop display: goods displayed with prices are generally an invitation to customers to offer to buy; the customer offers at the checkout.
  • Advertisements: generally invitations to offer, unless wording shows a clear, definite promise intended to bind upon performance.
  • Tenders and auctions: an invitation to submit tenders or bids is usually an invitation to offer; the submitted bid is the offer, subject to the terms of the process.

Case: Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v. Boots Cash Chemists is the leading shop-display example. Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co. shows that a sufficiently definite advertisement can be a unilateral offer.

Conclusion: classify the communication by its wording, certainty, intention and surrounding circumstances.

QUESTION 6 · SHORT NOTEIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain tenders and electronic contracts.

Tenders

A tender notice normally invites interested parties to submit offers. The tender submitted is ordinarily the offer, and acceptance according to the tender conditions may create a contract. The terms may impose procedural duties on the authority, so the notice and governing rules must be read carefully. A tender can be specific or may involve a standing offer for future orders.

Electronic contracts

An e-contract is formed using electronic communications, websites, apps, email or other digital systems. Ordinary contract principles—offer, acceptance, capacity, free consent, lawful consideration and object—still apply. The Information Technology Act, 2000 recognises electronic records and electronic signatures in specified circumstances; Section 10A provides that a contract is not unenforceable merely because proposal, acceptance or revocation was expressed electronically.

Click-wrap agreements may evidence assent when a user actively clicks to accept presented terms. Browse-wrap terms can raise notice and assent issues. Parties should preserve the terms shown, timestamps, identity/authentication evidence and transaction records.

Conclusion: electronic form does not by itself invalidate a contract, but the ordinary requirements of formation and applicable statutory formalities remain important.

Unit II — Capacity and Free Consent

Privity, minors, capacity, coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation and mistake

QUESTION 7 · LONG ANSWERPAST-PAPER THEME · verify against original papers

Explain the doctrine of privity of contract and its exceptions.

Meaning

The doctrine of privity generally means that only a party to a contract can sue to enforce its terms. A third party who benefits from a contract ordinarily cannot sue merely because the contract was made for their benefit.

Indian position

Section 2(d) allows consideration to move from the promisee or any other person, so Indian law differs from the strict English rule that consideration must move from the promisee. However, the doctrine of privity of contract generally remains relevant.

Recognised exceptions / routes

  • Trust or charge: a beneficiary may enforce a valid trust or charge created for them.
  • Family arrangements: a beneficiary under a family settlement may in appropriate circumstances enforce the arrangement.
  • Marriage or partition arrangements: a beneficiary may enforce terms where the legal arrangement and facts support it.
  • Assignment: an assignee may enforce assigned contractual rights, subject to law and defences.
  • Agency: a principal may sue or be sued on a contract made by an authorised agent.
  • Acknowledgment or estoppel: in appropriate circumstances, a clear undertaking or representation may prevent denial of an obligation.
  • Statutory rights: legislation may confer rights on non-parties.

Case law

M.C. Chacko v. State Bank of Travancore is an important Supreme Court authority on privity. Dunlop Pneumatic Tyre Co. v. Selfridge is a classic English authority.

Conclusion

Begin with the general rule, distinguish privity of contract from the rule on consideration, and explain only exceptions supported by the facts and law.

QUESTION 8 · LONG ANSWERPAST-PAPER THEME · verify against original papers

Explain the legal effects of a minor’s agreement.

Capacity — Sections 11 and 12

A person is competent to contract if they are of the age of majority under applicable law, of sound mind, and not disqualified by law. A minor is not competent to contract.

Main effects

  • Void from the beginning: a minor’s agreement is void ab initio. Mohori Bibee v. Dharmodas Ghose is the leading authority.
  • No personal contractual liability: a minor cannot ordinarily be made personally liable on the agreement.
  • No ratification after majority of the void agreement: a fresh contract supported by fresh consideration is needed where required.
  • Restitution is limited: Section 33 of the Specific Relief Act, 1963 and equitable principles may permit restoration of identifiable property or benefits in appropriate cases, but do not turn the void agreement into a valid contract or impose disguised personal liability.
  • Necessaries: under Section 68, a supplier may be reimbursed from the minor’s property for necessaries suited to the minor’s condition in life and actual requirements. The minor is not personally liable.
  • Beneficial arrangements: a minor may take benefits under an arrangement and may enforce a beneficial right where law permits.
  • Guardian’s contract: a contract made by a lawful guardian within authority and for the minor’s benefit may bind the minor’s estate in appropriate circumstances.
  • No estoppel to validate: a minor’s representation of age generally cannot be used to make an otherwise void contract valid.

Conclusion

The law protects minors from contractual exploitation. Explain voidness, lack of personal liability, the necessaries rule and the limited role of restitution separately.

QUESTION 9 · LONG ANSWERIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain capacity to contract and the rules governing soundness of mind.

Section 11: competent persons

A person is competent to contract if they have attained majority under applicable law, are of sound mind, and are not disqualified from contracting by any law to which they are subject.

Section 12: sound mind

A person is of sound mind for making a contract if, at the time of making it, they are capable of understanding it and of forming a rational judgment as to its effect upon their interests.

  • A person usually of unsound mind may contract during a lucid interval if capable at that time.
  • A person usually of sound mind cannot contract while temporarily incapable of understanding or judging the transaction, for example due to severe intoxication or illness.
  • Capacity is assessed at the time the contract is made and in relation to the transaction.

Other categories

Minors are incompetent to contract. Certain persons may be disqualified or restricted by statute or legal status, depending on the applicable law and circumstances.

Conclusion

Capacity protects parties who cannot legally or rationally undertake contractual obligations. In a problem, identify age, mental capacity at the relevant time and any statutory disqualification.

QUESTION 10 · LONG ANSWERIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Define free consent. Explain coercion and undue influence.

Consent and free consent

Under Section 13, parties consent when they agree upon the same thing in the same sense. Under Section 14, consent is free when it is not caused by coercion, undue influence, fraud, misrepresentation or mistake, subject to the Act’s rules.

Coercion — Section 15

Coercion includes committing or threatening to commit an act forbidden by the Indian Penal Code (now read with applicable criminal law) or unlawfully detaining or threatening to detain property, to the prejudice of any person, with the intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement.

Effect: under Section 19, a contract caused by coercion is voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused. Benefits received may have to be restored under Section 64.

Undue influence — Section 16

Undue influence arises where one party is in a position to dominate the will of another and uses that position to obtain an unfair advantage. Relationships of authority, fiduciary influence, or impaired capacity may be relevant, but the relationship alone does not automatically prove undue influence.

Where a transaction appears unconscionable and one party was in a position to dominate the other’s will, Section 16(3) may shift the burden to the dominant party to show that consent was not caused by undue influence.

Case law

Raghunath Prasad v. Sarju Prasad explains the approach to undue influence. In Subhas Chandra Das Mushib v. Ganga Prosad Das Mushib, the Supreme Court stressed the need to establish the relevant elements rather than infer undue influence merely from a relationship.

Conclusion

Coercion involves specified unlawful pressure; undue influence involves domination of will and unfair advantage. Both may make a contract voidable, but their ingredients differ.

QUESTION 11 · LONG ANSWER / DISTINCTIONIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Distinguish fraud from misrepresentation.

Fraud — Section 17

Fraud includes acts committed with intent to deceive or induce a contract, such as suggesting as true a fact the maker does not believe to be true, active concealment, a promise made without intention to perform, or other acts specifically covered by Section 17. Mere silence is not generally fraud unless there is a duty to speak or silence is equivalent to speech in the circumstances.

Misrepresentation — Section 18

Misrepresentation includes an unwarranted positive assertion believed true, a breach of duty that gains an advantage by misleading another without intent to deceive, or innocently causing a party to make a mistake about the substance of the subject matter.

Differences

  • State of mind: fraud involves intent to deceive; misrepresentation may be innocent.
  • Knowledge: fraud commonly involves knowledge of falsity or recklessness; misrepresentation may involve a genuine belief.
  • Remedy: both may make a contract voidable under Section 19, subject to statutory qualifications; fraud may also support damages where a recognised cause of action is established.
  • Exception to rescission: Section 19 limits relief for misrepresentation or fraudulent silence where the party had the means of discovering the truth with ordinary diligence, subject to the statutory wording. This qualification does not apply in the same way to fraud by active deception.

Conclusion

The key distinction is dishonest intention. Identify the false statement, its materiality, reliance and the applicable remedy.

QUESTION 12 · LONG ANSWERIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain mistake of fact and mistake of law under the Contract Act.

Meaning

Mistake is an erroneous belief about a fact or law relevant to an agreement. Sections 20–22 set out the consequences.

  • Bilateral mistake of essential fact — Section 20: where both parties are mistaken about a matter of fact essential to the agreement, the agreement is void. A wrong opinion about value is not by itself an essential mistake.
  • Mistake of law — Section 21: a mistake as to a law in force in India does not make a contract voidable. A mistake about foreign law is treated like a mistake of fact.
  • Unilateral mistake — Section 22: a contract is not voidable merely because one party is mistaken as to a matter of fact, subject to other applicable principles and special cases.

Examples

If both parties contract for the sale of specific goods that, unknown to them, had already perished, Section 20 may apply. If only one party misjudges the market price, that ordinarily does not make the contract void.

Conclusion

Determine whether the mistake is shared or unilateral, whether it concerns an essential fact, and whether it is a mistake of Indian law or foreign law.

Exam method: Define the concept, state the relevant section, explain the ingredients, apply a case/example where useful, and conclude.

QUESTION 13 · SHORT NOTEIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

What is the doctrine of consideration? Explain lawful and unlawful consideration.

Consideration under Section 2(d) is an act, abstinence or promise done at the desire of the promisor by the promisee or any other person. It may be past, present or future and need not be adequate, but must be real and lawful.

Section 23 provides that consideration or object is unlawful if it is forbidden by law; would defeat the provisions of any law; is fraudulent; involves or implies injury to the person or property of another; or is regarded by the court as immoral or opposed to public policy. An agreement with unlawful consideration or object is void.

Example: a promise to pay for an unlawful act is not enforceable. A low price alone does not make consideration unlawful, although inadequacy may be relevant to whether consent was freely given under Section 25’s explanation.

Conclusion: consideration must be genuine and lawful, and must satisfy Section 2(d) read with Sections 23–25.

Unit III — Void Agreements, Contingency and Discharge

Void agreements, contingent contracts, frustration, performance, breach and discharge

QUESTION 14 · LONG ANSWERIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain agreements expressly declared void under the Indian Contract Act.

Introduction

Some agreements fail to create enforceable contractual rights because the Act expressly declares them void or because they fail a statutory requirement.

Important categories

  • Agreements with unlawful consideration or object — Section 23.
  • Agreements partly unlawful — Section 24: where unlawful and lawful parts cannot be separated as the section requires, the agreement may be void.
  • Agreements without consideration — Section 25: void unless a statutory exception applies.
  • Restraint of marriage — Section 26: agreements in restraint of the marriage of an adult are void, subject to the exact statutory rule.
  • Restraint of trade — Section 27: agreements restraining lawful profession, trade or business are void to that extent, subject to statutory exceptions such as sale of goodwill and applicable special laws.
  • Restraint of legal proceedings — Section 28: clauses that absolutely restrict enforcement through usual legal proceedings or unlawfully limit time are void, subject to statutory exceptions.
  • Uncertain agreements — Section 29: agreements whose meaning is not certain or capable of being made certain are void.
  • Wagering agreements — Section 30: wagering agreements are void; enforceability and collateral consequences may depend on the applicable state law.
  • Impossible acts — Section 56: an agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void.

Conclusion

For each category, state the section, the rule, any exception and a short example. Do not assume every void agreement is illegal or that every void agreement has identical consequences.

Exam method: Define the concept, state the relevant section, explain the ingredients, apply a case/example where useful, and conclude.

QUESTION 15 · LONG ANSWERPAST-PAPER THEME · verify against original papers

Define a contingent contract and explain the rules governing its enforcement.

Definition — Section 31

A contingent contract is a contract to do or not to do something if some event collateral to the contract does or does not happen.

Rules — Sections 32–36

  • Event happens — Section 32: a contract contingent on an uncertain future event happening cannot be enforced unless and until that event happens; it becomes void if the event becomes impossible.
  • Event does not happen — Section 33: where the contract depends on an event not happening, it can be enforced when the happening becomes impossible.
  • Future conduct of a living person — Section 34: the event may be treated as impossible when that person acts in a way that makes the contingency impossible within the statutory rule.
  • Specified time — Section 35: the Act sets rules for events that must happen or not happen within a fixed time.
  • Impossible event — Section 36: agreements contingent on impossible events are void, whether or not the parties knew of the impossibility.

Example

A agrees to pay B ₹50,000 if B’s house is damaged by a specified storm. The obligation depends on a collateral uncertain event and is enforceable according to the contract and applicable law if the contingency occurs.

Distinction from wager

A contingent contract is not automatically a wager. A wager ordinarily involves reciprocal promises dependent on an uncertain event in which the parties have no interest other than winning or losing the stake. A genuine insurance contract, for example, is generally not a wager because of its insurable interest and regulatory framework.

Conclusion

Identify the contingency, apply the relevant section, and decide whether the event happened, became impossible, or failed to happen within the stipulated time.

QUESTION 16 · LONG ANSWERPAST-PAPER THEME · verify against original papers

Explain the modes of discharge of a contract.

Meaning

Discharge ends the contractual obligations of the parties, fully or to the extent recognised by law.

Main modes

  1. Performance: parties perform their promises as required by Sections 37 onwards. Tender of performance may have consequences under Section 38.
  2. Mutual agreement: novation, rescission or alteration under Section 62; remission or waiver under Section 63.
  3. Impossibility/frustration: Section 56 applies when performance becomes impossible or unlawful after the contract is made, subject to the doctrine’s limits.
  4. Lapse of time: a claim may become barred by the Limitation Act, 1963; limitation generally bars the remedy rather than automatically erasing every underlying obligation.
  5. Operation of law: for example, insolvency consequences, merger or other legal rules where applicable.
  6. Breach: a party’s refusal or failure to perform may entitle the innocent party to terminate and claim remedies, depending on the contract and law.

Conclusion

Classify the facts under the correct mode. Distinguish discharge by agreement from frustration, and distinguish expiry of a limitation remedy from discharge of the underlying obligation.

QUESTION 17 · LONG ANSWERPAST-PAPER THEME · verify against original papers

Explain the doctrine of frustration under Section 56 with decided cases.

Statutory basis

Section 56 provides that an agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void. A contract becomes void when, after it is made, the act becomes impossible or unlawful due to an event the promisor could not prevent, subject to the section and the contract’s terms.

Principles

  • The event must fundamentally affect the basis of performance, not merely make it more expensive or inconvenient.
  • Frustration is not available where the alleged event is self-induced or the contract allocated the risk to the party claiming relief.
  • Temporary difficulty or commercial hardship does not automatically frustrate a contract.
  • Where a contract contains a force majeure clause, the clause should be interpreted first; Section 56 applies according to law and the nature of the contract.
  • Section 65 may require restoration of an advantage received under an agreement that becomes void, subject to the statutory requirements.

Cases

Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur & Co. explains impossibility under Section 56 and that impossibility is not limited to literal physical impossibility. Energy Watchdog v. CERC explains the distinction between contractual force majeure and frustration, and that a rise in cost alone does not ordinarily frustrate a contract.

Example

If a contract depends on the existence of a specific hall and, before performance, the hall is destroyed by an event not allocated as a contractual risk, performance may be frustrated. A mere increase in the price of materials ordinarily is not enough.

Conclusion

Frustration is a narrow doctrine. State Section 56, identify the supervening event, assess its effect on the foundation of the contract, and consider contractual risk allocation.

QUESTION 18 · SHORT NOTEIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain anticipatory breach of contract and its consequences.

Anticipatory breach occurs when, before the due date for performance, a party clearly refuses to perform or disables itself from performing the promise. Section 39 applies where a party refuses to perform, or disables itself from performing, its promise in its entirety, unless the promisee has signified acquiescence in its continuance.

  • The innocent party may treat the contract as ended and claim damages, or in appropriate cases may elect to keep the contract alive and wait for performance.
  • If the innocent party keeps the contract alive, later events may affect the right to sue and the ability to perform; the choice should be made carefully.
  • Damages are governed by Section 73 and the rules of causation, remoteness and mitigation.

Case: Hochster v. De La Tour is a classic English authority recognising an action for anticipatory breach before the performance date.

Conclusion: identify a clear refusal or disabling act, then explain the innocent party’s election and the resulting remedy.

QUESTION 19 · SHORT NOTEIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain wagering agreements and distinguish them from contingent contracts.

Wager — Section 30

A wagering agreement generally involves reciprocal promises to pay money or money’s worth depending on the outcome of an uncertain event, where the parties have no genuine interest in the event other than the stake. Section 30 declares wagering agreements void. State laws may affect the treatment of wagering and collateral transactions.

Wager vs contingent contract

  • A contingent contract under Section 31 depends on a collateral event; a wager is a particular kind of speculative arrangement.
  • In a genuine contingent contract, a party may have a real interest in the subject matter or performance; in a wager, the stake is the parties’ only interest in the outcome.
  • Insurance is generally not a wager where there is insurable interest and the transaction complies with insurance law.

Conclusion

Apply the substance of the transaction, not merely its label, and check relevant state legislation where enforcement is in issue.

QUESTION 29 · PROBLEM QUESTIONPRACTICE QUESTION

Problem: A agrees to sell B “100 litres of oil” without identifying the type, although A deals in several kinds of edible oil. Is the agreement valid?

Issue

Whether the subject matter is sufficiently certain under Section 29 of the Contract Act.

Rule

Agreements whose meaning is not certain or capable of being made certain are void. However, a term need not be written out in full if the parties’ intention can be objectively determined from context, trade usage, prior dealings or an agreed method of selection.

Application

If the parties have not identified the type of oil and no objective basis exists to determine it, the agreement may be void for uncertainty. If trade usage or surrounding circumstances make the intended type certain, the agreement may be enforceable.

Conclusion

The answer depends on whether the type of oil can be made certain from the contract and admissible context. Do not assume the agreement is automatically void solely because the description is brief.

Unit IV — Remedies and Quasi-Contracts

Damages, remoteness, anticipatory breach, penalty and restitutionary obligations

QUESTION 20 · LONG ANSWERPAST-PAPER THEME · verify against original papers

Explain the remedies available for breach of contract.

Introduction

When a contract is broken, the innocent party may seek remedies recognised by the Contract Act, Specific Relief Act and other applicable law. The remedy depends on the contract, the breach and the relief sought.

1. Damages — Sections 73 and 74

Section 73 allows compensation for loss that naturally arose in the usual course of things or that the parties knew, when contracting, was likely to result from breach. Remote and indirect loss is not recoverable. The injured party’s means of avoiding or reducing loss may be relevant.

Section 74 deals with a sum named in the contract or a penalty stipulation. The court awards reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount named, subject to the section; the named sum is not automatically recoverable in full.

2. Specific performance

Under the Specific Relief Act, 1963 as amended, specific performance may be ordered subject to Sections 10–14 and statutory bars. It is not available for every contract; personal-service contracts and contracts requiring continuous duties the court cannot supervise are examples of restricted categories.

3. Injunction

Sections 36–42 of the Specific Relief Act govern preventive relief by injunction, including temporary and perpetual injunctions and mandatory injunctions. The statutory conditions and equitable principles must be satisfied.

4. Rescission and restitution

Rescission may be available where the contract is voidable or in other cases allowed by law. Sections 64–65 of the Contract Act address restoration of benefits in specified situations.

5. Quantum meruit

Where applicable, a party may claim reasonable remuneration for work or benefits provided, for example in circumstances covered by Sections 65 or 70. It is not an automatic alternative to a valid express contract governing the same subject.

Conclusion

Damages are the ordinary remedy for breach. Specific performance and injunctions are available only where their statutory requirements are met.

QUESTION 21 · LONG ANSWERPAST-PAPER THEME · verify against original papers

Explain the rule in Hadley v. Baxendale and the doctrine of remoteness of damage.

Rule

In Hadley v. Baxendale (1854), the court stated that damages for breach may be recovered for losses that arise naturally according to the usual course of things, or losses that may reasonably be supposed to have been in the contemplation of both parties when they made the contract because special circumstances were communicated.

Section 73

Section 73 of the Indian Contract Act follows a similar approach. Compensation is available for loss that naturally arose in the usual course of things or that the parties knew was likely when they made the contract. Remote and indirect loss is excluded.

Application

  • Ordinary loss: loss normally resulting from the breach may be recoverable.
  • Special loss: special circumstances should have been disclosed or otherwise known at formation so that the loss was within the parties’ reasonable contemplation.
  • Proof and mitigation: the claimant must establish the loss and causation and should take reasonable steps to limit avoidable loss.

Example

If a carrier delays delivery of a machine part, ordinary delay-related loss may be foreseeable. Extraordinary profits lost because a unique factory will stop may not be recoverable unless the special circumstances were made known and the legal requirements are met.

Conclusion

The rule limits damages to foreseeable kinds of loss, protecting parties from liability for remote consequences they could not reasonably contemplate.

QUESTION 22 · SHORT NOTEIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain liquidated damages and penalty under Section 74.

Section 74 applies where a contract names a sum payable on breach or contains another stipulation by way of penalty. The aggrieved party is entitled to reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount named or penalty stipulated.

  • The court does not automatically award the full named amount.
  • Compensation must be reasonable in light of the contract, breach and applicable legal principles.
  • Proof of exact loss may not be necessary in every case, especially where loss is difficult to quantify, but the section does not authorise a windfall.

Case: Fateh Chand v. Balkishan Das is a leading Supreme Court authority on reasonable compensation under Section 74. Kailash Nath Associates v. DDA discusses the requirements for compensation and the relevance of loss or legal injury.

Conclusion: distinguish a genuine pre-estimate from a penalty in analysis, but remember that Section 74’s controlling test is reasonable compensation within the statutory ceiling.

QUESTION 23 · LONG ANSWER / SHORT NOTEIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain quasi-contractual obligations and their types under the Contract Act.

Meaning

Quasi-contracts are not contracts formed by offer and acceptance. They are obligations imposed by law to prevent one person from being unjustly enriched at another’s expense. The Contract Act deals with them in Sections 68–72.

Types

  1. Necessaries supplied to an incapable person — Section 68: a person supplying necessaries suited to the condition in life of a person incapable of contracting, or someone they are legally bound to support, may be reimbursed from that incapable person’s property. This is not personal contractual liability.
  2. Payment by an interested person — Section 69: a person interested in paying money that another is legally bound to pay, and who pays it, may be entitled to reimbursement if the statutory conditions are met.
  3. Non-gratuitous act — Section 70: where a person lawfully does something for another or delivers something, not intending to act gratuitously, and the other enjoys the benefit, compensation or restoration may be required.
  4. Finder of goods — Section 71: a finder who takes goods into custody has the responsibilities of a bailee.
  5. Money or thing received by mistake or coercion — Section 72: a person receiving money or goods by mistake or under coercion must repay or return them.

Case law

State of West Bengal v. B.K. Mondal & Sons is an important case on Section 70 and non-gratuitous acts.

Conclusion

For a problem, identify the relevant section, the benefit received, the absence of a gratuitous intention where required, and the remedy of reimbursement or restoration.

QUESTION 30 · PROBLEM QUESTIONPRACTICE QUESTION

Problem: A carrier knows that a delayed machine part will stop B’s entire factory, but still delivers it late. What loss can B claim?

Issue

Whether factory shutdown losses are recoverable or too remote.

Rule

Section 73 and Hadley v. Baxendale permit recovery of losses that naturally arise in the usual course of things or that the parties knew at contract formation were likely to result from breach. Remote and indirect loss is excluded, and avoidable loss may be reduced.

Application

If B communicated the special circumstances at formation and the shutdown loss was reasonably within the parties’ contemplation, B may claim proven foreseeable losses, subject to causation and mitigation. If the carrier did not know and the loss was extraordinary, the special loss may be too remote.

Conclusion

B can claim foreseeable, proven and legally caused loss; special shutdown loss is more likely recoverable if the carrier knew the relevant circumstances when contracting.

QUESTION 24 · LONG ANSWERIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain contracts which can be specifically enforced under the Specific Relief Act, 1963.

Current statutory framework

The Specific Relief Act, 1963 was substantially amended in 2018. Section 10 now provides that specific performance of a contract shall be enforced by the court subject to Sections 11(2), 14 and 16. Read the current amended text, not an outdated description that treats specific performance as wholly discretionary in the old sense.

Key conditions and bars

  • Section 10: specific performance is enforceable subject to the statutory exceptions and personal bars.
  • Section 11: contracts connected with trusts are governed by the provision; a contract made by a trustee in excess of powers or in breach of trust is not specifically enforceable as stated in Section 11(2).
  • Section 14: certain contracts cannot be specifically enforced, including where substituted performance has been obtained, contracts involving continuous duties the court cannot supervise, contracts dependent on personal qualifications, and contracts that are in their nature determinable, as the section specifies.
  • Section 16: personal bars apply, including failure to prove performance or readiness and willingness where required by the statute, and other specified disqualifications.
  • Section 20: substituted performance is available under the amended Act, subject to statutory notice and procedure; obtaining it affects the ability to seek specific performance as provided by law.

Conclusion

Specific performance requires a valid enforceable contract, satisfaction of the statutory conditions and absence of a bar. Always refer to the post-2018 amended provisions.

QUESTION 25 · LONG ANSWERIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Define injunction and explain its types under the Specific Relief Act.

Meaning

An injunction is a court order requiring a party to do a specified act or restraining a party from doing an act. The Specific Relief Act provisions on injunctions include Sections 36–42, read with the Code of Civil Procedure and other applicable law.

Types

  • Temporary injunction: governed principally by procedural law, including Order XXXIX of the Code of Civil Procedure. It operates for a specified period or until further order.
  • Perpetual injunction — Section 37: granted by decree at the hearing on the merits, permanently restraining the defendant from asserting a right or committing an act contrary to the plaintiff’s rights.
  • Mandatory injunction — Section 39: compels performance of certain acts necessary to prevent or remedy a breach of obligation, where the court considers it proper.
  • Prohibitory injunction: restrains specified conduct; it may be temporary or perpetual depending on the order.

Important provisions

Section 38 concerns when a perpetual injunction may be granted; Section 40 permits damages in addition to or in substitution for an injunction in appropriate cases; Section 41 lists situations in which an injunction must be refused; Section 42 deals with injunction to perform a negative agreement, subject to its conditions.

Conclusion

State the type of injunction, the right or obligation protected, the relevant statutory provision and any statutory bar.

Explain recovery of specific immovable property and specific movable property.

Immovable property — Sections 5 and 6

Section 5 provides for recovery of specific immovable property according to the Code of Civil Procedure. Section 6 provides a summary remedy for a person dispossessed of immovable property without consent and otherwise than in due course of law, subject to its conditions and limitation period. A suit under Section 6 is not a substitute for deciding ultimate title.

Movable property — Sections 7 and 8

Section 7 permits a person entitled to possession of specific movable property to recover it according to civil procedure. Section 8 allows delivery of specific movable property in specified circumstances, for example where the defendant holds it as agent or trustee, where monetary compensation is inadequate, or where the property cannot readily be obtained in the market, subject to the section.

QUESTION 27 · SHORT NOTEIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain cancellation of instruments under Sections 31–33 of the Specific Relief Act.

Section 31: when cancellation may be ordered

A person against whom a written instrument is void or voidable, and who reasonably apprehends that leaving it outstanding may cause serious injury, may sue to have it adjudged void or voidable. The court may order cancellation and, where appropriate, direct the instrument to be delivered up and cancelled.

Section 32: partial cancellation

Where an instrument evidences different rights or obligations and the circumstances permit, the court may cancel it in part and allow the remainder to stand.

Section 33: restoration and compensation

On cancellation, the court may require a party to restore benefits received or make compensation as justice requires, subject to the section.

Example and conclusion

If a deed was procured by fraud and remains capable of being used to harm the claimant, cancellation may be sought if statutory conditions are met. Explain the instrument, the ground of voidness or voidability, the risk of injury and any consequential restoration.

QUESTION 28 · SHORT NOTEIMPORTANT SYLLABUS TOPIC

Explain rescission of contracts under the Specific Relief Act.

Sections 27–30 of the Specific Relief Act govern rescission in specified circumstances. A person interested in a contract may seek rescission where the contract is voidable or terminable by the plaintiff, or where it is unlawful for reasons not apparent on its face and the defendant is more to blame, subject to the statutory conditions.

Rescission may be refused in circumstances specified by Section 27, including where the plaintiff has expressly or impliedly ratified the contract, restoration of the parties to substantially their original positions is not possible in relevant cases, third-party rights have intervened in good faith for value, or only an inseverable part is sought to be rescinded, as the Act provides.

Sections 29–30 address alternative relief and the court’s power to require restoration of benefits. Rescission sets aside the contract; cancellation of an instrument focuses on removing a written instrument that may cause injury.

Conclusion: state the ground for rescission, check the statutory bars, and address restoration of benefits.
