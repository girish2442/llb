How to use this question bank

Use short answers to memorise article numbers and definitions. For medium answers, explain the provision and add a leading case. For long answers, cover the constitutional text, legal principles, case law and a clear conclusion.

Answer-writing formula: Introduction → Relevant Article → Rule / constitutional test → Case law → Application or significance → Conclusion.
The mark categories are preparation formats, not a guarantee of the exact marks or question pattern in a particular KSLU examination. Model answers should be revised against the prescribed textbook and official syllabus.

Part A – 2-mark questions

2 MARKS

1. What does Article 15 prohibit?

Article 15 prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. It also protects access to specified public places under clause (2).
2 MARKS

2. What is Article 15(3)?

Article 15(3) permits the State to make special provisions for women and children. It is a constitutional basis for protective and welfare measures.
2 MARKS

3. What is Article 15(4)?

Article 15(4) enables the State to make special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
2 MARKS

4. What is protective discrimination?

Protective discrimination means special measures taken to improve the position of disadvantaged groups and achieve substantive equality. It includes constitutionally permitted affirmative-action measures.
2 MARKS

5. What is Article 16?

Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity for all citizens in matters relating to employment or appointment to an office under the State.
2 MARKS

6. What does Article 16(4) provide?

Article 16(4) enables the State to make provision for reservation of appointments or posts in favour of a backward class of citizens that, in the opinion of the State, is not adequately represented in State services.
2 MARKS

7. What is Article 17?

Article 17 abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form. The enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability is an offence punishable in accordance with law.
2 MARKS

8. Name one law enacted to give effect to Article 17.

The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955. The Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 is also relevant.
2 MARKS

9. How many freedoms are guaranteed under Article 19(1)?

Six freedoms are currently guaranteed under Article 19(1).
2 MARKS

10. Which clause protects freedom of speech and expression?

Article 19(1)(a), subject to the reasonable restrictions under Article 19(2).
2 MARKS

11. Which clause deals with freedom of peaceful assembly?

Article 19(1)(b), subject to Article 19(3).
2 MARKS

12. Which clause protects the freedom to form associations?

Article 19(1)(c), subject to Article 19(4).
2 MARKS

13. What is the restriction clause for freedom of profession or trade?

Article 19(6) permits reasonable restrictions on the freedom guaranteed by Article 19(1)(g).
2 MARKS

14. Name a leading case on reasonable restrictions under Article 19.

State of Madras v. V.G. Row (1952) is a leading case on assessing the reasonableness of restrictions.

Part B – 5-mark questions

5 MARKS

1. Explain the scope of Article 15.

Article 15 prohibits discrimination against citizens on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Clause (2) protects access to specified public places and facilities. At the same time, clauses (3) to (6) enable special provisions for women and children, socially and educationally backward classes, SCs/STs and EWS, subject to the wording of each clause.

These provisions show that the Constitution seeks both to prevent discrimination and to promote substantive equality through affirmative action. State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) is important in the development of Article 15(4), which was introduced by the First Constitutional Amendment.

5 MARKS

2. Explain protective discrimination under the Constitution.

Protective discrimination refers to special measures for groups that have suffered historical or social disadvantage. Its object is to achieve substantive equality by addressing unequal starting conditions.

Article 15(3) permits special provisions for women and children. Article 15(4) and 15(5) concern the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes and SCs/STs, including educational admissions. Article 15(6) concerns EWS. In public employment, Article 16(4), 16(4A), 16(4B) and 16(6) provide specified enabling provisions.

In Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992), the Supreme Court considered OBC reservation and the creamy-layer principle. The Constitution therefore permits affirmative action within constitutional limits.

5 MARKS

3. Explain Article 16 and equality of opportunity in public employment.

Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity to citizens in public employment. Clause (1) states the general guarantee, while clause (2) prohibits discrimination on specified grounds. Clause (3) concerns residence requirements prescribed by Parliament in the constitutionally permitted manner.

Clause (4) enables reservation for inadequately represented backward classes. Clauses (4A) and (4B) deal with specified aspects of reservation in promotion and carry-forward vacancies. Clause (5) concerns religious offices, and clause (6) enables EWS reservation.

In Indra Sawhney (1992), the Court examined OBC reservation, creamy layer and reservation principles. Article 16 seeks fair access to public employment while permitting constitutionally authorised measures to remedy disadvantage.

5 MARKS

4. Explain Article 17 and its significance.

Article 17 abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form. It makes enforcement of any disability arising from untouchability an offence punishable in accordance with law.

It aims to end caste-based exclusion and social disabilities. Unlike provisions that primarily regulate State action, Article 17 also addresses private social practices. Parliament enacted the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955, and the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 provides further protection against specified atrocities.

Article 17 is a significant constitutional commitment to dignity, equality and social justice.

5 MARKS

5. List the six freedoms under Article 19(1).

  1. Freedom of speech and expression – 19(1)(a).
  2. Peaceful assembly without arms – 19(1)(b).
  3. Form associations, unions or co-operative societies – 19(1)(c).
  4. Move freely throughout India – 19(1)(d).
  5. Reside and settle in any part of India – 19(1)(e).
  6. Practise any profession or carry on any occupation, trade or business – 19(1)(g).

The former property freedom under Article 19(1)(f) was omitted by the 44th Amendment.

5 MARKS

6. Explain the reasonable restrictions on freedom of speech and expression.

Article 19(1)(a) protects freedom of speech and expression. Article 19(2) permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation and incitement to an offence.

A restriction must fall within one of these grounds and must be reasonable. In State of Madras v. V.G. Row (1952), the Supreme Court explained that reasonableness depends on the nature of the right, purpose and extent of the restriction and relevant circumstances. In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015), Section 66A of the IT Act was struck down because it did not satisfy constitutional standards for restricting speech.

5 MARKS

7. Explain the restrictions on freedom of movement and residence.

Article 19(1)(d) protects the right of citizens to move freely throughout India, and Article 19(1)(e) protects the right to reside and settle in any part of India. Under Article 19(5), reasonable restrictions may be imposed in the interests of the general public or for the protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.

These restrictions recognise that individual freedom may need to be balanced with public welfare and the protection of vulnerable communities. The restriction must remain within the grounds expressly stated in Article 19(5).

5 MARKS

8. Explain the restrictions on freedom of profession, occupation, trade or business.

Article 19(1)(g) gives citizens the right to practise any profession or carry on any occupation, trade or business. Article 19(6) permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of the general public. It also permits laws prescribing professional or technical qualifications and laws enabling the State to carry on specified trade, business, industry or service to the exclusion of citizens, wholly or partly.

Thus, the right is important but not absolute. Public health, safety, standards and regulation may justify restrictions if constitutionally valid and reasonable.

Part C – 10-mark questions

10 MARKS

1. Discuss Articles 15 and 16 as constitutional guarantees of equality and social justice.

Introduction: Articles 15 and 16 give practical effect to equality by prohibiting specified discrimination and enabling special measures to address disadvantage.

Article 15: Clause (1) prohibits discrimination against citizens on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Clause (2) protects access to specified public places. Clauses (3) to (6) enable special provisions for women and children, socially and educationally backward classes, SCs/STs and EWS, subject to the constitutional text.

Article 16: Clause (1) guarantees equality of opportunity in public employment. Clause (2) prohibits discrimination on specified grounds. Other clauses enable certain reservation measures, including provisions for backward classes, SCs/STs and EWS, and address particular employment situations.

Case law: Champakam Dorairajan (1951) led to the insertion of Article 15(4). Indra Sawhney (1992) considered OBC reservation, creamy layer and reservation limits. M. Nagaraj (2006) examined reservation in promotion.

Conclusion: Articles 15 and 16 combine the prohibition of discrimination with constitutionally permitted affirmative action to promote substantive equality and social justice.

10 MARKS

2. Explain Article 17 and the constitutional and legal measures for abolition of untouchability.

Introduction: Article 17 abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form.

Constitutional rule: The enforcement of any disability arising from untouchability is an offence punishable in accordance with law. The Article seeks to eliminate caste-based exclusion and protect human dignity.

Legal measures: The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 penalises the practice of untouchability and enforcement of related disabilities. The Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 addresses specified atrocities and offences against SCs and STs.

Significance: Article 17 is not merely a restriction on government. It is directed against social practices and can operate in relation to private conduct through implementing laws.

Conclusion: Article 17 is a powerful constitutional instrument for social reform, equality and dignity.

10 MARKS

3. Explain the six freedoms under Article 19 and the reasonable restrictions on them.

Introduction: Article 19(1) guarantees six freedoms to citizens. These freedoms are essential to individual liberty and democratic participation but are subject to reasonable restrictions.

Six freedoms and restrictions:

  1. Speech and expression – 19(1)(a), restricted under 19(2) on specified grounds.
  2. Peaceful assembly without arms – 19(1)(b), restricted under 19(3) on grounds of sovereignty and integrity and public order.
  3. Associations, unions and co-operative societies – 19(1)(c), restricted under 19(4) on grounds of sovereignty and integrity, public order and morality.
  4. Movement – 19(1)(d), and residence/settlement – 19(1)(e), restricted under 19(5) in the interests of the general public or protection of Scheduled Tribes.
  5. Profession, occupation, trade or business – 19(1)(g), restricted under 19(6) in the interests of the general public, including qualifications and State monopoly provisions.

Case law: Romesh Thappar and Bennett Coleman concern speech and press freedom. V.G. Row explains reasonableness. Shreya Singhal illustrates the limits on restrictions on speech.

Conclusion: Each freedom must be read with its own restriction clause. A restriction must be authorised by law, fall within the permitted ground and be reasonable.

Part D – 15-mark / essay questions

15 MARKS

1. “The Constitution permits protective discrimination to achieve substantive equality.” Discuss with reference to Articles 15 and 16.

Suggested answer structure: define formal and substantive equality; explain Article 15(1) and (2); discuss special provisions under Article 15(3), (4), (5) and (6); explain Article 16(1) and (2); discuss reservation provisions under Article 16(4), (4A), (4B) and (6); explain the role of social justice; refer to Champakam Dorairajan, Indra Sawhney, M. Nagaraj and Janhit Abhiyan; conclude that affirmative action is constitutionally enabled but must comply with the text and judicial standards.

15 MARKS

2. Explain the constitutional abolition of untouchability and its importance in Indian society.

Suggested answer structure: explain the meaning and social context of untouchability; state Article 17; explain that the prohibition applies to practice in any form and addresses private as well as public conduct; discuss the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 and SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989; explain dignity, equality and social reform; conclude that Article 17 is a direct constitutional rejection of caste-based exclusion.

15 MARKS

3. “The freedoms under Article 19 are not absolute.” Explain all six freedoms and their reasonable restrictions.

Suggested answer structure: introduction; explain that Article 19 rights are available to citizens; discuss all six freedoms with clause numbers; match each with its exact restriction clause; explain reasonableness and judicial review; discuss V.G. Row, Chintaman Rao, Romesh Thappar, Bennett Coleman and Shreya Singhal; conclude that restrictions must fit within the specific constitutional grounds and be reasonable.

Part E – Problem questions with IRAC answers

Problem 1 – Refusal of entry to a public restaurant based on caste

Facts: A restaurant open to the public refuses entry to a citizen because of the person's caste.

Issue: Whether the refusal violates Article 15(2).

Rule: Article 15(2) prohibits denial of access to shops, public restaurants, hotels and specified public facilities on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.

Application: The restaurant is open to the public and the refusal is expressly based on caste. This falls within the constitutional prohibition, subject to proof of the facts.

Conclusion: The refusal is likely unconstitutional under Article 15(2). Depending on the facts, relevant statutory remedies may also apply.

Problem 2 – Special admission provision for women

Facts: A State establishes a special educational programme for women to address barriers to access. A candidate challenges it as discrimination based on sex.

Issue: Whether the special programme violates Article 15(1).

Rule: Article 15(1) prohibits specified discrimination, but Article 15(3) expressly permits special provisions for women and children.

Application: If the measure is genuinely a special provision for the advancement or welfare of women and is otherwise constitutionally valid, Article 15(3) may support it.

Conclusion: The measure is not automatically invalid merely because it benefits women; it must be assessed under Article 15(3) and the facts.

Problem 3 – Prohibition of a public meeting

Facts: Citizens plan a peaceful meeting without arms. The authorities prohibit it, citing a possible disturbance of public order.

Issue: Whether the prohibition is a valid restriction on Article 19(1)(b).

Rule: Article 19(1)(b) protects peaceful assembly without arms. Article 19(3) permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of sovereignty and integrity of India or public order.

Application: The authority must show a proper connection between the restriction and the permitted ground. A blanket or arbitrary prohibition without sufficient justification may be challenged.

Conclusion: The validity depends on the facts, evidence of public-order concerns and proportionality/reasonableness of the restriction.

Problem 4 – Online post and restriction of speech

Facts: A person posts criticism of a government policy online. The State penalises the person under a broadly worded rule that does not clearly identify a ground in Article 19(2).

Issue: Whether the restriction violates Article 19(1)(a).

Rule: Freedom of speech is protected by Article 19(1)(a). A restriction must fall within one of the grounds in Article 19(2) and satisfy constitutional standards of reasonableness.

Application: Mere criticism of government policy is not by itself a ground listed in Article 19(2). A vague or overbroad restriction may deter protected speech. Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) is relevant.

Conclusion: If the rule cannot be justified under Article 19(2), the restriction may be unconstitutional.
