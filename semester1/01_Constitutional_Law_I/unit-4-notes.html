1. Unit IV – Syllabus scope

  • Article 20: protection in respect of conviction for offences.
  • Article 21: protection of life and personal liberty; judicial interpretation and expansion of rights.
  • Article 22: safeguards against arrest and detention, including preventive detention.
  • Right against Exploitation: Articles 23 and 24.
  • Secularism and freedom of religion: Articles 25–28 and the constitutional limits on religious freedom.
Important syllabus clarification: The Right to Life and Personal Liberty is Article 21, not Article 20. Article 20 deals with protection in respect of conviction for offences. Article 22 deals with safeguards relating to arrest and detention.

2. Article 20 – Protection in respect of conviction for offences

Article 20 provides three important protections to persons accused or convicted of offences. These safeguards protect fairness in criminal justice and limit the power of the State.

Article 20(1) – Protection against ex post facto criminal laws

No person shall be convicted of an offence except for violation of a law in force at the time of the commission of the act charged as an offence. Nor shall a person be subjected to a penalty greater than the penalty that could have been imposed under the law in force when the offence was committed.

  • A person cannot be convicted for an act that was not an offence when it was done.
  • A heavier criminal penalty cannot be imposed retrospectively.
  • The protection is against retrospective criminal liability and heavier punishment.
  • A beneficial or lighter penalty may be applied retrospectively where the law so permits.

Article 20(2) – Protection against double jeopardy

No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. The constitutional protection requires both prosecution and punishment in the earlier proceeding, and the later proceeding must concern the same offence.

Article 20(3) – Protection against self-incrimination

No person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. The protection applies to an accused person and guards against compelled testimonial self-incrimination.

Memory aid: Article 20 = (1) No retrospective criminal punishment; (2) No double jeopardy; (3) No compelled self-incrimination.

Leading cases

Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay (1953) – Customs confiscation proceedings were not treated as a prosecution and punishment by a court or judicial tribunal for the purpose of Article 20(2).

S.A. Venkataraman v. Union of India (1954) – Departmental proceedings were distinguished from prosecution for an offence for Article 20(2).

State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961) – The protection against self-incrimination concerns testimonial compulsion; physical evidence such as fingerprints or specimen signatures is not ordinarily treated as compelled testimony.

Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani (1978) – The protection against compelled self-incrimination was considered in the context of police interrogation and questioning.

3. Article 21 – Protection of life and personal liberty

Article 21 states: “No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.” It applies to every person, including citizens and non-citizens.

Meaning and importance

  • “Life” means more than mere animal existence; it includes living with human dignity.
  • “Personal liberty” has been interpreted broadly to protect important aspects of individual freedom.
  • Deprivation must be authorised by law and the procedure must satisfy constitutional standards of fairness, justice and reasonableness.

Development through judicial interpretation

A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) – The early approach interpreted the fundamental rights separately and treated “procedure established by law” more narrowly.

Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) – The Supreme Court held that the procedure depriving a person of life or liberty must be right, just and fair, not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive. Articles 14, 19 and 21 are interconnected where applicable.

Core principle after Maneka Gandhi: A law affecting personal liberty must satisfy Article 21 and cannot be arbitrary; it may also need to meet the requirements of Articles 14 and 19 depending on the nature of the right and State action.

The Supreme Court has interpreted Article 21 to include several rights necessary for a life with dignity. The following are important examples for examination:

Right to privacy

In Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), a nine-judge bench unanimously recognised privacy as a fundamental right. Privacy is connected with dignity, autonomy and liberty. It is not absolute and may be restricted by a constitutionally valid law satisfying applicable requirements of legitimate State aim, necessity and proportionality.

Right to die with dignity – careful distinction

In Common Cause v. Union of India (2018), the Court recognised the right to die with dignity in the context of passive euthanasia and advance medical directives, subject to safeguards. This does not create a general fundamental right to commit suicide.

5. Article 22 – Protection against arrest and detention

Article 22 provides safeguards to persons arrested or detained. It distinguishes ordinary arrest under criminal law from preventive detention.

Safeguards for ordinary arrest – Article 22(1) and (2)

  1. An arrested person must be informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds of arrest.
  2. The person has the right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of their choice.
  3. The arrested person must be produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours of arrest, excluding the time necessary for the journey.
  4. No person may be detained in custody beyond that period without the authority of a magistrate.

Exceptions under Article 22(3)

The safeguards in clauses (1) and (2) do not apply to an enemy alien or a person arrested or detained under a law providing for preventive detention. Preventive detention is nevertheless subject to the safeguards in clauses (4) to (7) and other constitutional requirements.

Exam distinction: Ordinary arrest is generally connected with investigation or prosecution of an alleged offence. Preventive detention is intended to prevent a person from acting in a manner prejudicial to specified interests in the future.

Important cases

D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997) – The Supreme Court laid down safeguards and guidelines relating to arrest and detention to prevent custodial abuse.

Joginder Kumar v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1994) – Arrest should not be routine merely because it is lawful; the necessity and justification for arrest matter.

6. Preventive detention

Preventive detention means detention of a person without an ordinary criminal trial, with the object of preventing that person from acting in a manner prejudicial to specified interests. It is preventive, not punitive.

Constitutional safeguards

  • Article 22(4): A person cannot be detained under a preventive detention law beyond the constitutionally permitted initial period without the required review by an Advisory Board, subject to the clause and applicable law.
  • Article 22(5): The detaining authority must communicate the grounds of detention as soon as may be and give the earliest opportunity to make a representation against the order.
  • Article 22(6): Facts may be withheld if disclosure is considered against the public interest.
  • Article 22(7): Parliament may by law prescribe circumstances and classes of cases for detention beyond the specified period without obtaining an Advisory Board opinion, and the maximum period of detention, within the constitutional framework.

Safeguards and judicial review

Preventive detention is an exceptional power. Courts may examine whether the law and procedure have been followed, whether the grounds are relevant and sufficiently communicated, whether the representation was considered promptly, and whether there was mala fide or non-application of mind.

A.K. Roy v. Union of India (1982) – Considered constitutional safeguards and aspects of preventive detention under the National Security Act.

Khudiram Das v. State of West Bengal (1975) – Subjective satisfaction in preventive detention is not beyond judicial review; courts may intervene on recognised grounds such as mala fides or irrelevant material.

Remember: Do not confuse preventive detention with punishment after conviction. The object of preventive detention is prevention of anticipated prejudicial conduct, subject to constitutional safeguards.

7. Right against Exploitation – Articles 23 and 24

Articles 23 and 24 protect individuals against exploitation. They form part of the Fundamental Rights in Part III and are enforceable against State action and, in important respects, private conduct as well.

Article 23 – Prohibition of traffic in human beings, begar and forced labour

  • Article 23(1) prohibits traffic in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour. A contravention is an offence punishable according to law.
  • The protection is available to every person, not only citizens.
  • It operates against the State and private persons; the prohibition is not confined to government employment.
  • Article 23(2) permits the State to impose compulsory service for public purposes, but the State cannot discriminate on grounds only of religion, race, caste or class when imposing such service.

Forced labour: Compulsion may arise not only from physical force or legal compulsion but also from economic circumstances. Work extracted for less than the applicable minimum wage may amount to forced labour in the constitutional sense, depending on the facts.

Article 24 – Prohibition of employment of children in factories, etc.

No child below the age of fourteen years shall be employed to work in any factory or mine or engaged in any other hazardous employment. Read this constitutional protection with Article 21A, the child-labour legislation and other applicable child-protection laws. Do not overstate Article 24 as a blanket constitutional ban on every form of work by every person under eighteen; its text identifies children below fourteen and the specified prohibited employment.

Important cases

  • People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982): The Supreme Court explained the broad meaning of forced labour under Article 23; economic compulsion and payment below the statutory minimum wage may be relevant.
  • Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984): The Court addressed bonded labour and the State's duty to identify, release and rehabilitate bonded labourers.
  • M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996): The Court dealt with child labour in hazardous industries and directions for protection, rehabilitation and education.
Exam distinction: Article 23 concerns trafficking, begar and forced labour. Article 24 concerns employment of children below fourteen in factories, mines and hazardous employment.

8. Secularism – Constitutional principle

Secularism is a basic feature of the Indian constitutional framework. The State has no official religion and must act consistently with constitutional guarantees of freedom of conscience, equality and religious liberty. Indian secularism does not mean that religion is excluded from public life in every circumstance; the Constitution permits regulation of secular activities associated with religious practice and social reform, subject to its provisions.

  • Article 25: Freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and the other provisions of Part III. The State may regulate secular activities associated with religious practice and make laws for social welfare and reform.
  • Article 26: Rights of religious denominations to establish institutions, manage religious affairs, own and acquire property and administer it in accordance with law, subject to public order, morality and health.
  • Article 27: No person may be compelled to pay taxes specifically appropriated for promotion or maintenance of a particular religion or religious denomination.
  • Article 28: Deals with religious instruction and worship in educational institutions, with distinctions based on whether an institution is wholly State-funded, State-administered under an endowment or trust, or recognised/receiving aid.

Leading cases: S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) recognised secularism as a basic feature of the Constitution. Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments, Madras v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Shirur Mutt (1954) explained the distinction between matters of religion and secular administration. Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986) considered freedom of conscience and the rights of students who respectfully declined to sing the national anthem on religious grounds.

Answer tip: For a 10/15-mark answer, explain secularism first, then Articles 25–28 separately, their limitations and one or two relevant cases.

9. Freedom of religion – Articles 25 to 28

The Constitution guarantees freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and the other provisions of Part III.

Article 25 – Freedom of conscience and free profession, practice and propagation

  • Applies to all persons.
  • Protects freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion.
  • Subject to public order, morality, health and the other provisions of Part III.
  • The State may regulate or restrict secular activities associated with religious practice and may make laws for social welfare and reform.

Article 26 – Freedom to manage religious affairs

Every religious denomination or section thereof has the right, subject to public order, morality and health, to establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes; manage its own affairs in matters of religion; own and acquire property; and administer such property in accordance with law.

Article 27 – Freedom from taxes for promotion of a particular religion

No person shall be compelled to pay taxes, the proceeds of which are specifically appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination.

Article 28 – Religious instruction in educational institutions

  • No religious instruction shall be provided in an educational institution wholly maintained out of State funds.
  • This prohibition does not apply to an institution administered by the State but established under an endowment or trust requiring religious instruction.
  • No person attending a State-recognised or State-aided institution can be compelled to take part in religious instruction or worship without consent; for a minor, the guardian's consent is required.

Important principles and cases

Commissioner, Hindu Religious Endowments v. Sri Lakshmindra Thirtha Swamiar of Shirur Mutt (1954) – The Court discussed the distinction between matters of religion and secular administration of religious institutions.

Rev. Stainislaus v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1977) – The right to propagate religion does not include a fundamental right to convert another person by force, fraud or allurement.

Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986) – Students who respectfully stood during the National Anthem but did not sing due to their genuine religious belief were protected under freedom of conscience and expression on the facts.

Exam point: Religious freedom is broad but not absolute. It is subject to public order, morality, health and other Fundamental Rights. The State may regulate secular aspects and enact social welfare and reform laws.

11. Last-minute revision sheet

  • Article 20(1): No retrospective conviction or heavier penalty.
  • Article 20(2): No double jeopardy – prosecution and punishment for the same offence.
  • Article 20(3): No compelled self-incrimination.
  • Article 21: No deprivation of life or personal liberty except according to constitutionally valid procedure established by law.
  • Maneka Gandhi: Procedure must be fair, just and reasonable.
  • Article 22(1)–(2): Grounds of arrest, lawyer, magistrate within 24 hours.
  • Article 22(4)–(7): Preventive detention safeguards.
  • Article 25: Freedom of conscience and profession, practice and propagation of religion.
  • Article 26: Manage religious affairs.
  • Article 27: No tax specifically appropriated to promote a particular religion.
  • Article 28: Rules on religious instruction in educational institutions.
Do not mix up: Article 20 = criminal conviction safeguards; Article 21 = life and personal liberty; Article 22 = arrest and detention safeguards.
