Part A – 2-mark questions

2 MARKS

1. What is Article 29(1)?

Article 29(1) gives any section of citizens having a distinct language, script or culture the right to conserve it.
2 MARKS

2. What is Article 29(2)?

Article 29(2) prohibits denial of admission to a citizen in a State-maintained or State-aided educational institution on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language.
2 MARKS

3. What does Article 30 protect?

Article 30 protects the right of religious and linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.
2 MARKS

4. Why is Article 32 called the heart and soul of the Constitution?

It guarantees the right to approach the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and empowers the Court to issue appropriate directions, orders and writs. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described it as the heart and soul of the Constitution.
2 MARKS

5. What is Article 226?

Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue directions, orders and writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose.
2 MARKS

6. Name the five constitutional writs.

Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari and Quo Warranto.
2 MARKS

7. What is Habeas Corpus?

Habeas Corpus is a writ used to challenge unlawful detention and seek production of the detained person before the court.
2 MARKS

8. What is Mandamus?

Mandamus is a writ directing a public authority to perform a public or legal duty that it has failed or refused to perform.
2 MARKS

9. What is Article 300A?

Article 300A provides that no person shall be deprived of property except by authority of law. It protects property as a constitutional right, not a Fundamental Right.
2 MARKS

10. What are Directive Principles of State Policy?

DPSP are constitutional guidelines in Part IV, Articles 36–51, directing the State to promote social and economic welfare and justice.
2 MARKS

11. Are DPSP enforceable by courts?

No. Under Article 37, DPSP are not enforceable by any court, but they are fundamental in the governance of the country.
2 MARKS

12. What are Fundamental Duties?

Fundamental Duties are civic and constitutional obligations of citizens listed in Part IVA, Article 51A. There are eleven duties.
2 MARKS

13. Which amendment introduced Fundamental Duties?

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 introduced Fundamental Duties. The 86th Amendment, 2002 added the eleventh duty concerning educational opportunities for children aged six to fourteen.
2 MARKS

14. Which case held that harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSP is part of the basic structure?

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980).

Part B – 5-mark questions

5 MARKS

1. Explain the cultural and educational rights under Articles 29 and 30.

Article 29(1) protects the right of any section of citizens having a distinct language, script or culture to conserve it. Article 29(2) prohibits denial of admission to a citizen in a State-maintained or State-aided educational institution on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language.

Article 30(1) gives religious and linguistic minorities the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. Article 30(2) prevents discrimination in granting aid merely because an institution is managed by a minority. These provisions protect diversity and educational autonomy while allowing reasonable regulation to maintain standards.

5 MARKS

2. Distinguish Articles 32 and 226.

Article 32 empowers the Supreme Court to issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and is itself a Fundamental Right. Article 226 empowers High Courts to issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose, including other legal rights. Article 226 therefore has a wider subject-matter scope. Both are important constitutional remedies and are part of the judicial review framework.

5 MARKS

3. Explain the five writs.

  1. Habeas Corpus: Challenges unlawful detention.
  2. Mandamus: Commands a public authority to perform a legal duty.
  3. Prohibition: Prevents a lower court or tribunal from proceeding beyond jurisdiction.
  4. Certiorari: Quashes an order of a lower court, tribunal or authority on recognised grounds.
  5. Quo Warranto: Challenges the legal authority of a person holding a public office.
5 MARKS

4. Explain the right to property under Article 300A.

Article 300A states that no person shall be deprived of property except by authority of law. The right to property was formerly a Fundamental Right under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31. The 44th Amendment removed those provisions and inserted Article 300A. Property is now a constitutional right, not a Fundamental Right. Deprivation must be supported by law and comply with constitutional requirements. K.T. Plantation v. State of Karnataka (2011) is an important case.

5 MARKS

5. Explain the nature and importance of DPSP.

DPSP are contained in Part IV, Articles 36–51. They guide the State in making laws and policies to promote social and economic justice and establish a welfare society. Under Article 37, they are not enforceable by courts but are fundamental in the governance of the country. They cover matters such as welfare, livelihood, equal pay, legal aid, education, public health, environment and international peace.

5 MARKS

6. Classify the Directive Principles.

The Constitution does not formally classify DPSP, but they are commonly grouped for study as:

  • Socialist principles: Articles 38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 43A and 47.
  • Gandhian principles: Articles 40, 43, 46, 47 and 48.
  • Liberal-intellectual principles: Articles 44, 45, 48A, 49, 50 and 51.

This classification is a study aid; some Articles may fit more than one theme.

5 MARKS

7. Explain Fundamental Duties under Article 51A.

Fundamental Duties are listed in Part IVA, Article 51A. Ten duties were introduced by the 42nd Amendment, 1976, and an eleventh duty was added by the 86th Amendment, 2002. They include respecting the Constitution and national symbols, protecting sovereignty and unity, promoting harmony, preserving culture, protecting the environment, developing scientific temper, safeguarding public property and providing educational opportunities to children aged six to fourteen. They are generally non-justiciable by themselves, but may guide legislation and judicial interpretation.

5 MARKS

8. Explain the relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSP.

Fundamental Rights in Part III are generally enforceable by courts and protect individual liberty. DPSP in Part IV are non-justiciable guidelines for social and economic welfare. In Champakam Dorairajan (1951), the Court held that Fundamental Rights prevailed in a conflict under the law then applicable. Later, Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and Minerva Mills (1980) emphasised constitutional balance. Minerva Mills held that harmony between Parts III and IV is part of the basic structure.

Part C – 10-mark questions

10 MARKS

1. Discuss the constitutional protection of minority cultural and educational rights.

Introduction: Articles 29 and 30 protect India's cultural and educational diversity.

Article 29: Clause (1) protects the right of any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture to conserve it. Clause (2) prohibits denial of admission to a citizen in a State-maintained or aided educational institution on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language.

Article 30: Religious and linguistic minorities have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. The State cannot discriminate in granting aid merely because an institution is minority-managed.

Regulation: The right to administer does not mean a right to maladminister. Reasonable regulations to maintain educational standards are permissible if they do not destroy the protected minority character.

Cases: Kerala Education Bill (1958), T.M.A. Pai Foundation (2002) and P.A. Inamdar (2005).

Conclusion: Articles 29 and 30 protect cultural identity, educational autonomy and pluralism within the constitutional framework.

10 MARKS

2. Explain the writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts under Articles 32 and 226.

Article 32: Guarantees access to the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Court may issue directions, orders and writs.

Article 226: Empowers High Courts to issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose, including other legal rights.

Five writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari and Quo Warranto. Explain the purpose of each and give a short example.

Difference: Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right and is limited to Fundamental Rights. Article 226 has wider subject-matter reach but is a discretionary constitutional remedy subject to jurisdictional and judicial principles.

Case: L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997) affirmed the central role of judicial review under Articles 32 and 226/227.

Conclusion: Writ jurisdiction is an essential safeguard against unlawful State action and protects the rule of law.

10 MARKS

3. Explain the nature and importance of Directive Principles of State Policy.

Introduction: DPSP are contained in Part IV, Articles 36–51 and guide the State in promoting social and economic justice.

Article 37: They are not enforceable by courts but are fundamental in governance; it is the duty of the State to apply them in making laws.

Objectives: Welfare, reduction of inequality, fair distribution of resources, livelihood, equal pay, legal aid, humane working conditions, education, public health, environmental protection and international peace.

Classification: Commonly grouped as socialist, Gandhian and liberal-intellectual principles for study, though the Constitution does not formally classify them.

Importance: DPSP guide legislation and public policy and help courts interpret constitutional provisions harmoniously.

Conclusion: Although non-justiciable, DPSP are fundamental to the constitutional vision of a welfare State.

10 MARKS

4. Discuss Fundamental Duties under Article 51A.

Introduction: Fundamental Duties are in Part IVA, Article 51A and express civic and constitutional responsibilities of citizens.

Origin: The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 introduced ten duties. The 86th Amendment Act, 2002 added the duty of parents or guardians to provide educational opportunities to children aged six to fourteen.

Content: The duties concern respect for the Constitution and national symbols, sovereignty and unity, national service, harmony, dignity of women, cultural heritage, environment, scientific temper, public property, excellence and children's education.

Enforceability: They are generally not directly enforceable by themselves, but Parliament may enact laws to give effect to them and courts may use them as interpretive aids.

Conclusion: Fundamental Duties remind citizens that constitutional democracy requires responsible participation as well as rights.

10 MARKS

5. Explain the relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSP with reference to important cases.

Introduction: Fundamental Rights protect individual freedoms; DPSP guide the State towards social and economic justice.

Champakam Dorairajan (1951): The Court held that DPSP could not override Fundamental Rights in a conflict under the constitutional position then applicable.

Kesavananda Bharati (1973): Parliament's amendment power is wide but cannot damage the basic structure.

Minerva Mills (1980): Harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSP form part of the basic structure. The Court rejected an attempt to give blanket primacy to all DPSP over Fundamental Rights.

Article 31C: The protection is limited to laws genuinely giving effect to Article 39(b) and (c), as interpreted by the Supreme Court.

Conclusion: Parts III and IV should be harmoniously interpreted; neither should be treated as destroying the other.

Part E – Problem questions with IRAC answers

Problem 1 – Minority institution and State regulation

Facts: A linguistic minority establishes a school. The State prescribes minimum teacher qualifications and academic standards. The management argues that Article 30 gives it complete freedom from regulation.

Issue: Whether Article 30 prevents all State regulation of a minority educational institution.

Rule: Article 30(1) protects the right of religious and linguistic minorities to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice. The right to administer does not mean a right to maladminister. Reasonable regulations to maintain educational standards may be permissible if they do not destroy the minority character or substantially take away the protected right.

Application: Teacher qualifications and academic standards may be legitimate regulatory measures, depending on their design and effect on institutional autonomy.

Conclusion: The institution is not automatically exempt from regulation. The validity of the rules depends on whether they are reasonable and preserve the substance of Article 30.

Problem 2 – Public authority refuses to perform a statutory duty

Facts: A public authority refuses to consider an application even though a statute requires it to decide eligible applications.

Issue: Which writ may compel the authority to perform its duty?

Rule: Mandamus may be issued to direct a public authority to perform a public or statutory duty that it has failed or refused to perform.

Application: If the authority has a legal duty to consider the application and has unlawfully refused to act, the applicant may seek mandamus from a competent High Court under Article 226, subject to maintainability requirements.

Conclusion: Mandamus is the appropriate writ to consider, depending on the facts and available remedies.

Problem 3 – Government takes private land without legal authority

Facts: Officials take possession of a person's land without identifying any law authorising the deprivation.

Issue: Whether the action violates Article 300A.

Rule: Article 300A states that no person shall be deprived of property except by authority of law.

Application: If the State has taken possession without legal authority, the constitutional requirement is not satisfied. The affected person may seek an appropriate legal remedy, including under Article 226 where maintainable.

Conclusion: Deprivation of property without authority of law is constitutionally impermissible under Article 300A.

Problem 4 – Conflict between a Fundamental Right and a Directive Principle

Facts: A State law claims to implement a Directive Principle but restricts a Fundamental Right. The State argues that every law implementing DPSP automatically prevails over Fundamental Rights.

Issue: Does every law implementing any DPSP automatically override Fundamental Rights?

Rule: Fundamental Rights and DPSP must be read harmoniously. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) held that balance between Parts III and IV is part of the basic structure. Article 31C protection is limited to laws genuinely giving effect to Article 39(b) and (c), subject to judicial review.

Application: The State cannot rely on a general DPSP to claim blanket immunity. The court must examine the law, the right affected and whether Article 31C or another constitutional basis applies.

Conclusion: The State's blanket argument is incorrect. The law must satisfy the applicable constitutional requirements.
