How to use this question bank

Practise the short answers for definitions and article numbers. For medium answers, explain the rule and add a case. For long answers, use an introduction, constitutional provision, legal principles, leading cases and conclusion.

Answer-writing formula: Meaning / Introduction → Relevant Article → Legal test or principle → Case law → Application or significance → Conclusion.
The mark categories below are preparation formats, not a guarantee of the exact marks or question pattern in a particular KSLU examination. These are model answers to practise and revise.

Part A – 2-mark questions

2 MARKS

1. What is Article 12?

Article 12 defines the term “State” for the purpose of Part III of the Constitution. It includes the Government and Parliament of India, State Governments and Legislatures, local authorities and other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India.
2 MARKS

2. What are “other authorities” under Article 12?

“Other authorities” is a broad expression that may include statutory corporations, government companies and bodies functioning as an instrumentality or agency of government. Courts examine factors such as government ownership, financial support, deep control and public functions.
2 MARKS

3. What is meant by pre-Constitution law?

A pre-Constitution law is a law that existed before 26 January 1950. Under Article 13(1), it continues to operate but becomes void to the extent that it is inconsistent with Fundamental Rights.
2 MARKS

4. What is a post-Constitution law?

A post-Constitution law is a law made after the Constitution came into force. Under Article 13(2), the State cannot make a law that takes away or abridges Fundamental Rights; such a law is void to the extent of the contravention.
2 MARKS

5. What is the doctrine of severability?

The doctrine of severability means that when only a separable part of a law violates Fundamental Rights, the court may strike down that part and allow the valid remainder to continue if it can operate independently.
2 MARKS

6. What is the doctrine of eclipse?

The doctrine of eclipse means that a pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right becomes dormant or unenforceable to the extent of the inconsistency, rather than necessarily being erased from the statute book.
2 MARKS

7. State the two parts of Article 14.

Article 14 guarantees (1) equality before the law and (2) equal protection of the laws.
2 MARKS

8. What is equality before law?

Equality before law is a negative concept. It means that no person is above the law and all persons are subject to the ordinary law of the land, subject to constitutionally recognised exceptions.
2 MARKS

9. What is equal protection of laws?

Equal protection of laws is a positive concept. It requires equal treatment of persons who are similarly situated and permits reasonable different treatment of persons who are genuinely different.
2 MARKS

10. What are the two tests of reasonable classification?

The classification must be based on (1) intelligible differentia and (2) a rational nexus between that differentia and the object of the law.
2 MARKS

11. Name a leading case on the meaning of “State” under Article 12.

Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981) is a leading case. It explains that a society may be treated as “State” if it is in substance an instrumentality or agency of government.
2 MARKS

12. Name a leading case on the doctrine of eclipse.

Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955) is a leading case on the doctrine of eclipse.
2 MARKS

13. Does Article 14 apply only to citizens?

No. Article 14 uses the expression “any person” and protects citizens as well as non-citizens within the territory of India, subject to the Constitution.
2 MARKS

14. What is class legislation?

Class legislation means making an arbitrary or unreasonable law that favours or burdens a particular group without a valid basis. Article 14 prohibits class legislation but permits reasonable classification.

Part B – 5-mark questions

5 MARKS

1. Explain the scope of Article 12.

Article 12 defines “State” for Part III. It includes the Union Government and Parliament, State Governments and Legislatures, local authorities and other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India.

The expression “other authorities” has been interpreted broadly. It may cover statutory corporations and bodies that function as government instrumentalities. Courts examine government ownership, financial assistance, administrative control, public functions and whether a government department has been transferred to the body.

In R.D. Shetty and Ajay Hasia, the Supreme Court explained the indicators for identifying an instrumentality or agency of government. In Pradeep Kumar Biswas, the Court emphasised financial, functional and administrative domination and pervasive control.

Thus, Article 12 has a wide scope and ensures that bodies substantially controlled by government cannot avoid Fundamental Rights obligations merely by adopting a separate legal form.

5 MARKS

2. Distinguish pre-Constitution and post-Constitution laws under Article 13.

Pre-Constitution lawPost-Constitution law

Both provisions protect Fundamental Rights and allow courts to review laws for constitutional validity.

5 MARKS

3. Explain the doctrine of severability.

The doctrine of severability allows a court to preserve the valid portion of a law when another portion violates Fundamental Rights. The unconstitutional part is removed only if it can be separated from the valid part.

The remaining provisions must be workable and capable of operating independently. The court also considers legislative intention: whether the legislature would have enacted the remaining valid portion on its own. If the valid and invalid parts are inseparable, the whole law or provision may be invalid.

In R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala v. Union of India (1957), the Supreme Court explained the principles governing severability.

5 MARKS

4. Explain the doctrine of eclipse.

The doctrine of eclipse mainly applies to pre-Constitution laws that conflict with Fundamental Rights. Under Article 13(1), such a law becomes unenforceable to the extent of the inconsistency. It is described as being eclipsed by the Fundamental Right.

The law is not necessarily erased from the statute book. If the constitutional obstacle is later removed, it may become enforceable again to the extent permitted by law.

In Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955), the Court applied this principle to a pre-Constitution law after the constitutional position changed.

5 MARKS

5. Distinguish equality before law and equal protection of laws.

Both expressions appear in Article 14. Equality before law is a negative concept associated with the rule of law: no person is above the law. Equal protection of laws is a positive concept requiring equal treatment of persons in similar circumstances.

Equal protection does not mean that every person must be treated identically. The State may classify persons for a legitimate purpose, provided the classification satisfies the tests of intelligible differentia and rational nexus.

5 MARKS

6. Explain the reasonable classification test under Article 14.

Article 14 prohibits class legislation but permits reasonable classification. A valid classification must satisfy two conditions:

  1. There must be an intelligible differentia distinguishing the persons or things grouped together from those excluded.
  2. The differentia must have a rational nexus with the object sought to be achieved by the law.

Both conditions must be satisfied. If the classification is arbitrary or unrelated to the object, it may violate Article 14. Ram Krishna Dalmia v. Justice S.R. Tendolkar (1958) is an important authority on this test.

5 MARKS

7. Explain the relationship between Article 14 and arbitrariness.

Article 14 is not limited to the prohibition of unreasonable classification. The Supreme Court has recognised that arbitrariness is opposed to equality. In E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974), the Court explained that equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies. Therefore, arbitrary State action may violate Article 14 even where a traditional classification analysis is not sufficient.

In Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), the Court reinforced the requirement that State action and procedure must be fair, just and non-arbitrary.

5 MARKS

8. Explain the importance of Article 13 in protecting Fundamental Rights.

Article 13 gives effect to Fundamental Rights by providing a constitutional standard against which laws can be tested. It deals with pre-Constitution laws under clause (1), prohibits the State from making rights-violating laws under clause (2), gives a broad meaning to “law” under clause (3), and excludes constitutional amendments under Article 368 from Article 13 through clause (4), subject to the Basic Structure limitation on the amending power.

It empowers constitutional courts to declare a law void to the extent of inconsistency or contravention and is therefore central to judicial review.

Part C – 10-mark questions

10 MARKS

1. Discuss the meaning and scope of “State” under Article 12 with leading cases.

Introduction: Article 12 defines “State” for the purpose of Part III. The definition identifies the authorities against which Fundamental Rights can generally be enforced.

Constitutional scope: It includes the Union Government and Parliament, State Governments and Legislatures, local authorities and other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India.

Other authorities: The Supreme Court has interpreted this phrase broadly. In Rajasthan Electricity Board v. Mohan Lal (1967), a statutory board was included within the expression. In R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority (1979), the Court identified indicators for deciding whether a body is an instrumentality or agency of government.

In Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981), the Court held that the legal form of a body is not decisive; a registered society may be “State” if it is in substance an instrumentality or agency of government. In Pradeep Kumar Biswas (2002), the Court emphasised financial, functional and administrative domination and pervasive control. In Zee Telefilms (2005), BCCI was held not to be “State” under Article 12 on the facts.

Conclusion: Article 12 has a broad and purposive meaning. The court examines the total relationship between the body and government rather than relying only on its name or legal form.

10 MARKS

2. Explain Article 13 and discuss the doctrines of severability and eclipse.

Introduction: Article 13 safeguards Fundamental Rights by declaring laws inconsistent with those rights void to the extent of the inconsistency.

Pre-Constitution laws: Article 13(1) provides that existing laws continue except to the extent they conflict with Fundamental Rights. Post-Constitution laws: Article 13(2) prohibits the State from making laws that take away or abridge Fundamental Rights. Article 13(3) gives “law” a wide meaning. Article 13(4) states that Article 13 does not apply to constitutional amendments under Article 368, but the Basic Structure doctrine limits the amending power.

Doctrine of severability: If the unconstitutional portion can be separated and the remainder can operate independently, the court may strike down only the offending portion. The principle is explained in R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala v. Union of India (1957).

Doctrine of eclipse: A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right becomes dormant to the extent of the inconsistency. It is not necessarily wiped out and may revive if the constitutional obstacle is removed. Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955) is the leading illustration.

Conclusion: These doctrines help courts protect Fundamental Rights while preserving valid laws wherever constitutionally possible.

10 MARKS

3. Explain the scope and judicial interpretation of Article 14.

Introduction: Article 14 guarantees equality before law and equal protection of laws to every person within India.

Meaning: Equality before law is a negative concept: no person is above the law. Equal protection is a positive concept: persons in similar circumstances should be treated alike.

Reasonable classification: Article 14 does not prohibit every classification. A valid classification must be based on intelligible differentia and must have a rational nexus with the object of the law. In Ram Krishna Dalmia v. Justice S.R. Tendolkar (1958), the Court explained the principles of reasonable classification. In State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952), arbitrary selection for special procedure was held unconstitutional.

Arbitrariness: Article 14 also prohibits arbitrary State action. In E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974), the Court connected equality with the absence of arbitrariness. In Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), fairness and non-arbitrariness in State procedure were emphasised. In D.S. Nakara v. Union of India (1983), an unreasonable pension cut-off date was struck down on the facts.

Conclusion: Article 14 is a guarantee against both unreasonable classification and arbitrary State action. It is a central protection for the rule of law.

Part D – 15-mark / essay questions

15 MARKS

1. “Article 12 has been given a wide interpretation by the judiciary.” Discuss.

Write a full answer using the Article 12 definition, four categories of authorities, meaning of local authorities and “other authorities”, the instrumentality/agency tests, and the cases Rajasthan Electricity Board, R.D. Shetty, Ajay Hasia, Pradeep Kumar Biswas and Zee Telefilms. Conclude that the inquiry is fact-sensitive and considers the cumulative degree of government domination and control.

15 MARKS

2. Discuss Article 13 and explain the doctrines of severability and eclipse with case law.

Structure the answer under: introduction and purpose of Article 13; clauses (1) to (4); meaning of law under clause (3); pre- and post-Constitution laws; doctrine of severability and its conditions; R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala; doctrine of eclipse and its features; Bhikaji Narain Dhakras; comparison of the two doctrines; conclusion on judicial review and protection of Fundamental Rights.

15 MARKS

3. “Equality is a dynamic concept and Article 14 is a guarantee against arbitrariness.” Explain.

Begin with the text of Article 14 and explain equality before law and equal protection. Discuss the prohibition of class legislation and the permission for reasonable classification. Explain both classification tests. Then trace the judicial development through Anwar Ali Sarkar, Ram Krishna Dalmia, E.P. Royappa, Maneka Gandhi and D.S. Nakara. Conclude that Article 14 controls both discriminatory classification and arbitrary State action.

Part E – Problem questions with answer framework

Problem 1 – Different tax rates for two classes of hotels

Facts: A State law imposes a higher tax on luxury hotels and a lower tax on small roadside eating houses. A hotel owner challenges the law under Article 14.

Issue: Whether the different tax treatment violates Article 14.

Rule: Article 14 permits reasonable classification if there is intelligible differentia and rational nexus with the object of the law.

Application: Luxury hotels and small eating houses may differ in scale, facilities, clientele and capacity to pay. If these differences are real and the tax distinction reasonably advances a legitimate revenue or taxation objective, the classification may be valid. If the distinction is arbitrary or unrelated to the object, it may fail.

Conclusion: The validity depends on the evidence and the purpose of the tax law. Different treatment alone does not automatically violate Article 14.

Problem 2 – Government corporation refuses a service without fair procedure

Facts: A statutory corporation substantially owned and controlled by government refuses a public service to an applicant without giving reasons.

Issue: Whether the corporation is “State” under Article 12 and whether its action violates Article 14.

Rule: Under Article 12, a body may be State if it is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by government. Article 14 prohibits arbitrary State action.

Application: Examine ownership, financial support, deep administrative control, public function and the overall relationship with government. If the corporation qualifies as State, its unexplained and arbitrary refusal may be challenged under Article 14, depending on the facts and applicable rules.

Conclusion: The applicant has a possible constitutional challenge if the corporation is State and the refusal is arbitrary or discriminatory.

Problem 3 – One section of an Act violates equality

Facts: One section of an Act violates Article 14, but the remaining sections can function independently.

Issue: Whether the entire Act must be struck down.

Rule: The doctrine of severability allows the unconstitutional part to be removed if it is separable and the valid remainder can operate independently.

Application: The court examines the text, legislative intention, workability of the remaining provisions and whether the valid part would stand independently.

Conclusion: If the offending section is genuinely separable, only that section may be invalidated; otherwise the wider law may fail.

Problem 4 – Pre-Constitution law later becomes consistent

Facts: A law enacted before 1950 conflicts with a Fundamental Right. A later constitutional amendment removes the conflict.

Issue: Can the earlier law operate after the conflict is removed?

Rule: Under the doctrine of eclipse, a pre-Constitution law may become dormant to the extent of inconsistency rather than being completely erased.

Application: If the constitutional obstacle is validly removed, the law may become enforceable again, subject to the terms of the amendment and other constitutional limitations.

Conclusion: The law may revive to the extent legally permissible. Bhikaji Narain Dhakras is the key case to discuss.
