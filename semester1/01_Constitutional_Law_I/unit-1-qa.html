Part A – 2-mark questions and answers

1. Define a Constitution. 2 marks

A Constitution is the fundamental or supreme law of a country. It establishes the organs of government, distributes their powers, limits the exercise of public power and provides the framework for protecting rights.

2. What is Constitutionalism? 2 marks

Constitutionalism is the principle that government must function within constitutional limits. It requires limited government, rule of law, protection of rights and accountability of public authorities.

3. What is a written Constitution? 2 marks

A written or codified Constitution is one in which the principal constitutional rules are organised in an authoritative written document. The Constitution of India is a written and detailed Constitution.

4. Distinguish between rigid and flexible Constitution. 2 marks

A rigid Constitution requires a special and comparatively difficult procedure for amendment. A flexible Constitution can be amended through a procedure similar to ordinary law-making. The Indian Constitution combines both features.

5. What is meant by a federal Constitution? 2 marks

A federal Constitution divides constitutional powers between a national government and regional governments. In India, powers are divided between the Union and the States, with a strong Union structure.

6. What is the Preamble? 2 marks

The Preamble is the introductory statement to the Constitution. It declares the source of constitutional authority and sets out the nature of the Indian State and the objectives of Justice, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity.

7. Which words were added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment? 2 marks

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 added “Socialist” and “Secular” and inserted the word “Integrity” in the expression “unity and integrity of the Nation”.

8. Is the Preamble part of the Constitution? 2 marks

Yes. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the Supreme Court held that the Preamble is part of the Constitution. It can be amended under Article 368, subject to the basic structure limitation.

9. Which Articles of the Constitution deal with citizenship? 2 marks

Articles 5 to 11 in Part II of the Constitution deal with citizenship. Article 11 empowers Parliament to make laws regulating citizenship.

10. Name the five modes of acquiring citizenship under the Citizenship Act, 1955. 2 marks

Citizenship may be acquired by birth, descent, registration, naturalisation and incorporation of territory, under Sections 3 to 7 respectively, subject to the Act's conditions.

11. Name the three modes of termination of citizenship. 2 marks

The three modes are renunciation (Section 8), termination (Section 9) and deprivation (Section 10) under the Citizenship Act, 1955.

12. What is domicile? 2 marks

Domicile generally means a person's permanent home. It involves residence together with the intention to make that place a permanent home. Mere temporary residence does not automatically establish domicile.

13. What is meant by a Republic? 2 marks

A Republic is a State in which the head of State is not a hereditary monarch. In India, the President is the constitutional head of the Union and is elected according to the Constitution.

14. What is meant by a Secular State? 2 marks

A secular State does not establish one official State religion. It is required to deal with religions in accordance with constitutional principles and protect freedom of religion.

Part B – 5-mark questions and answers

1. Explain the need for a Constitution. 5 marks

Introduction: A Constitution is the basic legal framework of a country. It is necessary for orderly and limited government.

  1. Creates government: It establishes the legislature, executive and judiciary.
  2. Distributes powers: It allocates functions among the organs and, in a federation, between levels of government.
  3. Limits authority: It prevents arbitrary exercise of public power.
  4. Protects rights: It recognises and safeguards constitutional rights.
  5. Provides stability: It establishes procedures for government and orderly constitutional change.

Conclusion: A Constitution provides the legal foundation for government while protecting people against misuse of power.

2. Explain the different kinds of Constitution. 5 marks

Constitutions may be classified on different bases:

  1. Written and uncodified: A written Constitution is organised in a formal document; an uncodified Constitution draws on statutes, conventions and judicial decisions.
  2. Rigid and flexible: A rigid Constitution requires a special amendment process; a flexible one is easier to amend.
  3. Federal and unitary: A federal system divides powers between central and regional governments; a unitary system concentrates ultimate authority centrally.
  4. Parliamentary and presidential: In a parliamentary system the executive is responsible to the legislature; in a presidential system the executive is separately constituted.

India has a written Constitution, a mixture of rigidity and flexibility, a federal structure with a strong Centre and a parliamentary system.

3. Write a short note on Constitutionalism. 5 marks

Constitutionalism means government under constitutional limits. Its main principles are limited government, rule of law, separation of powers, Fundamental Rights, independent judiciary, judicial review and accountability. These principles ensure that public power is exercised lawfully and not arbitrarily. In Kesavananda Bharati (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament's amending power cannot destroy the basic structure. In Minerva Mills (1980), the Court emphasised limited amending power. Thus, constitutionalism requires not merely a Constitution in writing but respect for its limits in practice.

4. Explain any five salient features of the Indian Constitution. 5 marks

Five important features are:

  1. Written and detailed Constitution.
  2. Federal structure with a strong Union.
  3. Parliamentary form of government.
  4. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
  5. Independent judiciary with judicial review.

Other features include a secular democratic republic, Fundamental Duties, single citizenship, universal adult franchise, a combination of rigidity and flexibility, and emergency provisions.

5. Explain the significance of the Preamble. 5 marks

The Preamble is the introductory statement of the Constitution. It identifies “We, the People of India” as the source of authority. It describes India as Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic and Republic. It sets out Justice, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity as constitutional objectives. It guides the interpretation of the Constitution where provisions are ambiguous, though it is not an independent source of governmental power. In Kesavananda Bharati (1973), the Supreme Court held that it is part of the Constitution.

6. Explain the four objectives in the Preamble. 5 marks
  1. Justice: Social, economic and political justice.
  2. Liberty: Liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship.
  3. Equality: Equality of status and opportunity.
  4. Fraternity: A sense of brotherhood assuring individual dignity and the unity and integrity of the nation.

These objectives express the values that guide the constitutional and democratic life of India.

7. Explain the importance of the 42nd Amendment to the Preamble. 5 marks

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 made three notable changes to the Preamble. It inserted “Socialist” and “Secular” after “Sovereign” and added “Integrity” to the phrase “unity and integrity of the Nation”. These insertions expressly emphasised social and economic justice, the secular character of the State and national unity and integrity. The Preamble remains subject to the basic structure doctrine.

8. Briefly explain Articles 5 to 11 relating to citizenship. 5 marks

Articles 5–11 form Part II of the Constitution. Article 5 dealt with citizenship at commencement. Articles 6 and 7 address specified categories of persons who migrated between India and Pakistan. Article 8 concerns persons of Indian origin residing outside India. Article 9 deals with voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship. Article 10 provides for continuance of citizenship subject to law. Article 11 gives Parliament power to regulate citizenship. Parliament enacted the Citizenship Act, 1955.

9. Explain the modes of termination of citizenship. 5 marks

Under the Citizenship Act, 1955, citizenship may be terminated in three ways:

  1. Renunciation (Section 8): A citizen voluntarily makes a declaration renouncing Indian citizenship, subject to statutory conditions.
  2. Termination (Section 9): Voluntary acquisition of citizenship of another country may result in termination of Indian citizenship, subject to the Act.
  3. Deprivation (Section 10): The Central Government may deprive a person of citizenship in specified circumstances and by following the prescribed procedure.

Part C – 10-mark questions and answers

1. Define Constitution and discuss its different kinds. 10 marks

Introduction

A Constitution is the fundamental law of a country. It creates the institutions of government, distributes their powers, limits their authority and provides the legal basis for the State.

Classification of Constitutions

1. Written and uncodified: A written Constitution places the principal constitutional rules in a formal document. An uncodified Constitution is drawn from several sources such as statutes, conventions and judicial decisions. The Indian Constitution is written; the UK is commonly described as uncodified, although it has many written constitutional laws.

2. Rigid and flexible: A rigid Constitution requires a special amendment procedure. A flexible Constitution may be amended through a process similar to ordinary legislation. India combines both approaches.

3. Federal and unitary: A federal Constitution divides powers between national and regional governments. A unitary Constitution concentrates ultimate authority in the central government. India has a federal structure with a strong Centre.

4. Parliamentary and presidential: In a parliamentary system, the executive is drawn from and responsible to the legislature. In a presidential system, the executive is separately constituted and generally has a fixed tenure. India follows the parliamentary system.

Indian position

India has a written and detailed Constitution, a combination of rigidity and flexibility, a federal arrangement with a strong Union and a parliamentary executive.

Conclusion

Constitutional classifications help us understand how power is organised and amended. The Indian Constitution adopts a mixed and distinctive model suited to its needs.

2. Explain Constitutionalism and discuss its essential principles. 10 marks

Introduction and meaning

Constitutionalism is the principle that government must be limited by the Constitution and must exercise public power according to law. A Constitution may exist as a document, but constitutionalism requires real observance of constitutional limits.

Essential principles

  1. Limited government: No organ may exercise unlimited power.
  2. Rule of law: Government and citizens are subject to law.
  3. Separation of powers: Governmental functions are distributed among legislature, executive and judiciary, with checks and balances.
  4. Fundamental Rights: Rights protect individual liberty and dignity.
  5. Independent judiciary: Courts must be able to decide disputes impartially.
  6. Judicial review: Courts examine whether laws and executive actions comply with the Constitution.
  7. Accountability: Public authorities must explain and justify their actions.
  8. Democratic government: Free and fair elections provide democratic legitimacy.

Judicial support

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) established that Parliament cannot destroy the basic structure through amendment. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) reaffirmed limited amending power and constitutional balance.

Conclusion

Constitutionalism ensures that government is not merely established by law but is also controlled by law.

3. Discuss the salient features of the Constitution of India. 10 marks

Introduction

The Constitution of India establishes a democratic system and provides a framework for governance, rights and social transformation.

Salient features

  1. Written and detailed Constitution: It contains comprehensive provisions for government and constitutional institutions.
  2. Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic: These terms express the character and goals of the Indian State.
  3. Federal system with a strong Centre: Powers are divided between Union and States, but the Union has significant constitutional powers.
  4. Parliamentary government: The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha at Union level.
  5. Fundamental Rights: Part III protects important rights subject to constitutional restrictions.
  6. Directive Principles: Part IV guides the State towards social and economic welfare.
  7. Fundamental Duties: Part IVA identifies duties of citizens.
  8. Independent judiciary: Courts interpret the Constitution and protect legal and constitutional rights.
  9. Single citizenship: India provides one citizenship for the country as a whole.
  10. Universal adult franchise: Adult citizens may vote subject to constitutional and statutory qualifications.
  11. Rigidity and flexibility: Amendment procedures vary according to the subject.
  12. Emergency provisions: Special constitutional powers apply in exceptional situations.

Cases

Kesavananda Bharati (1973) concerns the basic structure. S.R. Bommai (1994) is important for federalism and secularism. Minerva Mills (1980) concerns limited amending power and the relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

Conclusion

The Indian Constitution combines federalism, parliamentary democracy, individual rights and welfare objectives within a constitutional framework.

4. Explain the Preamble to the Constitution of India and its importance. 10 marks

Introduction

The Preamble is the introductory statement of the Constitution. It expresses the source of constitutional authority, the nature of the State and the objectives to be achieved.

Source of authority and nature of State

The words “We, the People of India” indicate that the Constitution derives its authority from the people. India is described as Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic and Republic.

Objectives

  • Justice: Social, economic and political.
  • Liberty: Thought, expression, belief, faith and worship.
  • Equality: Status and opportunity.
  • Fraternity: Dignity of the individual and unity and integrity of the nation.

42nd Amendment

The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 inserted “Socialist” and “Secular” and added “Integrity” to the phrase “unity and integrity of the Nation”.

Judicial interpretation

In In re Berubari Union (1960), the Court expressed the view that the Preamble was not part of the Constitution. In Kesavananda Bharati (1973), it held that the Preamble is part of the Constitution and may be amended, subject to the basic structure doctrine.

Importance and conclusion

The Preamble is an important guide to constitutional interpretation and expresses the ideals of the Constitution. It does not independently confer governmental powers, but it helps explain the Constitution's purpose and philosophy.

5. Explain the constitutional provisions relating to citizenship in India. 10 marks

Introduction

Citizenship identifies a person's legal membership of the State. Part II of the Constitution, Articles 5–11, deals with citizenship, while Parliament regulates the subject under Article 11. The Citizenship Act, 1955 is the principal legislation.

Constitutional provisions

Article 5 dealt with citizenship at commencement. Articles 6 and 7 concern specified migration between India and Pakistan. Article 8 concerns persons of Indian origin residing outside India. Article 9 deals with voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship. Article 10 provides for continuance of citizenship subject to law. Article 11 empowers Parliament to regulate citizenship.

Acquisition under the Citizenship Act, 1955

  1. Birth – Section 3: The rule depends on date of birth. Broadly, births from 26 January 1950 to before 1 July 1987 are covered by the earlier birth rule subject to exceptions; from 1 July 1987 to before 3 December 2004 at least one parent must be an Indian citizen; on or after 3 December 2004 both parents must be citizens or one must be a citizen and the other not an illegal migrant, subject to statutory exceptions.
  2. Descent – Section 4: Citizenship may be acquired by birth outside India to an Indian parent, subject to statutory requirements, including registration where applicable.
  3. Registration – Section 5: Specified categories may apply if the prescribed conditions are met.
  4. Naturalisation – Section 6: A foreign national may apply after satisfying the qualifications in the Act and Third Schedule, subject to statutory discretion.
  5. Incorporation of territory – Section 7: The Central Government may specify persons who become citizens when a territory becomes part of India.

Termination

Citizenship may end by renunciation (Section 8), termination following voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship (Section 9), or deprivation by the Central Government in specified circumstances (Section 10).

Conclusion

The Constitution lays down the framework and gives Parliament power to regulate citizenship. The 1955 Act provides the detailed modes and conditions of acquisition and termination.

Part D – 15-mark questions and answers

1. “The Constitution is the foundation of a democratic State.” Define Constitution and explain its nature, kinds and importance with reference to India. 15 marks

1. Introduction

A Constitution is the supreme legal framework of a country. It establishes the organs of government, distributes their powers, limits public authority and recognises the rights and duties of people. It is the foundation on which the political and legal system operates.

2. Nature and functions of a Constitution

  • It creates the legislature, executive and judiciary.
  • It distributes powers and defines institutional relationships.
  • It limits arbitrary government action.
  • It protects rights and establishes remedies.
  • It provides continuity, stability and procedures for change.
  • It expresses the fundamental values and objectives of the State.

3. Kinds of Constitution

Written and uncodified: A written Constitution is organised in a formal document. An uncodified Constitution draws on multiple sources, including statutes, conventions and decisions. India has a written Constitution; the UK is commonly described as uncodified, not as having no written laws.

Rigid and flexible: A rigid Constitution requires a special amendment process; a flexible one is amended more easily. India combines both.

Federal and unitary: Federalism divides powers between central and regional governments; a unitary system concentrates ultimate authority centrally. India has a federal structure with a strong Centre.

Parliamentary and presidential: Parliamentary government makes the executive responsible to the legislature; presidential government separately constitutes the executive. India follows the parliamentary model.

4. Nature of the Indian Constitution

India has a written and detailed Constitution; a federal arrangement with a strong Union; parliamentary government; an independent judiciary; Fundamental Rights; Directive Principles; Fundamental Duties; and a mixture of rigid and flexible amendment procedures.

5. Importance of constitutionalism

The Constitution must operate with constitutionalism: limited government, rule of law, separation of powers, judicial review, rights protection and accountability. Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and Minerva Mills (1980) are important for the basic structure and limits on amending power.

6. Conclusion

The Constitution is both a framework of government and a restraint on government. The Indian Constitution combines democratic institutions, federal arrangements, rights and welfare objectives to maintain a constitutional democracy.

2. Discuss the Preamble in detail. Explain its ideals, legal status, amendability and judicial interpretation. 15 marks

1. Introduction

The Preamble is the introductory declaration to the Constitution. It states the source of constitutional authority, describes the Indian State and identifies the objectives the Constitution seeks to secure.

2. “We, the People of India”

These words indicate popular sovereignty: the Constitution derives its authority from the people of India.

3. Nature of the Indian State

  • Sovereign: India is independent in its internal and external affairs.
  • Socialist: The State is committed to social and economic justice and reducing inequalities.
  • Secular: The State has no official religion and must act consistently with constitutional religious freedom and equality.
  • Democratic: Government is based on the will of the people and representative institutions.
  • Republic: The head of State is not a hereditary monarch.

4. Objectives

Justice – social, economic and political. Liberty – thought, expression, belief, faith and worship. Equality – status and opportunity. Fraternity – dignity of the individual and unity and integrity of the nation.

5. 42nd Amendment Act, 1976

The Amendment inserted “Socialist” and “Secular” and added “Integrity” to the expression “unity and integrity of the Nation”.

6. Judicial interpretation

In In re Berubari Union (1960), the Supreme Court expressed the view that the Preamble was not part of the Constitution. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the Court held that the Preamble is part of the Constitution. The Preamble can be amended under Article 368, but the basic structure cannot be destroyed.

7. Importance and conclusion

The Preamble is a guide to the philosophy and interpretation of the Constitution. It is not an independent source of power, but it helps courts understand constitutional objectives. It summarises the ideals of Indian constitutional democracy.

3. Explain the constitutional and statutory provisions relating to acquisition and termination of Indian citizenship. 15 marks

1. Introduction

Citizenship is the legal status of membership of a State. Articles 5–11 in Part II of the Constitution deal with citizenship. Article 11 authorises Parliament to regulate citizenship by law. The Citizenship Act, 1955 contains the detailed rules.

2. Constitutional framework

Article 5 dealt with citizenship at commencement. Articles 6 and 7 address specified migration between India and Pakistan. Article 8 concerns persons of Indian origin living outside India. Article 9 concerns voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship. Article 10 provides for continuance subject to law. Article 11 empowers Parliament to make citizenship legislation.

3. Acquisition under the Citizenship Act, 1955

(a) Birth – Section 3: The applicable rule depends on the date of birth. For births from 26 January 1950 to before 1 July 1987, the earlier rule generally applies subject to statutory exceptions. For births from 1 July 1987 to before 3 December 2004, at least one parent must be an Indian citizen. For births on or after 3 December 2004, both parents must be Indian citizens or one must be an Indian citizen and the other must not be an illegal migrant, subject to statutory exceptions.

(b) Descent – Section 4: A person born outside India to an Indian parent may qualify, subject to the applicable statutory conditions and registration requirements where relevant.

(c) Registration – Section 5: The Act provides specified categories of persons who may apply for registration, subject to eligibility and residence or other requirements applicable to the category.

(d) Naturalisation – Section 6: A foreign national may apply after satisfying the qualifications prescribed in the Act and Third Schedule, including residence-related conditions, subject to the Central Government's statutory discretion.

(e) Incorporation of territory – Section 7: Where territory becomes part of India, the Central Government may specify the persons who become citizens.

4. Termination of citizenship

  1. Renunciation – Section 8: Voluntary declaration by a citizen, subject to statutory conditions.
  2. Termination – Section 9: Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship may result in termination, subject to the Act.
  3. Deprivation – Section 10: The Central Government may deprive a person of citizenship in specified circumstances and after following the required procedure.

5. Domicile and residence

Domicile generally means a permanent home and requires consideration of intention as well as residence. Residence is simply the fact of living in a place and may be temporary. Article 5 is a commencement provision; it is not the general present-day route for acquiring citizenship. A long stay or residential permit alone does not automatically prove Indian domicile or citizenship.

6. Conclusion

The Constitution provides the basic framework, while the Citizenship Act, 1955 regulates the detailed conditions for acquisition and termination. Each claim must be decided by applying the relevant statutory provision to the facts.

Part E – Problem question practice

A foreign national has lived in India for several years on a foreign passport and residential permit. The person claims Indian domicile and citizenship. Discuss. 10/15-mark problem

Issue

Whether long residence and a residential permit automatically establish Indian domicile or citizenship.

Rule

Domicile generally means a permanent home and involves residence together with intention to make that place a permanent home. Residence alone is not conclusive. Citizenship must be acquired under the Constitution and the Citizenship Act, 1955 through a recognised route and by satisfying its conditions.

Application

The person's long stay may establish residence. However, a foreign passport and residential permit do not by themselves establish Indian domicile or citizenship. The person's intention to make India a permanent home must be examined for domicile. Separately, the person must establish eligibility under an applicable statutory route, such as registration or naturalisation, if relevant.

Conclusion

On the facts stated alone, Indian domicile or citizenship cannot automatically be presumed. The intention and the statutory requirements must be examined.

Exam technique: Do not confuse domicile with citizenship. They are distinct legal concepts. Answer each part separately.
