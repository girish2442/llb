1. Unit V – Syllabus scope

  • Minority cultural and educational rights.
  • Constitutional remedies under Articles 32 and 226 and the writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
  • Right to property under Article 300A.
  • Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP).
  • Fundamental Duties.
  • Relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
Exam approach: Learn the article numbers, distinguish the powers of the Supreme Court and High Courts, memorise the five writs, and prepare the landmark cases on the relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSP.

2. Minority cultural and educational rights – Articles 29 and 30

Article 29 – Protection of interests of minorities

Article 29(1): Any section of citizens residing in India or any part thereof having a distinct language, script or culture has the right to conserve the same. This right is not confined only to religious or linguistic minorities; it is available to any section of citizens satisfying the constitutional description.

Article 29(2): No citizen shall be denied admission into any educational institution maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language.

Article 30 – Right of minorities to establish and administer educational institutions

Article 30(1): All minorities, whether based on religion or language, have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

Article 30(1A): Where property of a minority educational institution is compulsorily acquired, the amount fixed or determined must not restrict or abrogate the right guaranteed by Article 30(1).

Article 30(2): The State shall not discriminate against an educational institution in granting aid on the ground that it is under the management of a minority, whether based on religion or language.

Important principles

  • Minority status may be based on religion or language.
  • The right to administer does not mean a right to maladminister.
  • Reasonable regulations may be imposed to maintain educational standards, fairness, transparency and academic excellence, provided they do not destroy the minority character or substantially take away the protected right.
  • Article 29(2) and Article 30 must be read harmoniously.

Leading cases

State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) – Considered the prohibition on denial of admission on specified grounds under Article 29(2).

In re Kerala Education Bill (1958) – Explained aspects of minority educational rights and the State's power to regulate educational institutions.

T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002) – A Constitution Bench considered the scope of the rights of minorities and educational institutions under Articles 29 and 30.

P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra (2005) – Considered admissions and regulatory powers in unaided professional educational institutions, including minority institutions.

Remember: Article 29(1) protects language, script and culture. Article 29(2) protects citizens against admission denial on specified grounds. Article 30 protects minority rights to establish and administer educational institutions.

3. Constitutional remedies – Articles 32 and 226

Article 32 – Supreme Court

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court may issue directions, orders or writs, including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari, as appropriate.

  • Article 32 itself is a Fundamental Right.
  • It is primarily for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
  • The Supreme Court has power to issue constitutional writs for that purpose.
  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described Article 32 as the “heart and soul” of the Constitution.

Article 226 – High Courts

Every High Court has power to issue directions, orders or writs, including the five traditional writs, for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and “for any other purpose”. This gives Article 226 a wider subject-matter reach than Article 32.

  • Article 226 is a constitutional power of the High Courts, but the right to approach a High Court under it is not itself a Fundamental Right.
  • It may be used to enforce Fundamental Rights and other legal rights.
  • The High Court's territorial and jurisdictional requirements must be satisfied.
  • High Courts generally exercise writ jurisdiction with recognised principles of discretion, including the availability of an effective alternative remedy, subject to exceptions.

Article 32 and Article 226 – Comparison

Article 32Article 226
Supreme Court.High Courts.
Enforcement of Fundamental Rights.Enforcement of Fundamental Rights and other legal rights.
It is itself a Fundamental Right.It is a constitutional power, not itself a Fundamental Right.
Jurisdiction is national in scope, subject to constitutional requirements.Territorial and cause-of-action requirements apply.

L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997) – Judicial review under Articles 32 and 226/227 forms part of the basic structure of the Constitution.

4. The five constitutional writs

WritMeaning and purposeSimple example
Habeas Corpus“To have the body.” Used to challenge unlawful detention and secure production of the detained person before the court.A person is detained without legal authority; a petition seeks release.
Mandamus“We command.” Directs a public authority to perform a public or statutory duty that it has failed or refused to perform.An authority refuses to consider an application it is legally required to decide.
ProhibitionIssued by a higher court to a lower court or tribunal to prevent it from continuing proceedings beyond its jurisdiction.A tribunal begins hearing a matter that it has no legal power to decide.
CertiorariIssued to quash an order or decision of a lower court, tribunal or authority where recognised grounds such as jurisdictional error or legal error exist.A tribunal passes an order without jurisdiction; the order is challenged and quashed.
Quo Warranto“By what authority.” Challenges a person's legal authority to hold a public office.A person occupies a public office without satisfying mandatory legal qualifications.
Quick distinction: Prohibition generally prevents an excess-of-jurisdiction proceeding before it is completed; certiorari generally quashes an order already made. The precise availability of each writ depends on the nature of the authority and legal error.

T.C. Basappa v. T. Nagappa (1954) – Discussed the nature and scope of certiorari.

Hari Vishnu Kamath v. Ahmad Ishaque (1955) – Explained the scope of certiorari and review of decisions of tribunals.

5. Right to property – Article 300A

Article 300A provides: “No person shall be deprived of his property save by authority of law.” It protects property as a constitutional right, but it is no longer a Fundamental Right.

Historical background

  • Originally, the right to property was protected as a Fundamental Right under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31.
  • The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 removed Article 19(1)(f) and omitted Article 31.
  • Article 300A was inserted in Part XII of the Constitution.

Meaning and safeguards

  • A person can be deprived of property only under authority of law.
  • Executive action alone, without legal authority, cannot deprive a person of property.
  • The law and action must comply with applicable constitutional requirements.
  • Article 300A applies to every person, not only citizens.

Leading cases

K.T. Plantation Pvt. Ltd. v. State of Karnataka (2011) – The Court considered Article 300A and held that deprivation of property must be under authority of law; the law is subject to constitutional scrutiny.

Kolkata Municipal Corporation v. Bimal Kumar Shah (2024) – The Supreme Court explained important procedural protections associated with deprivation of property under Article 300A, including notice, hearing, reasoned decision, public purpose, fair procedure and completion of the acquisition process, in the context of the case.

Exam correction: Do not write that the right to property is still a Fundamental Right. It is a constitutional right under Article 300A. Article 32 is generally not available merely for enforcement of Article 300A as though it were a Fundamental Right; Article 226 may be available where its requirements are met.

6. Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP)

Directive Principles are guidelines given to the State for governance and law-making. They are contained in Part IV, Articles 36 to 51, of the Constitution.

Article 37 – Nature of DPSP

The Directive Principles are not enforceable by any court, but they are nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country. It is the duty of the State to apply these principles in making laws.

Objectives

  • Establish social and economic justice.
  • Reduce inequalities in income, status, facilities and opportunities.
  • Promote welfare of the people.
  • Protect workers, children and vulnerable groups.
  • Promote public health, education, environmental protection and international peace.

Classification for study

The Constitution does not formally divide DPSP into categories. For study purposes, they are commonly grouped as follows:

CategoryImportant Articles / examples
Socialist principlesArticle 38 – social order and welfare; Article 39 – livelihood, distribution of resources, equal pay and protection of workers and children; Article 39A – equal justice and free legal aid; Article 41 – right to work, education and public assistance within the State's capacity; Article 42 – humane work conditions and maternity relief; Article 43 – living wage; Article 43A – workers' participation in management; Article 47 – nutrition, standard of living and public health.
Gandhian principlesArticle 40 – village panchayats; Article 43 – cottage industries; Article 46 – educational and economic interests of weaker sections, especially SCs/STs; Article 47 – public health and prohibition of intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health; Article 48 – agriculture and animal husbandry.
Liberal / intellectual principlesArticle 44 – uniform civil code; Article 45 – early childhood care and education for children below six years; Article 48A – environment, forests and wildlife; Article 49 – protection of monuments; Article 50 – separation of judiciary from executive; Article 51 – international peace and security.

Important individual provisions

  • Article 38: Promote welfare of the people and minimise inequalities.
  • Article 39: Adequate means of livelihood, distribution of material resources for the common good, prevention of concentration of wealth, equal pay for equal work and protection of workers and children.
  • Article 39A: Equal justice and free legal aid.
  • Article 40: Organisation of village panchayats.
  • Article 44: Uniform Civil Code.
  • Article 45: Early childhood care and education for children below six years.
  • Article 48A: Protection and improvement of environment and safeguarding forests and wildlife.
  • Article 50: Separation of judiciary from executive.
  • Article 51: Promotion of international peace and security.
Remember: DPSP are non-justiciable, but Article 37 declares them fundamental in governance. They guide the State in creating a welfare society.

7. Fundamental Duties – Article 51A

Fundamental Duties are moral and civic obligations of citizens. They are listed in Part IVA, Article 51A. They were introduced by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976, following the recommendations of the Swaran Singh Committee. The 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002 added the duty relating to educational opportunities for children.

The eleven Fundamental Duties

  1. To abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem.
  2. To cherish and follow the noble ideals that inspired the national struggle for freedom.
  3. To uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.
  4. To defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so.
  5. To promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood, transcending religious, linguistic, regional or sectional diversities, and to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
  6. To value and preserve the rich heritage of India's composite culture.
  7. To protect and improve the natural environment, including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures.
  8. To develop scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform.
  9. To safeguard public property and abjure violence.
  10. To strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity.
  11. As a parent or guardian, to provide opportunities for education to a child or ward between six and fourteen years of age.

Nature and enforceability

  • Fundamental Duties are generally non-justiciable by themselves; a person cannot ordinarily be punished merely because a duty is stated in Article 51A.
  • Parliament may enact laws to give effect to particular duties, and existing laws may support their observance.
  • Courts may refer to Fundamental Duties when interpreting laws and constitutional obligations.

AIIMS Students' Union v. AIIMS (2001) – The Supreme Court observed the significance of Fundamental Duties as constitutional responsibilities, while recognising their non-enforceable character by themselves.

8. Relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSP

Fundamental Rights (Part III) protect individual freedoms and are generally enforceable by courts. DPSP (Part IV) guide the State in promoting social and economic welfare and are not directly enforceable by courts. Both are important and are intended to work together.

Constitutional development through cases

State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) – The Court held that in case of conflict, Fundamental Rights would prevail over DPSP as the law then stood. This decision led to constitutional amendments and later developments.

Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967) – The Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights, a position later modified by the Kesavananda Bharati decision.

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) – Parliament may amend the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot destroy its basic structure. The Court also considered the relationship between Parts III and IV.

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) – The harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSP is part of the basic structure. Giving absolute primacy to one at the cost of destroying the other would damage the constitutional scheme.

Present constitutional understanding

  • Fundamental Rights and DPSP should be interpreted harmoniously wherever possible.
  • Neither Part III nor Part IV should be read as wholly unimportant.
  • Parliament may enact laws to implement DPSP, but such laws remain subject to applicable constitutional limitations and judicial review.
  • Article 31C gives limited protection to laws genuinely giving effect to the principles in Article 39(b) and (c), subject to the law as interpreted by the Supreme Court. The wider extension attempted by the 42nd Amendment was invalidated in Minerva Mills.
Exam caution: Do not state that all laws implementing any DPSP automatically override Fundamental Rights. The current protection under Article 31C is limited, particularly to Article 39(b) and (c), as explained by the Supreme Court.

10. Last-minute revision sheet

  • Article 29(1): Conserve distinct language, script or culture.
  • Article 29(2): No denial of admission on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language in specified institutions.
  • Article 30: Religious and linguistic minorities may establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.
  • Article 32: Supreme Court remedy for enforcement of Fundamental Rights; itself a Fundamental Right.
  • Article 226: High Court writs for Fundamental Rights and other legal purposes.
  • Five writs: Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari and Quo Warranto.
  • Article 300A: No person deprived of property except by authority of law; constitutional, not Fundamental Right.
  • DPSP: Part IV, Articles 36–51; non-justiciable but fundamental in governance under Article 37.
  • Fundamental Duties: Part IVA, Article 51A; eleven duties.
  • Minerva Mills: Harmony and balance between Parts III and IV is basic structure.
Quick memory: Article 32 = Supreme Court; Article 226 = High Court; Article 300A = property; Article 37 = DPSP; Article 51A = Fundamental Duties.
