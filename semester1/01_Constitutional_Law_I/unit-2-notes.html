2. Article 12 – Meaning of “State”

Article 12 gives the meaning of the word “State” for Part III of the Constitution, which contains Fundamental Rights. It is important because Fundamental Rights are generally enforceable against the State and its authorities.

Constitutional wording – components

For Part III, “State” includes:

  1. The Government and Parliament of India.
  2. The Government and Legislature of each State.
  3. All local authorities.
  4. Other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.

What are local authorities?

Local authorities are bodies that administer local areas or provide local civic services. Examples may include municipalities, municipal corporations, panchayats and certain statutory local bodies.

Meaning of “other authorities”

The expression is wider than government departments. It can include statutory corporations, government companies and other bodies that are financially, functionally and administratively dominated by the Government, depending on the facts.

Exam point: Do not say that every government-aided or regulated private organisation automatically becomes “State”. The court examines the nature and degree of government control and the overall relationship.

3. Judicial tests for determining “State”

The Supreme Court has developed indicators to decide whether a body is an instrumentality or agency of government. No single factor is always conclusive; the court looks at the cumulative effect.

IndicatorWhat the court examines
Government ownershipWhether the entire or substantial share capital is held by government.
Financial assistanceWhether the body receives substantial financial support from the State.
State controlWhether control is deep, pervasive and administrative, not merely regulatory.
Public functionWhether the body performs a function of public importance closely related to governmental functions.
MonopolyWhether the State has conferred or protected a monopoly.
Government department transferWhether a government department or activity has been transferred to the body.

Leading cases

Rajasthan Electricity Board v. Mohan Lal (1967) – The expression “other authorities” was interpreted broadly; a statutory electricity board could fall within Article 12.

R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority of India (1979) – The Court identified important indicators for deciding whether a body is an instrumentality or agency of the State.

Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981) – A society registered under a law can be “State” if it is in substance an instrumentality or agency of government. The listed indicators are not rigid or exhaustive.

Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology (2002) – The controlling question is whether the body is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by, or under the control of, government; the control must be particular and pervasive.

Zee Telefilms Ltd. v. Union of India (2005) – BCCI was held not to be “State” under Article 12 on the facts, though its public functions may attract other forms of public-law review in appropriate cases.

Distinction: “State” under Article 12 is not the same as “State” in every other constitutional context. Article 12 specifically defines it for Part III.

4. Article 13 – Laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights

Article 13 protects Fundamental Rights by declaring that laws inconsistent with or in derogation of those rights are void to the extent of the inconsistency.

Article 13(1): Pre-Constitution laws

Pre-Constitution laws are laws that existed before 26 January 1950. They continue to operate after the Constitution came into force, but become void to the extent that they conflict with Fundamental Rights.

Article 13(2): Post-Constitution laws

The State shall not make any law that takes away or abridges Fundamental Rights. A law made in violation of this prohibition is void to the extent of the contravention.

Meaning of “law” – Article 13(3)

“Law” includes an ordinance, order, bye-law, rule, regulation, notification, custom or usage having the force of law. “Laws in force” includes laws passed or made by a legislature or other competent authority before the Constitution and not previously repealed, even if they were not then in operation everywhere.

Constitutional amendments – Article 13(4)

Article 13 does not apply to constitutional amendments made under Article 368, as stated in Article 13(4). However, the power to amend the Constitution is limited by the Basic Structure doctrine.

Remember: Article 13(1) deals with pre-Constitution laws; Article 13(2) deals with post-Constitution laws. The invalidity is generally “to the extent” of the inconsistency, not automatically the whole enactment.

5. Doctrine of Severability

The doctrine of severability is also called the doctrine of separability. If only a part of a law violates Fundamental Rights, the unconstitutional part may be separated and struck down while the valid remainder continues, provided the remainder can operate independently and reflects the legislative intention.

Key principles

  • The valid and invalid portions should be separable.
  • The remaining part must be workable and meaningful on its own.
  • The court considers whether the legislature would have enacted the valid part independently.
  • If the valid and invalid portions are inseparable, the whole provision or Act may fail.

R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala v. Union of India (1957) – The Supreme Court explained the principles governing severability, including whether the valid portion can stand independently.

A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950) – An early illustration of examining the validity of statutory provisions in light of Fundamental Rights and the separability of offending provisions.

Simple example

Suppose an Act contains five independent sections. One section violates Article 14, while the other four can function independently. The court may strike down the offending section and preserve the remaining valid provisions.

6. Doctrine of Eclipse

The doctrine of eclipse mainly applies to pre-Constitution laws that are inconsistent with Fundamental Rights. Such a law is not treated as completely erased; it becomes unenforceable or dormant to the extent of the inconsistency. If the constitutional obstacle is later removed, the law may revive to the extent legally permissible.

Key points

  • It is principally associated with pre-Constitution laws under Article 13(1).
  • The inconsistent law is eclipsed by the Fundamental Right.
  • It remains on the statute book but cannot be enforced to the extent of the inconsistency.
  • Removal of the inconsistency may allow the law to operate again, subject to the law and later constitutional developments.

Bhikaji Narain Dhakras v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1955) – A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right was treated as eclipsed; after the constitutional position changed, it could become enforceable.

State of Gujarat v. Ambica Mills (1974) – The Court discussed the operation of Article 13 and the doctrine of eclipse, including its application in relation to persons who could not claim the relevant Fundamental Right.

Memory aid: Eclipse = dormant, not necessarily dead. Severability = separate the invalid part from the valid part.

7. Article 14 – Right to Equality

Article 14 states: “The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India.” It applies to every person, not only citizens.

Two expressions

Equality before lawEqual protection of laws
A negative concept derived from the rule of law. No person is above the law; all are subject to ordinary law and courts, subject to constitutionally recognised exceptions.A positive concept. Persons in similar circumstances should be treated alike, and different treatment may be given to persons who are genuinely differently situated.

Reasonable classification test

Article 14 does not prohibit all classification. It prohibits class legislation and permits reasonable classification. A classification must satisfy both tests:

  1. Intelligible differentia: The classification must be based on a clear and understandable distinction separating the included group from others.
  2. Rational nexus: That distinction must have a reasonable connection with the object the law seeks to achieve.

If either test fails, the classification may violate Article 14.

Important judicial interpretations

State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952) – Arbitrary selection for special procedure without a proper guiding principle was held to violate equality.

Ram Krishna Dalmia v. Justice S.R. Tendolkar (1958) – The Court explained the principles and presumptions governing reasonable classification.

E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974) – Equality is opposed to arbitrariness; arbitrary State action may violate Article 14.

Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) – State action and procedure must be fair, just and non-arbitrary; Articles 14, 19 and 21 are read together where applicable.

D.S. Nakara v. Union of India (1983) – An unreasonable cut-off date dividing pensioners into two groups was held discriminatory on the facts.

Modern approach: arbitrariness

Article 14 is not confined to classification. It also guards against arbitrary, irrational or unfair State action. A law or State action may be challenged where it is manifestly arbitrary, depending on the legal context and applicable doctrine.

10. Last-minute revision sheet

  • Article 12: Government + legislatures + local authorities + other authorities.
  • Article 13(1): Pre-Constitution laws – void to the extent of inconsistency.
  • Article 13(2): State cannot make laws taking away or abridging Fundamental Rights.
  • Article 13(3): Broad meaning of “law”, including rules, regulations, orders, customs and usages with force of law.
  • Severability: Separate the invalid part if the remainder can function independently.
  • Eclipse: Inconsistent pre-Constitution law becomes dormant to the extent of conflict.
  • Article 14: Equality before law + equal protection of laws.
  • Classification: Intelligible differentia + rational nexus.
  • Arbitrariness: Also prohibited by Article 14.
