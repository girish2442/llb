1. Unit III – Syllabus scope

  • Articles 15–17: prohibition of discrimination, equality of opportunity in public employment, protective discrimination and abolition of untouchability.
  • Article 19: six freedoms guaranteed to citizens and the reasonable restrictions that may be imposed on them.
Exam approach: For Articles 15–17, identify the protected ground, the constitutional prohibition and the permitted special provisions. For Article 19, always connect each freedom with its corresponding restriction clause.

2. Article 15 – Prohibition of discrimination

Article 15 prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.

Article 15(1)

The State shall not discriminate against any citizen only on the listed grounds. The word “only” is important: a classification based solely on a prohibited ground is constitutionally objectionable.

Article 15(2)

No citizen shall, on the listed grounds only, be subject to a disability, liability, restriction or condition regarding access to shops, public restaurants, hotels and places of public entertainment, or the use of wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort maintained wholly or partly out of State funds or dedicated to the use of the general public.

Article 15(3)

Nothing in Article 15 prevents the State from making special provisions for women and children. This enables affirmative measures designed to address disadvantage and promote their welfare.

Article 15(4)

The State may make special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes of citizens and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

Article 15(5)

The State may make special provisions, by law, for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes and SCs/STs in relation to admission to educational institutions, including private educational institutions, whether aided or unaided, subject to the constitutional exception for minority educational institutions referred to in Article 30(1).

Article 15(6)

Special provisions for the advancement of economically weaker sections (EWS) may be made, including in admissions to educational institutions, subject to the terms of the clause. The provision permits reservation up to 10% in addition to existing reservations, as provided by the Constitution.

Remember: Article 15(1) and 15(2) prohibit specified discrimination. Clauses (3) to (6) enable constitutionally permitted special provisions.

3. Protective discrimination and social justice

Protective discrimination, also called affirmative action or positive discrimination, refers to special measures taken to improve the position of groups that have experienced social and educational disadvantage. The purpose is to achieve substantive equality rather than merely identical treatment.

Constitutional basis

  • Article 15(3): special provisions for women and children.
  • Article 15(4): advancement of socially and educationally backward classes and SCs/STs.
  • Article 15(5): special provisions relating to admission to educational institutions, subject to the minority-institution exception.
  • Article 15(6): special provisions for economically weaker sections, subject to its terms.
  • Article 16(4), 16(4A), 16(4B) and 16(6): specified reservation provisions in public employment.

Important principles

  • Reservation is a constitutional method of addressing historical and structural disadvantage.
  • Protective measures must operate within the relevant constitutional clause and judicial interpretation.
  • For backward-class reservation, the creamy-layer principle has an important role in the applicable legal framework.
  • Reservation provisions are enabling provisions; their application depends on the constitutional text, legislation, government policy and judicial review.

State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951) – The communal allocation of seats in educational institutions was held invalid under the then constitutional provisions. The First Constitutional Amendment introduced Article 15(4).

Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) – The Supreme Court considered reservation for Other Backward Classes in public employment, the creamy-layer principle and the general 50% ceiling, subject to the judgment's scope and later constitutional developments.

Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) – The Supreme Court upheld the 103rd Constitutional Amendment providing EWS reservation.

Do not oversimplify: The 50% ceiling and its exceptions have a detailed case-law context. Do not state it as an absolute rule without explaining the relevant constitutional and judicial position.

4. Article 16 – Equality of opportunity in public employment

Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity for all citizens in matters relating to employment or appointment to an office under the State.

Important clauses

ClausePrinciple
16(1)Equality of opportunity for all citizens in public employment.
16(2)No discrimination in public employment on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth, residence or any of them.
16(3)Parliament may prescribe residence requirements for certain classes of employment under a State or Union Territory, within the constitutional framework.
16(4)Reservation of appointments or posts for a backward class of citizens that, in the opinion of the State, is not adequately represented in State services.
16(4A)Enables reservation in promotion, with consequential seniority, for SCs/STs under the conditions stated in the clause.
16(4B)Allows unfilled reserved vacancies to be treated as a separate class for the purpose of carry-forward, as stated in the clause.
16(5)Allows a law requiring the holder of an office connected with a religious or denominational institution to belong to a particular religion or denomination.
16(6)Enables reservation of appointments or posts for EWS, subject to the clause, up to 10% in addition to existing reservations.

Key judicial principles

Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (1992) – Important decision on OBC reservation, creamy layer, the general ceiling on reservation and the distinction between reservation in initial appointments and promotion under the law as then considered.

M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006) – Considered the constitutional validity of amendments enabling reservation in promotion for SCs/STs and the conditions governing their implementation.

Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018) – Clarified aspects of the requirements considered in reservation in promotion, including the application of the creamy-layer principle.

Exam distinction: Article 15 primarily concerns discrimination in specified fields and special provisions including education. Article 16 specifically concerns equality of opportunity in public employment.

5. Article 17 – Abolition of untouchability

Article 17 abolishes “untouchability” and forbids its practice in any form. The enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability is an offence punishable in accordance with law.

Purpose and scope

  • It seeks to remove caste-based exclusion and social disabilities associated with untouchability.
  • It is an absolute constitutional prohibition; it is not subject to a list of reasonable restrictions like Article 19.
  • It is enforceable against private individuals as well as public authorities through laws enacted to give effect to it.

Important legislation

  • Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955: Provides punishment for the practice of untouchability and enforcement of disabilities arising from it.
  • Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989: Provides penal and protective measures against specified atrocities and offences against members of SCs and STs.
Exam point: Article 17 is not limited to discrimination by the State. It also addresses social practices and private conduct through constitutional prohibition and implementing legislation.

6. Article 19 – Six freedoms

Article 19(1) guarantees six freedoms to citizens. These rights are not absolute: the Constitution permits reasonable restrictions under clauses (2) to (6), depending on the freedom involved.

FreedomClauseRestriction clause
Freedom of speech and expression19(1)(a)19(2)
Peaceful assembly without arms19(1)(b)19(3)
Form associations, unions or co-operative societies19(1)(c)19(4)
Move freely throughout India19(1)(d)19(5)
Reside and settle in any part of India19(1)(e)19(5)
Practise any profession, or carry on any occupation, trade or business19(1)(g)19(6)
Important correction: Article 19(1)(f), the former right to acquire, hold and dispose of property, was omitted by the 44th Constitutional Amendment. Property is now protected under Article 300A, not as a Fundamental Right under Article 19.

Article 19(1)(a) – Speech and expression

Includes the freedom to express opinions through words, writing, printing, art and other forms of communication. The Supreme Court has recognised aspects such as freedom of the press and the right to receive information as part of the constitutional protection.

Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950) – Early decision emphasising the importance of freedom of speech and expression.

Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India (1973) – Recognised the protection of newspaper circulation and press freedom under Article 19(1)(a).

Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) – Section 66A of the Information Technology Act was struck down for violating freedom of speech and expression; the Court discussed the distinction between discussion, advocacy and incitement.

Article 19(1)(b) – Peaceful assembly

Citizens have the right to assemble peaceably and without arms. The right does not include a right to assemble violently or with weapons. Restrictions may be imposed in the interests listed in Article 19(3), including sovereignty and integrity of India and public order.

Article 19(1)(c) – Associations, unions and co-operative societies

Citizens may form associations, unions and co-operative societies. The right includes the freedom to form an association, but does not automatically guarantee every objective or demand of the association. Article 19(4) permits restrictions in the interests of sovereignty and integrity of India, public order or morality.

Article 19(1)(d) and (e) – Movement and residence

Citizens may move freely throughout India and reside and settle in any part of India. Reasonable restrictions may be imposed under Article 19(5) in the interests of the general public or for the protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.

Article 19(1)(g) – Profession, occupation, trade or business

Citizens may practise any profession or carry on any occupation, trade or business. Article 19(6) permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of the general public, professional or technical qualifications, and State monopoly in specified trades, businesses, industries or services.

7. Reasonable restrictions under Article 19

The Constitution does not permit the State to restrict all freedoms on any ground it chooses. Each freedom has its own restriction clause and specified grounds.

FreedomPermitted grounds under the Constitution
Speech – 19(2)Sovereignty and integrity of India; security of the State; friendly relations with foreign States; public order; decency or morality; contempt of court; defamation; incitement to an offence.
Assembly – 19(3)Sovereignty and integrity of India; public order.
Association – 19(4)Sovereignty and integrity of India; public order; morality.
Movement and residence – 19(5)Interests of the general public; protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.
Profession / trade – 19(6)Interests of the general public; prescribed professional or technical qualifications; State monopoly in specified activities.

What makes a restriction reasonable?

  • There must be legal authority for the restriction.
  • The restriction must fall within the grounds listed in the relevant clause.
  • The measure must have a reasonable relationship with the permitted objective.
  • The court examines the nature of the right, the extent of the restriction, the public interest and the circumstances.

Chintaman Rao v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1950) – A restriction that is arbitrary or excessive cannot be treated as reasonable.

State of Madras v. V.G. Row (1952) – The reasonableness of a restriction is assessed by considering the nature of the right, purpose of the restriction, extent and urgency of the evil, and other relevant factors.

Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) – A restriction on speech must fit within Article 19(2); vague and overbroad restrictions are constitutionally problematic.

Answer-writing tip: Never write that “public interest” is a ground for restricting every Article 19 freedom. Match the facts to the exact restriction clause for the relevant freedom.

9. Last-minute revision sheet

  • Article 15(1): No discrimination against citizens on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.
  • Article 15(2): Access to specified public places and facilities.
  • Article 15(3): Special provisions for women and children.
  • Article 15(4) and (5): Advancement of socially and educationally backward classes and SCs/STs; education-related provisions.
  • Article 15(6): EWS special provisions, subject to the clause.
  • Article 16: Equality of opportunity in public employment; reservation clauses are enabling provisions.
  • Article 17: Untouchability abolished; practice forbidden in any form.
  • Article 19: Six freedoms for citizens, subject to specified restrictions.
  • 19(1)(a) → 19(2); (b) → (3); (c) → (4); (d)/(e) → (5); (g) → (6).
  • Reasonableness: Restriction must be within the relevant clause and not arbitrary or excessive.
Accuracy reminder: Article 19 rights are available to citizens. Article 15(1) and 16 are also citizen-focused, while Article 14 protects every person. Check the precise wording of each clause before the exam.
