1. Meaning, definition and kinds of Constitution

Meaning

A Constitution is the fundamental or supreme law of a country. It establishes the organs of government, distributes their powers, explains how those powers are exercised and places limits on public authority. It also protects rights and expresses the basic values of the State.

Why is a Constitution necessary?

  • Creates and defines the organs of government.
  • Distributes legislative, executive and judicial powers.
  • Limits arbitrary government action and protects liberty.
  • Provides stability, continuity and procedures for change.
  • States the values and goals of the country.

Nature of the Indian Constitution

  • Written and detailed Constitution.
  • Combination of rigid and flexible amendment procedures.
  • Federal structure with a comparatively strong Union.
  • Parliamentary form of government.
  • Independent judiciary and judicial review.
Exam caution: Do not say the UK has no written laws. Write “uncodified Constitution”.

Conclusion: A Constitution is the basic legal framework that organises government, limits power and protects the constitutional order.

2. Constitutionalism

Constitutionalism is the principle that government must function under the Constitution and that its powers must be limited by law. A country may have a written Constitution, but constitutionalism requires public authorities to respect constitutional limits in practice.

Main principles

  1. Limited government: Government cannot exercise unlimited or arbitrary power.
  2. Rule of law: Everyone, including public authorities, is subject to law.
  3. Separation of powers: Legislative, executive and judicial functions are distributed, with checks and balances.
  4. Fundamental Rights: Individual freedoms receive constitutional protection.
  5. Independent judiciary: Courts decide disputes independently and protect the Constitution.
  6. Judicial review: Courts examine whether laws and government actions comply with the Constitution.
  7. Accountability: Government must answer for the exercise of public power.
  8. Free and fair elections: Democratic authority is based on genuine electoral choice.

Cases: Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) – basic structure doctrine; Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) – limited amending power and constitutional balance.

3. Salient features of the Indian Constitution

  1. Lengthy written Constitution: Detailed provisions cover the Union, States, rights, institutions and administration.
  2. Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic Republic: These words describe the constitutional character of India.
  3. Federal system with a strong Centre: Powers are divided between Union and States, but the Union has significant powers.
  4. Parliamentary government: The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha at Union level.
  5. Fundamental Rights: Part III protects important individual rights, subject to constitutional limitations.
  6. Directive Principles: Part IV guides the State towards social and economic welfare.
  7. Fundamental Duties: Part IVA sets out duties of citizens.
  8. Independent and integrated judiciary: Courts protect the Constitution and decide disputes.
  9. Single citizenship: Citizenship is for India as a whole.
  10. Universal adult franchise: Adult citizens may vote subject to constitutional and statutory qualifications.
  11. Rigidity and flexibility: Article 368 provides a special amendment procedure, while some changes follow other procedures.
  12. Emergency provisions: Special constitutional arrangements apply in exceptional circumstances.

Cases to connect

  • Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973) – basic structure doctrine.
  • S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994) – federalism and secularism as basic features in the relevant constitutional context.
  • Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980) – limited amending power and harmony between Fundamental Rights and DPSP.

4. The Preamble

Meaning and importance

The Preamble is the introductory statement to the Constitution. It identifies the source of constitutional authority, describes the nature of the Indian State and states the objectives the Constitution seeks to achieve.

Opening words: “We, the People of India” – constitutional authority is derived from the people.

Words describing the State

  • Sovereign: India is independent and not subject to external control.
  • Socialist: The State aims at social and economic justice and reducing inequalities; it does not mean that all private enterprise is abolished.
  • Secular: The State has no official religion and must treat religions according to constitutional principles.
  • Democratic: Government derives authority from the people through representative institutions and elections.
  • Republic: The head of State is not a hereditary monarch.

Objectives

ObjectiveMeaning
JusticeSocial, economic and political justice.
LibertyLiberty of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship.
EqualityEquality of status and opportunity.
FraternityDignity of the individual and unity and integrity of the nation.

Important history and case law

  • 26 November 1949: Constitution adopted.
  • 26 January 1950: Constitution came into force.
  • 42nd Amendment Act, 1976: Added “Socialist”, “Secular” and “Integrity”.

In In re Berubari Union (1960), the Supreme Court expressed the view that the Preamble was not part of the Constitution. In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the Court held that the Preamble is part of the Constitution. It can be amended under Article 368, but an amendment cannot damage the basic structure.

Common exam error: Do not state that the Preamble is not part of the Constitution. Explain the development from Berubari (1960) to Kesavananda Bharati (1973).

5. Citizenship: acquisition and termination

Constitutional provisions

Articles 5–11 in Part II deal with citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution and empower Parliament to make citizenship laws. The Citizenship Act, 1955 is the principal legislation governing acquisition and termination.

ArticleBroad subject
5Citizenship at commencement of the Constitution.
6Citizenship of certain persons who migrated from Pakistan to India.
7Persons who migrated to Pakistan but later returned to India, subject to the provision.
8Persons of Indian origin residing outside India.
9Voluntary acquisition of citizenship of a foreign State.
10Continuance of citizenship rights, subject to laws made by Parliament.
11Power of Parliament to regulate citizenship by law.

Acquisition under the Citizenship Act, 1955

  1. Birth – Section 3: The applicable rule depends on date of birth. Broadly: 26 January 1950 to before 1 July 1987 – citizenship by birth, subject to statutory exceptions; 1 July 1987 to before 3 December 2004 – at least one parent must be an Indian citizen; on or after 3 December 2004 – both parents must be Indian citizens, or one must be an Indian citizen and the other must not be an illegal migrant, subject to statutory exceptions.
  2. Descent – Section 4: A person born outside India to an Indian parent may qualify, subject to statutory conditions, including registration where applicable.
  3. Registration – Section 5: Specified categories may apply if statutory eligibility conditions are met.
  4. Naturalisation – Section 6: A foreign national may apply if statutory qualifications, including residence and other Third Schedule conditions, are met. The Central Government has statutory discretion.
  5. Incorporation of territory – Section 7: If a territory becomes part of India, the Central Government may specify the persons who become citizens.

Termination

  1. Renunciation – Section 8: A citizen may make a declaration renouncing citizenship, subject to the Act.
  2. Termination – Section 9: Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship may result in termination of Indian citizenship, subject to the Act.
  3. Deprivation – Section 10: The Central Government may deprive a person of citizenship in specified statutory circumstances and by following the required procedure.

Domicile and residence

Domicile generally means a person's permanent home together with the intention to make it a permanent home. Residence is the fact of living in a place and may be temporary. A long stay, foreign passport or residential permit does not automatically establish Indian domicile or citizenship. Article 5 dealt with citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution; it is not the general current route for acquiring citizenship.

Problem-question tip: If a foreign national has lived in India for several years on a foreign passport or residential permit, do not automatically conclude that the person became an Indian citizen. Discuss domicile and intention separately from the statutory citizenship routes.

6. Exam-focused questions and answers

1. Define Constitution. Explain the different kinds of Constitution. Long answer

Introduction: A Constitution is the supreme legal framework of a country. It creates government institutions, distributes their powers and limits their authority.

Kinds: written and unwritten/uncodified; rigid and flexible; federal and unitary; parliamentary and presidential.

Indian position: India has a written and detailed Constitution, a combination of rigidity and flexibility, a federal structure with a strong Centre and a parliamentary system.

Conclusion: Constitutions differ in form, but their central purpose is to organise and limit public power.

2. Explain the meaning and importance of Constitutionalism. Long answer

Constitutionalism is the principle of limited government. Every organ of the State must act within constitutional limits. Its elements include rule of law, separation of powers, Fundamental Rights, independent judiciary, judicial review, accountability and democratic elections. A written Constitution alone does not guarantee constitutionalism; constitutional limits must be respected in practice. Kesavananda Bharati and Minerva Mills are important cases on limits to constitutional power.

3. Distinguish between Constitution and Constitutionalism. Short answer

A Constitution is the basic legal framework of the State. Constitutionalism is the principle that government must be limited and must exercise power according to that framework. A Constitution may exist in form, but constitutionalism requires effective respect for limits, rights and accountability.

4. Explain the salient features of the Indian Constitution. Long answer

Write under separate headings: written and lengthy Constitution; sovereign, socialist, secular, democratic republic; federal system with strong Centre; parliamentary government; Fundamental Rights; DPSP; Fundamental Duties; independent judiciary; single citizenship; universal adult franchise; combination of rigidity and flexibility; and emergency provisions. Conclude that the Constitution combines democratic government, individual rights and social welfare goals.

5. Explain the significance and objectives of the Preamble. Long answer

The Preamble is the introductory statement. It identifies the people as the source of authority, describes India as Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic and Republic, and sets out Justice, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity. It guides constitutional interpretation but is not an independent source of power. Mention the 42nd Amendment, 1976, and Berubari and Kesavananda Bharati.

6. Is the Preamble part of the Constitution? Can it be amended? Long answer

In In re Berubari Union (1960), the Court expressed the view that the Preamble was not part of the Constitution. In Kesavananda Bharati (1973), the Court held that it is part of the Constitution. It can be amended under Article 368, but Parliament cannot damage or destroy the basic structure. The 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 is an example.

7. Explain Sovereign, Socialist, Secular, Democratic and Republic. Short answer

Sovereign: independent authority. Socialist: commitment to social and economic justice and reducing inequality. Secular: no State religion and constitutional treatment of religions. Democratic: government based on the will of the people. Republic: elected, non-hereditary head of State.

8. Explain Justice, Liberty, Equality and Fraternity in the Preamble. Short answer

Justice includes social, economic and political justice. Liberty includes thought, expression, belief, faith and worship. Equality means equality of status and opportunity. Fraternity promotes dignity of the individual and unity and integrity of the nation.

9. Explain the constitutional provisions relating to citizenship. Long answer

Part II, Articles 5–11, deals with citizenship. Article 5 dealt with citizenship at commencement; Articles 6–8 addressed specified migration and persons of Indian origin abroad; Article 9 concerns voluntary foreign citizenship; Article 10 provides continuance subject to law; Article 11 empowers Parliament to regulate citizenship. Parliament enacted the Citizenship Act, 1955.

10. Explain the modes of acquisition of citizenship under the Citizenship Act, 1955. Long answer

The Act provides acquisition by birth (Section 3), descent (Section 4), registration (Section 5), naturalisation (Section 6) and incorporation of territory (Section 7). Conditions differ by mode. For citizenship by birth, the date of birth is important because statutory rules changed in 1987 and 2004. Registration and naturalisation have detailed statutory qualifications.

11. Explain the modes of termination of citizenship. Short answer

Citizenship may end by renunciation (Section 8), termination on voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship (Section 9), or deprivation by the Central Government in specified circumstances (Section 10), subject to the Act and applicable procedure.

12. Distinguish between domicile and residence. Short answer

Residence is the place where a person actually lives and may be temporary. Domicile generally means a person's permanent home and involves residence plus intention to make that place a permanent home. A long stay alone does not automatically establish domicile.

13. “India is federal in form but has a strong Centre.” Explain. Short answer

The Constitution divides powers between Union and States and provides an independent judiciary to resolve disputes. However, the Union has significant powers, including residuary legislative power and special powers during emergencies. India is therefore commonly described as federal with a strong Centre. Refer to State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963).

14. What is the importance of the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 in relation to the Preamble? Short answer

The 42nd Amendment added “Socialist” and “Secular” and changed “unity of the Nation” to “unity and integrity of the Nation”. It is the key amendment to mention in an answer on the Preamble.

15. A foreign national has lived in India for several years on a foreign passport and residential permit. Has the person automatically acquired Indian citizenship or domicile? Problem

Issue: Whether long residence and a residential permit establish Indian domicile or citizenship.

Rule: Domicile generally requires a permanent home and intention to remain. Residence is not the same as domicile. Citizenship must be acquired under the Constitution and Citizenship Act through a recognised statutory route.

Application: Long stay and a permit may show residence, but do not by themselves prove an intention to make India a permanent home or establish acquisition of citizenship. Examine intention and whether statutory requirements for registration, naturalisation or another applicable route are met.

Conclusion: Citizenship or domicile cannot be presumed merely from these facts; the legal conditions and evidence must be examined.
