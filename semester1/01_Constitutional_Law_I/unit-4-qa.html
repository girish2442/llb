Part A – 2-mark questions

2 MARKS

1. What is Article 20?

Article 20 provides protection in respect of conviction for offences. It protects against ex post facto criminal laws, double jeopardy and compelled self-incrimination.
2 MARKS

2. What is an ex post facto criminal law?

It is a retrospective criminal law that seeks to punish an act that was not an offence when committed or imposes a heavier penalty than the law provided at the time of the act. Article 20(1) prohibits such retrospective criminal liability and heavier punishment.
2 MARKS

3. What is double jeopardy?

Article 20(2) provides that no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once.
2 MARKS

4. What is self-incrimination?

Self-incrimination means being compelled to give evidence against oneself. Article 20(3) protects a person accused of an offence from being compelled to be a witness against himself or herself.
2 MARKS

5. State Article 21.

Article 21 states that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.
2 MARKS

6. Name the leading case that expanded Article 21.

Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978). It held that the procedure depriving life or liberty must be fair, just and reasonable.
2 MARKS

7. Name one right recognised under Article 21.

Examples include the right to privacy, speedy trial, legal aid, livelihood, dignity and a clean environment.
2 MARKS

8. What is Article 22?

Article 22 provides constitutional safeguards relating to arrest and detention, including safeguards for ordinary arrest and special provisions concerning preventive detention.
2 MARKS

9. Within how many hours must an arrested person ordinarily be produced before a magistrate?

Within 24 hours of arrest, excluding the time necessary for the journey, under Article 22(2).
2 MARKS

10. What is preventive detention?

Preventive detention is detention intended to prevent a person from acting in a manner prejudicial to specified interests in the future. It is preventive, not punishment for a past offence.
2 MARKS

11. What is Article 25?

Article 25 protects freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to constitutional limitations.
2 MARKS

12. What is Article 26?

Article 26 gives religious denominations or sections thereof rights to establish and maintain institutions, manage religious affairs, own and acquire property and administer property in accordance with law, subject to public order, morality and health.
2 MARKS

13. What does Article 27 prohibit?

Article 27 prohibits compelling a person to pay taxes whose proceeds are specifically appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of a particular religion or religious denomination.
2 MARKS

14. What is the main restriction on freedom of religion?

Articles 25 and 26 are subject to public order, morality and health, and Article 25 is also subject to the other provisions of Part III.

Part B – 5-mark questions

5 MARKS

1. Explain the three protections under Article 20.

Article 20 provides three safeguards. First, Article 20(1) prohibits conviction for an act that was not an offence when committed and prohibits a heavier retrospective penalty. Second, Article 20(2) protects a person from being prosecuted and punished more than once for the same offence. Third, Article 20(3) protects a person accused of an offence from being compelled to be a witness against himself or herself.

These protections ensure fairness and prevent abuse of criminal process. Important cases include Maqbool Hussain, Kathi Kalu Oghad and Nandini Satpathy.

5 MARKS

2. Explain the protection against double jeopardy under Article 20(2).

Article 20(2) states that no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. The constitutional protection requires a prior prosecution and punishment and a subsequent prosecution for the same offence. A departmental inquiry or administrative action does not automatically amount to prosecution and punishment for an offence.

In Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay (1953), customs confiscation was not treated as prosecution and punishment by a court or judicial tribunal. In S.A. Venkataraman v. Union of India (1954), departmental proceedings were distinguished from criminal prosecution.

5 MARKS

3. Explain the protection against self-incrimination under Article 20(3).

Article 20(3) states that no person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself. It protects an accused from compelled testimonial evidence that is incriminating. In State of Bombay v. Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961), the Court distinguished testimonial compulsion from the taking of physical evidence such as fingerprints. In Nandini Satpathy v. P.L. Dani (1978), the Court considered the protection during police questioning.

5 MARKS

4. Explain the importance of Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India.

In Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), the Supreme Court gave Article 21 a broad interpretation. It held that the procedure established by law for depriving a person of life or personal liberty must be fair, just and reasonable and not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive. The Court also recognised the relationship among Articles 14, 19 and 21. This judgment transformed the protection of personal liberty in India.

5 MARKS

5. Explain the safeguards available to an arrested person under Article 22(1) and (2).

An arrested person must be informed as soon as may be of the grounds of arrest and has the right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of choice. The person must be produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours, excluding journey time. Detention beyond this period requires the authority of a magistrate. These safeguards do not apply in the same way to enemy aliens or persons detained under preventive detention laws, as provided in Article 22(3).

5 MARKS

7. Explain freedom of religion under Article 25.

Article 25 protects freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion. It applies to all persons. The right is subject to public order, morality, health and the other provisions of Part III. The State may regulate secular activities associated with religious practice and make laws for social welfare and reform. In Rev. Stainislaus v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1977), the Court held that propagation does not include a right to convert another person by force, fraud or allurement.

5 MARKS

8. Explain Articles 26, 27 and 28.

Article 26 protects the rights of religious denominations to establish institutions, manage religious affairs and administer property, subject to public order, morality and health. Article 27 protects persons from being compelled to pay taxes specifically appropriated to promote or maintain a particular religion. Article 28 regulates religious instruction in educational institutions, including a prohibition in institutions wholly maintained out of State funds, subject to the exceptions and consent requirements in the Article.

Part C – 10-mark questions

10 MARKS

1. Discuss the protections guaranteed by Article 20 with suitable case law.

Introduction: Article 20 protects persons in criminal proceedings and contains three safeguards.

Ex post facto laws – Article 20(1): A person cannot be convicted for an act that was not an offence when committed or given a heavier penalty retrospectively.

Double jeopardy – Article 20(2): No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once. In Maqbool Hussain v. State of Bombay (1953), customs confiscation was not treated as prosecution and punishment by a court or judicial tribunal.

Self-incrimination – Article 20(3): An accused person cannot be compelled to be a witness against himself. Kathi Kalu Oghad (1961) distinguished testimonial evidence from physical evidence. Nandini Satpathy (1978) considered the protection during questioning.

Conclusion: Article 20 protects fairness, prevents retrospective criminal punishment and guards against repeated prosecution and compelled self-incrimination.

10 MARKS

2. Explain the scope and judicial interpretation of Article 21.

Introduction: Article 21 protects life and personal liberty of every person from deprivation except according to procedure established by law.

Judicial development: In A.K. Gopalan (1950), the Court adopted an early narrow approach. In Maneka Gandhi (1978), the Court held that the procedure must be fair, just and reasonable and not arbitrary. Articles 14, 19 and 21 are interconnected where applicable.

Expanded rights: Judicial interpretation has recognised rights connected with dignity and liberty, including speedy trial (Hussainara Khatoon), livelihood (Olga Tellis), privacy (Puttaswamy), legal aid and environmental protection.

Conclusion: Article 21 is a broad guarantee of dignified life and personal liberty, subject to constitutionally valid law and fair procedure.

10 MARKS

3. Explain the safeguards against arrest and preventive detention under Article 22.

Introduction: Article 22 contains safeguards for persons arrested or detained and makes a distinction between ordinary arrest and preventive detention.

Ordinary arrest: Under clauses (1) and (2), the arrested person must be informed of the grounds of arrest, may consult and be defended by a lawyer, and must be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours excluding journey time. Detention beyond that period requires magistrate authority.

Exceptions: Clause (3) excludes enemy aliens and persons detained under preventive detention laws from the clauses (1) and (2) safeguards.

Preventive detention: Clauses (4) to (7) provide safeguards relating to Advisory Board review, communication of grounds, opportunity to make a representation and Parliament's power to prescribe specified matters.

Judicial review: D.K. Basu (1997) provides arrest safeguards. Khudiram Das (1975) confirms that preventive detention is subject to judicial review on recognised grounds.

Conclusion: Article 22 attempts to balance preventive powers with safeguards for personal liberty.

10 MARKS

4. Discuss the constitutional right to freedom of religion under Articles 25 to 28.

Introduction: Articles 25 to 28 protect religious freedom and support India's constitutional commitment to freedom of conscience and religious neutrality.

Article 25: Protects freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and other Fundamental Rights. The State may regulate secular activities and enact social welfare and reform laws.

Article 26: Protects religious denominations in establishing institutions, managing religious affairs and administering property, subject to public order, morality and health.

Article 27: Prevents compulsory payment of taxes specifically appropriated for promoting or maintaining a particular religion.

Article 28: Regulates religious instruction in educational institutions and protects against compulsory participation in specified circumstances.

Cases: Shirur Mutt (1954) discussed religious matters and secular administration. Rev. Stainislaus (1977) held that propagation does not include forced conversion. Bijoe Emmanuel (1986) protected freedom of conscience on the facts.

Conclusion: Religious freedom is important but subject to constitutional limitations and the rights of others.

Part D – 15-mark / essay questions

15 MARKS

1. “Article 21 is the foundation of the right to live with human dignity.” Discuss with leading cases.

Suggested answer structure: quote Article 21; explain “life” and “personal liberty”; describe the early approach in A.K. Gopalan; explain the transformation in Maneka Gandhi; discuss dignity, livelihood (Olga Tellis), speedy trial (Hussainara Khatoon), privacy (Puttaswamy), legal aid and environment; explain that rights are subject to constitutionally valid restrictions; conclude that Article 21 protects dignified human existence.

15 MARKS

2. Explain the constitutional safeguards against arrest and preventive detention under Article 22.

Suggested answer structure: introduction and purpose; ordinary arrest safeguards under Article 22(1) and (2); exceptions under clause (3); meaning and purpose of preventive detention; safeguards under clauses (4) to (7); communication of grounds and representation; Advisory Board review; judicial review and relevant cases such as D.K. Basu, Joginder Kumar and Khudiram Das; conclusion on balancing liberty and public safety.

15 MARKS

3. Discuss the right to freedom of religion under Articles 25 to 28.

Suggested answer structure: introduction; Article 25 and its limitations; Article 26 rights of religious denominations; Article 27 and taxation; Article 28 and religious instruction; distinction between religious and secular activities; social reform and State regulation; cases Shirur Mutt, Rev. Stainislaus and Bijoe Emmanuel; conclusion that religious freedom is protected subject to constitutional limits.

Part E – Problem questions with IRAC answers

Problem 1 – Retrospective increase in criminal punishment

Facts: A person commits an act when the maximum punishment is two years. A later law increases the punishment to five years and the person is sentenced under the new law.

Issue: Whether the heavier penalty can be imposed retrospectively.

Rule: Article 20(1) prohibits a person from being subjected to a penalty greater than that which could have been imposed under the law in force when the offence was committed.

Application: The act was committed when the maximum penalty was two years. Applying the later, heavier penalty retrospectively would violate Article 20(1).

Conclusion: The heavier retrospective penalty is constitutionally prohibited.

Problem 2 – Police compel an accused to give an incriminating confession

Facts: During police questioning, an accused is threatened and compelled to make a statement that incriminates him.

Issue: Whether the statement violates Article 20(3).

Rule: Article 20(3) protects an accused person from being compelled to be a witness against himself. The protection concerns compelled testimonial self-incrimination.

Application: The person is accused of an offence and the statement is allegedly obtained through compulsion. The facts may also raise issues under criminal procedure and evidence law.

Conclusion: The compelled statement may violate Article 20(3), subject to the precise circumstances and applicable law. Nandini Satpathy is relevant.

Problem 3 – Arrested person not produced before magistrate within 24 hours

Facts: A person is arrested and kept in police custody for 36 hours without being produced before a magistrate. No journey-time explanation is offered.

Issue: Whether Article 22(2) has been violated.

Rule: An arrested person must be produced before the nearest magistrate within 24 hours of arrest, excluding time necessary for the journey. Detention beyond that period requires magistrate authority.

Application: The person was held for 36 hours without production and no journey-time justification is given.

Conclusion: The detention appears to violate Article 22(2), subject to any legally relevant facts not stated.

Problem 4 – Religious conversion by force

Facts: An organisation claims that Article 25 gives it an unrestricted right to convert persons by force or fraud.

Issue: Whether the right to propagate religion includes a right to convert another person by force or fraud.

Rule: Article 25 protects the right to propagate religion but is subject to public order, morality, health and other provisions of Part III. In Rev. Stainislaus v. State of Madhya Pradesh (1977), the Court held that propagation does not include a right to convert another person by force, fraud or allurement.

Application: The claimed activity involves force or fraud, which is not protected as an unrestricted religious freedom.

Conclusion: Article 25 does not create a fundamental right to convert another person by force or fraud.

Part F – Supplementary syllabus questions: Articles 23–24 and Secularism

These questions fill topics expressly included in the KSLU Unit IV syllabus but not separately covered in the earlier Unit IV notes/question bank.

2-mark answers

2 MARKS

15. What is Article 23?

Article 23 prohibits trafficking in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour. It applies against the State and private persons.
2 MARKS

16. What is begar?

Begar is a form of forced labour in which a person is compelled to work without payment or without lawful freedom to refuse.
2 MARKS

17. What is Article 24?

Article 24 prohibits employment of a child below fourteen years in any factory or mine or engagement in any other hazardous employment.
2 MARKS

18. Which case explained forced labour broadly under Article 23?

People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982).

5-mark answers

5 MARKS

19. Explain the scope of Article 23.

Article 23(1) prohibits traffic in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour. The protection is available to every person and is enforceable against both State and private conduct. Forced labour is not limited to physical compulsion; economic compulsion may also be relevant. Article 23(2) permits compulsory service for public purposes, but prohibits discrimination on specified grounds when such service is imposed. In People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982), the Supreme Court interpreted forced labour broadly and treated payment below the applicable minimum wage as capable of attracting Article 23, depending on the circumstances.

5 MARKS

20. Explain Article 24 and its importance.

Article 24 prohibits employment of children below fourteen years in factories, mines and other hazardous employment. It protects children from dangerous work and supports their health, development and education. It should be read with Article 21A and child-protection legislation. In M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996), the Supreme Court addressed child labour in hazardous industries and issued directions relating to protection, rehabilitation and education.

5 MARKS

21. Write a short note on secularism under the Indian Constitution.

Secularism means that the State has no official religion and must respect constitutional equality and freedom of conscience. Articles 25–28 protect religious freedom while permitting restrictions for public order, morality, health and other constitutional purposes. In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), secularism was recognised as a basic feature of the Constitution.

10-mark answers

10 MARKS

22. Discuss the constitutional right against exploitation under Articles 23 and 24.

Introduction: Articles 23 and 24 protect human dignity by prohibiting specified forms of exploitation.

Article 23: Prohibits trafficking in human beings, begar and similar forms of forced labour. It applies to every person and operates against State and private conduct. Economic compulsion can be relevant to forced labour. Article 23(2) permits compulsory service for public purposes subject to the non-discrimination condition.

Article 24: Prohibits employment of children below fourteen in factories, mines and hazardous employment. It should be read with Article 21A and child-protection laws.

Cases: People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982) on forced labour; Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984) on bonded labour; and M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996) on child labour.

Conclusion: These provisions place constitutional limits on exploitation and require the State to take effective protective measures.

10 MARKS

23. Explain secularism and freedom of religion under Articles 25–28.

Introduction: Secularism is a basic feature of the Indian Constitution. It requires the State to act without establishing an official religion and to respect religious freedom consistently with constitutional limits.

Article 25: Freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion, subject to public order, morality, health and other Part III provisions. It also permits social welfare and reform laws.

Article 26: Rights of religious denominations to manage religious affairs and institutions, subject to public order, morality and health.

Article 27: Protection against being compelled to pay taxes specifically appropriated for promotion or maintenance of a particular religion.

Article 28: Rules on religious instruction and worship in educational institutions.

Cases: S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994); Shirur Mutt (1954); Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986).

Conclusion: Articles 25–28 balance individual conscience, denominational autonomy, secular governance and legitimate constitutional regulation.

15-mark answer

15 MARKS

24. Critically discuss the constitutional protection against exploitation and the role of secularism in protecting individual freedom.

Suggested structure: (1) Explain the constitutional values of dignity, liberty and equality. (2) Discuss Article 23: trafficking, begar, forced labour, horizontal application and public-purpose exception. (3) Discuss Article 24: age threshold and prohibited employment, read with Article 21A and legislation. (4) Analyse Articles 25–28 separately, including constitutional restrictions. (5) Explain secularism as a basic feature and the balance between religious autonomy and regulation. (6) Apply relevant cases: PUDR (1982), Bandhua Mukti Morcha (1984), M.C. Mehta (1996), Shirur Mutt (1954), Bijoe Emmanuel (1986) and S.R. Bommai (1994). (7) Conclude that the Constitution protects human dignity and conscience while permitting proportionate regulation under its express provisions.

Problem questions with IRAC

PROBLEM / IRAC

25. A contractor employs workers on a public project and pays them less than the legally applicable minimum wage. The workers say they cannot refuse because of severe economic pressure. Discuss.

Issue: Whether the arrangement may amount to forced labour under Article 23.

Rule: Article 23 prohibits forced labour; economic compulsion may be relevant. People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982) explains the broad constitutional meaning.

Application: Examine the actual wage, applicable minimum-wage law, working conditions and degree of compulsion. If the contractor has extracted labour below the statutory minimum wage, the facts may support an Article 23 violation.

Conclusion: The arrangement is capable of violating Article 23; the final conclusion depends on proof and applicable law.

PROBLEM / IRAC

26. A factory employs children aged twelve and thirteen in hazardous work. Identify the constitutional issue.

Issue: Whether employing children below fourteen in hazardous work violates Article 24.

Rule: Article 24 prohibits such employment in factories, mines and other hazardous employment.

Application: The children are below fourteen and the work is hazardous; the constitutional prohibition is directly relevant. Applicable child-labour legislation and rehabilitation duties must also be considered.

Conclusion: The employment violates Article 24 on the stated facts.
