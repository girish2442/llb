Unit I — General Principles

BNS framework, kinds of punishment, actus reus and mens rea, mistake of fact and law, and general exceptions. Confirm the exact syllabus topics before final revision.

QUESTION 1 · LONG ANSWER

Explain the different kinds of punishments under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

Introduction

Punishment is a legal consequence imposed by a competent court on a person found guilty of an offence. It aims to protect society, prevent crime, punish offenders and promote respect for the law.

Section 4 of the BNS recognises six principal kinds of punishment.

1. Death penalty

The death penalty is the most severe punishment and involves execution pursuant to a lawful sentence. It is prescribed for certain serious offences. The Supreme Court has held that it should be imposed only in the rarest of rare cases.

Case law: Bachan Singh v. State of Punjab (1980) established the rarest-of-rare principle.

2. Imprisonment for life

Life imprisonment means imprisonment for the remainder of the offender’s natural life, subject to applicable legal provisions governing remission or commutation. It does not automatically mean imprisonment for only 14 years.

3. Imprisonment

  • Rigorous imprisonment: imprisonment involving labour as provided by law.
  • Simple imprisonment: imprisonment without the requirement of rigorous labour.

The type and duration depend on the offence and the punishment authorised by law.

4. Forfeiture of property

Forfeiture means the lawful loss of property to the State when the relevant law authorises that punishment.

5. Fine

A fine is a monetary penalty imposed by a court. The amount and any applicable limits depend on the relevant offence provision.

6. Community service

Community service requires an offender to perform prescribed work for the benefit of the community without remuneration. It is recognised as a punishment under the BNS.

Solitary confinement

Solitary confinement is not listed as a separate principal punishment under Section 4. The BNS provides for it in Sections 11 and 12, subject to statutory restrictions and limits.

Conclusion

Section 4 recognises death, life imprisonment, rigorous or simple imprisonment, forfeiture of property, fine and community service. The appropriate punishment depends on the offence, statutory provision and circumstances of the case.

QUESTION 2 · LONG ANSWER / SHORT NOTE

Explain actus reus and mens rea as essential elements of a crime.

Introduction

A crime is an act or omission prohibited by law and punishable by the State. In many offences, criminal liability depends on two important elements: actus reus and mens rea.

1. Actus reus

Actus reus means the guilty act or external element of a crime. Depending on the offence, it may consist of a positive act, a legally relevant omission, a particular consequence or a combination of these.

2. Mens rea

Mens rea means the guilty mind or mental element required by an offence. Depending on the statutory wording, it may involve intention, knowledge, recklessness, dishonesty, fraudulence or another specified mental state.

3. Relationship between them

In many crimes, the prosecution must establish both the prohibited conduct and the required mental element. The precise requirements depend on the definition of the particular offence. Some statutory offences may restrict or exclude the usual mental-element requirement.

4. Importance

Mens rea helps distinguish an intentional or knowing offence from an innocent mistake or accident. The prosecution must prove the elements required by law, subject to applicable legal rules.

Conclusion

Actus reus refers to the prohibited external element, while mens rea refers to the required mental element.

Easy memory aid: Actus reus = what the person did or failed to do. Mens rea = the state of mind required by the offence.

QUESTION 3 · LONG ANSWER

Explain the maxim “Ignorantia facti excusat, ignorantia juris non excusat” with reference to the BNS.

Introduction

The maxim means ignorance of fact may excuse, but ignorance of law does not excuse. A genuine mistake about facts may, in appropriate circumstances, affect criminal responsibility. A person generally cannot escape liability merely by claiming not to know the law.

1. Mistake of fact

A mistake of fact occurs when a person has an incorrect understanding of a factual situation. Under Section 14 of the BNS, an act may be protected where a person, because of a mistake of fact and in good faith, believes themselves to be bound by law to do the act or believes that the act is justified by law.

The protection is subject to statutory requirements and is not a general excuse for every factual mistake.

2. Mistake of law

A person cannot ordinarily avoid criminal responsibility simply by claiming, “I did not know that the law prohibited this conduct.”

3. Difference

  • Mistake of fact: concerns an incorrect belief about facts; it may provide a defence if statutory conditions are satisfied.
  • Mistake of law: concerns ignorance or misunderstanding of law; it generally does not excuse criminal liability.

Conclusion

The maxim distinguishes factual mistakes from mistakes about the law. Section 14 provides a specific protection for certain acts done under a qualifying mistake of fact.

Exam tip: Do not write that every mistake of fact is a defence. State the conditions of Section 14 and apply them to the problem.

QUESTION 4 · LONG ANSWER

Explain the general exceptions under the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023.

Introduction

General exceptions are legal circumstances in which an act that might otherwise appear to be an offence is not treated as criminal under the applicable law. The general exceptions in the BNS are contained in Sections 14–44.

Important general exceptions

1. Mistake of fact — Section 14: A qualifying act done in good faith under a mistake of fact, where the person believes they are bound or justified by law, may be protected.

2. Act of a judge — Section 15: An act done by a judge while acting judicially and exercising a power believed in good faith to be conferred by law may fall within the statutory protection.

3. Act done pursuant to a court judgment or order — Section 16: An act done in pursuance of a court judgment or order may be protected where statutory conditions are satisfied.

4. Accident — Section 18: An accidental occurrence may provide a defence where the act is done without criminal intention or knowledge, while doing a lawful act in a lawful manner, by lawful means and with proper care and caution.

5. Necessity — Section 19: An act may be protected where it is done without criminal intention to prevent or avoid other harm, subject to statutory requirements.

6. Infancy — Sections 20 and 21: The BNS provides protections for children below specified ages and qualified protection for children within the statutory age range where the required maturity of understanding is absent.

7. Unsoundness of mind — Section 22: A person may be protected where, at the time of the act, unsoundness of mind makes them incapable of knowing the nature of the act or that it is wrong or contrary to law, as specified in the section.

8. Intoxication — Sections 23 and 24: Criminal responsibility depends on how intoxication arose and the applicable statutory requirements. Voluntary intoxication does not automatically excuse a person.

9. Consent — Sections 25–30: Certain acts involving consent may fall within statutory exceptions, subject to restrictions concerning intention, knowledge, harm, age and capacity. Consent is not a defence to every offence.

10. Compulsion — Section 32: An act done under qualifying threats may receive protection, subject to the provision's conditions and exclusions.

11. Trivial harm — Section 33: An act causing harm so slight that a person of ordinary sense and temper would not complain of it may fall within this exception.

12. Private defence — Sections 34–44: The BNS recognises the right to defend one's own body, another person's body and property in prescribed circumstances. The right is subject to statutory limits and does not authorise unlimited force.

Importance and conclusion

General exceptions define circumstances in which criminal liability may not arise. The accused's conduct, intention, knowledge and surrounding circumstances must be assessed under the relevant BNS provision.

Exam tip: For a long answer, explain the exceptions most relevant to the question. For a problem question, identify the section, state its conditions, apply them to the facts and conclude.

QUESTION 5 · LONG ANSWER

Explain abetment under the BNS and its essential ingredients.

Introduction

Abetment means intentionally encouraging, helping or joining in a plan that leads to an offence, in the manner recognised by law. The BNS deals with abetment in Sections 45–60.

Ways in which abetment may occur — Section 45

A person may abet the doing of a thing in three principal ways:

  • Instigation: encouraging, urging or provoking another person to do the act.
  • Conspiracy: engaging with one or more persons in a conspiracy to do the thing, where an act or illegal omission takes place in pursuance of that conspiracy and in order to carry it out.
  • Intentional aid: intentionally helping the act through an act or illegal omission, including by facilitating it before or during its commission.

Essential ingredients

  • There must be conduct amounting to instigation, the legally required conspiracy, or intentional aid.
  • The required intention or mental element must be established from the facts.
  • The conduct must satisfy the statutory definition; mere presence or ordinary association is not automatically abetment.

Must the main offence be completed?

No. Section 45 explains that abetment may be an offence even if the act abetted is not committed or the intended effect does not occur. The applicable punishment depends on the relevant BNS provision and circumstances.

Example

A intentionally urges B to commit an offence. Even if B refuses, A may still be liable for abetment by instigation if the statutory ingredients are proved.

Conclusion

Abetment is a form of secondary criminal liability. The prosecution must establish the legally recognised form of abetment and the required mental element, not merely that the accused knew the principal offender.

Remember: Section 45 defines abetment; Section 46 explains who is an abettor. Punishment provisions follow in Sections 49 onwards.

QUESTION 6 · LONG ANSWER / DISTINCTION

Distinguish abetment by conspiracy from criminal conspiracy.

Introduction

The BNS recognises both abetment by conspiracy and the separate offence of criminal conspiracy. They are related, but their legal ingredients are not identical.

Abetment by conspiracy — Section 45

For abetment by conspiracy, the person must engage with one or more persons in a conspiracy to do the thing, and an act or illegal omission must take place in pursuance of that conspiracy and in order to carry out the object. The statutory requirement of an act or illegal omission in pursuance of the conspiracy is important.

Criminal conspiracy — Section 61

Criminal conspiracy involves an agreement between two or more persons to do an illegal act, or to do a legal act by illegal means. Where the agreement is to commit an offence, the agreement itself is generally sufficient; for other illegal objects, the statute requires an act beyond the agreement in the circumstances specified by Section 61.

Key differences

  • Legal character: abetment by conspiracy is one method of abetment; criminal conspiracy is a separate offence.
  • Act in pursuance: abetment by conspiracy requires an act or illegal omission in pursuance of the conspiracy and to carry it out. For a conspiracy to commit an offence, Section 61 generally treats the agreement itself as sufficient.
  • Focus: abetment by conspiracy is linked to abetting a particular thing; criminal conspiracy focuses on the unlawful agreement.
  • Proof: both may be proved through circumstances, but the prosecution must establish the ingredients of the specific offence charged.

Conclusion

Do not treat the two concepts as interchangeable. In an exam answer, state Section 45 for abetment by conspiracy and Section 61 for criminal conspiracy, then compare their ingredients.

QUESTION 7 · LONG ANSWER

Explain attempt to commit an offence and the tests used to distinguish preparation from attempt.

Introduction

Attempt is a stage of crime between preparation and completion. The offence may remain incomplete, but the accused has moved far enough towards committing it to attract criminal liability.

Preparation and attempt

Preparation means arranging or planning the means needed to commit an offence. Attempt involves an act sufficiently close to carrying out the offence, with the required intention or knowledge. Mere intention is not enough, and preparation is generally not punishable unless a specific law says otherwise.

Section 62 BNS

Section 62 provides a general punishment provision for attempting to commit offences punishable with imprisonment for life or other imprisonment, where the BNS does not provide a specific punishment for the attempt. The precise punishment depends on the section and circumstances.

Common tests used by courts

  • Proximity test: how close was the accused's act to completing the offence?
  • Unequivocality test: do the acts clearly show the criminal intention, rather than an innocent purpose?
  • Substantial-step approach: has the accused taken a significant step towards carrying out the offence? This is a useful explanatory approach, not a replacement for the governing Indian statutory test.

Illustration

Buying tools with an intention to break into a building may be preparation. Breaking a lock and entering to steal may amount to attempt or a completed offence depending on the facts and applicable provision.

Conclusion

Attempt requires both the required mental element and conduct that has progressed beyond mere preparation. The court assesses the entire sequence of acts and the relevant offence definition.

QUESTION 8 · LONG ANSWER

Explain rape under the BNS and briefly discuss related sexual offences.

Introduction

The BNS contains provisions addressing rape and other sexual offences in Sections 63–73. These offences protect bodily autonomy and sexual integrity. In an exam, use the statutory definition and avoid relying only on a general description.

Rape — Section 63

Section 63 defines rape by specifying the sexual acts covered and the circumstances in which they constitute rape. These circumstances include lack of consent and other situations expressly set out by the section. Consent must be assessed according to the statutory definition and the facts; silence or lack of physical resistance does not by itself establish consent.

Punishment — Sections 64–66

Sections 64–66 prescribe punishment for rape and specified aggravated circumstances, including certain cases involving the age of the victim and cases causing death or a persistent vegetative state. The applicable subsection must be identified before stating the punishment.

Other related offences

  • Section 67 addresses sexual intercourse by a husband with his wife during separation, as defined by the section.
  • Section 68 deals with sexual intercourse by a person in authority in the circumstances specified by law.
  • Section 69 addresses sexual intercourse by employing deceitful means or making a promise in the circumstances defined by the provision, where the conduct does not amount to rape.
  • Section 70 addresses gang rape; Sections 71–73 deal with repeat offenders and certain matters concerning the identity of victims and publication of court proceedings.

Importance of consent and evidence

The prosecution must establish the elements of the offence charged. Courts consider the evidence as a whole and apply the relevant criminal procedure and evidence law. Personal assumptions or stereotypes should not replace legal analysis.

Conclusion

The BNS distinguishes rape from other sexual offences and prescribes different provisions for different circumstances. A good answer should define the offence under Section 63, discuss the relevant punishment section and apply the correct subsection to the facts.

QUESTION 9 · LONG ANSWER / SHORT NOTE

Explain dowry death and cruelty by a husband or his relatives under the BNS.

Dowry death — Section 80

Section 80 addresses a woman's death caused by burns, bodily injury or otherwise than under normal circumstances within seven years of marriage, where it is shown that soon before her death she was subjected by her husband or a relative of her husband to cruelty or harassment in connection with a demand for dowry. Where the statutory conditions are satisfied, the death is treated as dowry death and the person responsible is liable to the punishment prescribed by the section.

Cruelty — Sections 85 and 86

Section 85 penalises a husband or relative of a husband who subjects a woman to cruelty. Section 86 defines cruelty for this purpose. It includes wilful conduct likely to drive the woman to suicide or cause grave injury or danger to life, limb or health, and harassment aimed at coercing her or her relatives to meet an unlawful demand for property or valuable security.

Difference

  • Dowry death: requires the death and the specific statutory circumstances, including the connection to dowry-related cruelty or harassment soon before death.
  • Cruelty: may be committed even when the woman has not died; the prosecution must prove the conduct defined by Section 86.

Conclusion

Sections 80, 85 and 86 address serious forms of abuse connected with marriage. The precise statutory ingredients and evidence must be considered separately for each offence.

Explain sexual harassment, stalking and assault against women under the BNS.

Relevant provisions

The BNS addresses assault or criminal force against a woman with intent to outrage her modesty in Section 74, sexual harassment in Section 75, assault or criminal force with intent to disrobe in Section 76, voyeurism in Section 77, stalking in Section 78, and words, gestures or acts intended to insult the modesty of a woman in Section 79.

Conclusion

These provisions address different conduct and have separate ingredients. In an exam answer, identify the conduct, intent or knowledge required, and the specific section instead of treating all harassment as one offence.

QUESTION 10 · LONG ANSWER · DISTINCTION

Distinguish culpable homicide from murder under the BNS.

Introduction

Culpable homicide and murder both concern causing the death of a human being, but murder is the more serious form. The distinction depends on the intention or knowledge of the accused and the statutory exceptions.

Culpable homicide — Section 100

A person commits culpable homicide when they cause death by doing an act with the intention of causing death, or with the intention of causing bodily injury likely to cause death, or with the knowledge that the act is likely to cause death, as described in Section 100.

Murder — Section 101

Section 101 sets out when culpable homicide amounts to murder. It covers specified levels of intention or knowledge and includes statutory exceptions. The facts must be tested against the exact clauses of the section.

Important distinction

  • Degree of intention/knowledge: murder involves the higher degree described in Section 101; culpable homicide is the broader category.
  • Probability of death: the degree of likelihood and the nature of the injury are important to the statutory test.
  • Exceptions: conduct that would otherwise fall within murder may be reduced to culpable homicide not amounting to murder if a statutory exception applies.
  • Punishment: Section 103 deals with punishment for murder; Section 105 deals with culpable homicide not amounting to murder.

Illustration

If A intentionally inflicts a fatal injury in circumstances meeting Section 101 and no exception applies, the offence may be murder. If the facts fall within a statutory exception, the offence may instead be culpable homicide not amounting to murder.

Conclusion

Every murder is culpable homicide, but every culpable homicide is not murder. A correct answer compares Sections 100 and 101 and then considers any applicable exception.

QUESTION 11 · LONG ANSWER

Explain causing death by negligence and distinguish it from murder and culpable homicide.

Introduction

Death may result not only from an intentional act but also from a rash or negligent act. The BNS addresses causing death by negligence in Section 106, subject to the applicable statutory wording and any amendments or commencement qualifications.

Meaning of negligence

Negligence generally involves failure to take the care that a reasonable person would take in the circumstances. Rashness involves acting with disregard for the consequences or risk. Criminal liability requires the level of negligence or rashness recognised by criminal law; every civil negligence claim is not automatically a crime.

Distinction from culpable homicide and murder

  • Murder/culpable homicide: the prosecution must prove the relevant intention or knowledge under Sections 100 and 101.
  • Death by negligence: the prosecution must prove the legally required rash or negligent act and causal connection with death; the mental element differs from intentional homicide.

Causation

The prosecution must establish that the accused's act or omission was legally connected to the death. A mere unfortunate outcome does not by itself prove criminal negligence.

Conclusion

Section 106 should be applied according to its exact wording and the facts. The key question is whether the death resulted from the level of rashness or negligence required by law, rather than the intention or knowledge required for homicide.

Exam note: Section 106 has had a special commencement qualification concerning sub-section (2). Check the current official position before writing about that sub-section.

QUESTION 12 · LONG ANSWER / SHORT NOTE

Explain hurt and grievous hurt under the BNS.

Hurt — Sections 114–115

Section 114 defines hurt as causing bodily pain, disease or infirmity to a person. Section 115 deals with voluntarily causing hurt, where the act is done with the intention of causing hurt or with the knowledge that hurt is likely, and hurt is caused.

Grievous hurt — Sections 116–117

Section 116 lists the kinds of injury treated as grievous hurt. These include specified serious injuries such as emasculation, permanent loss of sight or hearing, loss or impairment of a member or joint, permanent disfiguration of the head or face, fracture or dislocation of a bone or tooth, and other injuries meeting the statutory definition.

Section 117 addresses voluntarily causing grievous hurt. The punishment depends on the exact facts and the relevant provision.

Difference

  • Hurt: bodily pain, disease or infirmity.
  • Grievous hurt: injury falling within one of the categories specifically listed by the BNS.
  • Proof: medical evidence may be important, but the legal classification must match the statutory definition.

Conclusion

The distinction between hurt and grievous hurt affects the applicable offence and punishment. An answer should define each term, identify the relevant sections and explain the seriousness of the injury in light of the statute.

QUESTION 13 · DISTINCTION

Distinguish wrongful restraint from wrongful confinement.

Wrongful restraint — Section 126

Wrongful restraint involves voluntarily obstructing a person so as to prevent that person from proceeding in a direction in which they have a right to proceed, subject to the statutory exceptions.

Wrongful confinement — Section 127

Wrongful confinement is a more serious form of restraint in which a person is prevented from proceeding beyond certain circumscribed limits. It restricts the person's freedom within a boundary or area.

Difference

  • Restraint blocks a person's movement in a particular direction.
  • Confinement prevents the person from going beyond specified limits.
  • Confinement is therefore a more complete restriction of movement.

Example

Blocking a person's path so that they cannot proceed along a particular road may amount to wrongful restraint. Locking a person inside a room and preventing them from leaving may amount to wrongful confinement.

Conclusion

Both offences protect freedom of movement. The main distinction is whether the obstruction is directional or confines the person within defined limits.

QUESTION 14 · LONG ANSWER

Explain organised crime and terrorist acts under the BNS.

Organised crime — Section 111

Section 111 addresses continuing unlawful activity by a person or group acting as part of an organised crime syndicate, using unlawful means such as violence, intimidation, coercion or other methods specified by the section, for material or other benefits. The definition contains specific statutory requirements, including the relevant pattern of activity and the required prior charge-sheet/cognizance conditions. Each element must be checked in the current Bare Act.

Petty organised crime — Section 112

Section 112 addresses specified forms of organised criminal activity, including certain group or gang activities such as theft, snatching, cheating and other listed conduct, when the statutory conditions are satisfied.

Terrorist act — Section 113

Section 113 addresses acts committed with the intent and consequences described by the section, including acts threatening the unity, integrity, sovereignty, security or economic security of India, or intended to strike terror, where the statutory requirements are met.

Importance

These provisions target serious organised or terror-related conduct that may affect public safety and national security. They have detailed definitions and serious penalties, so a student should avoid relying on broad everyday meanings alone.

Conclusion

Sections 111, 112 and 113 deal with different but related categories of serious criminal activity. In an exam, define each separately and identify the exact statutory ingredients rather than treating them as interchangeable.

QUESTION 27 · LONG ANSWER

Explain kidnapping and abduction and distinguish between them.

Kidnapping — Section 137

The BNS recognises kidnapping from India and kidnapping from lawful guardianship. The statutory definition includes taking or enticing a child or a person of unsound mind out of the keeping of the lawful guardian without the guardian's consent, subject to the age and other requirements in the section.

Abduction — Section 138

Abduction involves compelling a person by force, or inducing a person by deceitful means, to go from any place. It is defined by the means used to cause the movement and is not limited to children.

Difference

  • Kidnapping from lawful guardianship is defined by the person's age/status, taking or enticing, and absence of guardian consent.
  • Abduction involves force or deceitful inducement to move a person.
  • Kidnapping is complete when the statutory act occurs; abduction often becomes punishable when linked with a further criminal purpose or a specific offence provision.

Conclusion

Kidnapping and abduction are distinct statutory concepts. The prosecution must establish the elements of the particular form charged and any additional purpose required by the applicable section.

QUESTION 15 · LONG ANSWER

Explain offences against the State under the BNS.

Introduction

Offences against the State are serious offences affecting the security, sovereignty or lawful functioning of the country. The BNS places these provisions mainly in Sections 147–158.

Examples of offences

  • Section 147: waging, attempting to wage, or abetting the waging of war against the Government of India.
  • Sections 148–150: certain conspiracies, collection of arms and concealment connected with waging war.
  • Section 151: assaulting the President, Governor or another specified constitutional authority with the intent described by the section.
  • Section 152: acts endangering the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India, as defined by the provision.
  • Sections 153–158: specified offences involving war against a foreign State at peace with India and certain prisoners of State or war.

Section 152

Section 152 addresses acts that purposely or knowingly excite or attempt to excite secession, armed rebellion, subversive activities, separatist activities, or otherwise endanger the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India, or encourage such acts, as stated in the section. The exact statutory wording and its explanation should be read carefully. Lawful criticism of government measures does not automatically amount to this offence; the statutory elements must be proved.

Conclusion

Offences against the State protect national security and constitutional order. An exam answer should identify the relevant section and distinguish the elements of the particular offence rather than making a general statement that all criticism is criminal.

QUESTION 16 · LONG ANSWER

Explain unlawful assembly, rioting and affray.

Unlawful assembly — Section 189

An assembly of five or more persons becomes unlawful when its common object falls within one of the categories specified by Section 189, such as resisting lawful authority or committing certain offences. The common object and the statutory conditions must be proved.

Liability of members — Section 190

Section 190 may make every member of an unlawful assembly liable for an offence committed by a member in prosecution of the assembly's common object, or an offence the members knew was likely to be committed in pursuing that object, subject to the section's requirements.

Rioting — Section 191

Rioting occurs when force or violence is used by an unlawful assembly, or by a member of it, in prosecution of the common object. Thus, an unlawful assembly does not always become a riot: the statutory use of force or violence is relevant.

Affray — Section 194

Affray involves two or more persons fighting in a public place and disturbing public peace, as required by the section.

Difference

  • Unlawful assembly focuses on the number of persons and an unlawful common object.
  • Rioting requires force or violence in the statutory circumstances.
  • Affray focuses on fighting between persons in a public place that disturbs public peace.

Conclusion

These offences protect public order. A good answer should distinguish common object, force or violence, and public fighting, and apply the correct section to the facts.

QUESTION 17 · LONG ANSWER / SHORT NOTE

Explain false evidence and fabricating false evidence under the BNS.

Introduction

The justice system depends on truthful evidence. The BNS contains offences dealing with false evidence and related conduct in the provisions on offences against public justice.

Giving false evidence

Giving false evidence generally involves a person who is legally bound by an oath or law to state the truth, or is required by law to make a declaration, making a statement that is false and that the person knows or believes to be false or does not believe to be true, as defined by the relevant provision.

Fabricating false evidence

Fabricating false evidence involves creating a circumstance, making a false entry or document, or otherwise producing something with the intention that it may appear in evidence in a judicial proceeding or other legally relevant proceeding and cause the decision-maker to form an incorrect opinion on a material point.

Related conduct

The BNS also deals with using evidence known to be false, destruction or concealment of evidence and harbouring an offender in specified circumstances. The exact offence and punishment depend on the relevant section and the nature of the proceeding.

Importance

False evidence can lead to wrongful conviction, acquittal or an incorrect judicial decision. These offences protect the integrity of courts and public justice.

Conclusion

In an exam answer, distinguish a false statement by a person giving evidence from the deliberate creation of false material intended to mislead a decision-maker. Then identify the applicable section and its mental element.

QUESTION 18 · LONG ANSWER

Explain public nuisance and briefly distinguish it from private nuisance.

Public nuisance

The BNS provisions on public health, safety, convenience, decency and morals include public nuisance. In general terms, public nuisance involves an act or illegal omission causing common injury, danger or annoyance to the public or to people in general who use a public right. The statutory definition and relevant section must be applied to the facts.

Examples

Examples may include obstructing a public way or creating a condition that endangers or seriously inconveniences the public, depending on the facts and the relevant provision.

Public nuisance and private nuisance

  • Public nuisance: affects the public or a section of the public in the manner recognised by law.
  • Private nuisance: is generally a civil wrong involving substantial and unreasonable interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land.

Conclusion

Public nuisance protects community interests such as health, safety and convenience. Students should use the BNS section for criminal liability and avoid importing civil tort principles without explaining the difference.

QUESTION 19 · DISTINCTION

Distinguish mischief, criminal trespass and forgery.

Mischief

Mischief under Section 324 involves intentionally or knowingly causing wrongful loss or damage to the public or any person by destroying property or changing property or its situation so as to destroy or diminish its value or utility, or affect it injuriously, as specified by the section.

Criminal trespass

Criminal trespass under Section 329 involves entering into or upon property in another's possession with the required intent to commit an offence or to intimidate, insult or annoy the person in possession, or unlawfully remaining there with the required intent.

Forgery

Forgery is addressed in Section 335 and related provisions. It generally involves making a false document or electronic record with the intention or knowledge specified by law, such as causing damage, supporting a claim, inducing a person to part with property, or committing fraud.

Key difference

  • Mischief concerns damage to property or its value/utility.
  • Criminal trespass concerns unlawful entry or remaining on property with a specified intent.
  • Forgery concerns making a false document or electronic record with the required fraudulent or other statutory intent.

Conclusion

Although one incident may involve more than one offence, each offence has separate ingredients. Apply the relevant section and mental element to the facts.

QUESTION 29 · SHORT NOTE / LONG ANSWER

Explain bribery and undue influence at elections under the BNS.

Relevant provisions

The BNS provisions relating to elections appear in Sections 169–177. Section 169 defines relevant election terms. Sections 170 and 171 address bribery and undue influence at elections, while Sections 173 and 174 prescribe punishment for bribery and undue influence/personation, as specified by the Act.

Bribery

Election bribery generally involves giving, offering, promising, accepting or obtaining gratification in connection with the exercise of an electoral right, in the circumstances described by the statute. The statutory definition and exceptions must be checked.

Undue influence

Undue influence involves voluntary interference or attempted interference with the free exercise of an electoral right through the methods specified by the law. Threats, coercion or certain forms of improper pressure may be relevant, depending on the provision.

Conclusion

These provisions protect free and fair elections. A good answer defines the prohibited conduct, identifies the relevant BNS sections and explains how the conduct interferes with a person's free electoral choice.

QUESTION 30 · SHORT NOTE

Explain offences relating to counterfeit currency and government stamps.

Relevant provisions

The BNS deals with offences relating to coin, currency-notes, bank-notes and government stamps in Sections 178–188. These include counterfeiting, using counterfeit items as genuine, possessing them in specified circumstances, and making or possessing instruments or materials for counterfeiting.

The relevant offence may depend on whether the accused made the counterfeit item, used it as genuine, possessed it with the required knowledge or intention, or dealt with tools or materials for counterfeiting. Each offence has its own ingredients.

QUESTION 20 · LONG ANSWER / DISTINCTION

Distinguish theft, snatching, extortion, robbery and dacoity.

Theft — Section 303

Theft involves dishonestly moving movable property out of the possession of a person without that person's consent, as required by the statutory definition. Dishonest intention and movement of property are important elements.

Snatching — Section 304

Snatching is a specifically defined offence involving sudden or forcible seizure or taking away of movable property from a person or their possession, in the manner described by the section.

Extortion — Section 308

Extortion involves intentionally putting a person in fear of injury and thereby dishonestly inducing that person to deliver property, valuable security or something signed or sealed that may be converted into valuable security.

Robbery — Section 309

Robbery is an aggravated form of theft or extortion. Theft becomes robbery when, for committing the theft or carrying away the property, the offender causes or attempts to cause death, hurt, wrongful restraint or fear of instant death, hurt or wrongful restraint in the circumstances specified by the section. Extortion becomes robbery when the offender is present and puts the person in fear of instant harm as required by the statute.

Dacoity — Section 310

Dacoity occurs when five or more persons conjointly commit or attempt to commit robbery, or are present and aid it, as described by the section.

Quick comparison

  • Theft: dishonest taking without consent.
  • Snatching: sudden or forcible seizure from a person or their possession.
  • Extortion: delivery induced by fear of injury.
  • Robbery: theft or extortion with the specified immediate violence or fear.
  • Dacoity: robbery involving five or more persons acting conjointly.

Conclusion

These offences differ in the way property is taken, the role of fear or force, and the number of participants. The exact statutory ingredients must be applied carefully.

QUESTION 21 · LONG ANSWER / DISTINCTION

Distinguish criminal misappropriation, criminal breach of trust and receiving stolen property.

Criminal misappropriation — Section 314

Criminal misappropriation involves dishonestly misappropriating or converting movable property to one's own use. The key issue is dishonest conversion of property.

Criminal breach of trust — Section 316

Criminal breach of trust involves entrustment of property or dominion over property, followed by dishonest misappropriation, conversion, use or disposal contrary to the law governing the trust or to a legal contract, as set out in the section.

Receiving stolen property — Section 317

This offence concerns dishonestly receiving or retaining property knowing or having reason to believe it is stolen property, subject to the statutory definition and exceptions.

Differences

  • Entrustment: central to criminal breach of trust; not a necessary element of criminal misappropriation.
  • Conversion: criminal misappropriation focuses on dishonest conversion of movable property to one's own use.
  • Knowledge: receiving stolen property requires the legally specified knowledge or reason to believe the property is stolen.

Conclusion

The offences protect property in different situations: dishonest conversion, abuse of entrusted property, and dishonest receipt or retention of stolen property.

QUESTION 22 · LONG ANSWER

Explain cheating under the BNS and distinguish it from a mere breach of contract.

Cheating — Section 318

Cheating involves deceiving a person and fraudulently or dishonestly inducing that person to deliver property or consent to its retention, or intentionally inducing the person to do or omit something that they would not otherwise do or omit, where the act or omission causes or is likely to cause the specified harm to body, mind, reputation or property.

Essential ingredients

  • Deception of a person.
  • Fraudulent or dishonest inducement, or intentional inducement of the type described in the statute.
  • The required delivery, action or omission and the legally specified harm or likelihood of harm.

Cheating and breach of contract

A breach of contract occurs when a party fails to perform a contractual obligation. That failure alone does not prove cheating. For cheating, the required deception and mental element must be established. If dishonest intention existed from the beginning and induced the transaction, that may support a cheating charge; later non-performance by itself is not conclusive proof.

Example

If A obtains money by knowingly making a false representation about a material fact and intends to deceive B, the facts may amount to cheating if the statutory ingredients are satisfied. If A honestly intended to perform but later could not perform the contract, the matter may be only a civil breach unless additional evidence establishes the offence.

Conclusion

Cheating requires the ingredients in Section 318. A student should not equate every broken promise or unpaid debt with a criminal offence.

QUESTION 23 · LONG ANSWER / SHORT NOTE

Explain criminal intimidation and defamation under the BNS.

Criminal intimidation — Section 351

Criminal intimidation generally involves threatening another person with injury to their person, reputation or property, or to the person or reputation of someone in whom they are interested, with the intent to cause alarm or to cause the person to do an act they are not legally bound to do, or omit an act they are legally entitled to do, as specified by Section 351.

Defamation — Section 356

Defamation involves making or publishing an imputation concerning a person with the intention of harming, or knowing or having reason to believe that it will harm, that person's reputation, subject to the statutory definition and exceptions. The BNS includes exceptions that must be considered before concluding that a statement is criminal defamation.

Difference

  • Criminal intimidation focuses on a threat and the intention to cause alarm or compel conduct.
  • Defamation focuses on an imputation that harms reputation and the required mental element.
  • A threat may exist without a defamatory imputation; a defamatory imputation need not contain a threat.

Conclusion

Both offences have detailed statutory ingredients and exceptions. In a problem question, identify the words or conduct, the intention or knowledge, and the relevant statutory conditions.

QUESTION 24 · LONG ANSWER / SHORT NOTE

Explain dishonest intention in property offences and the importance of consent.

Dishonesty

Dishonesty is an important mental element in offences such as theft, extortion, misappropriation and breach of trust. The BNS definition links dishonesty to intention to cause wrongful gain to one person or wrongful loss to another.

Consent

Consent is particularly important to theft, which involves moving property out of a person's possession without consent, and to other property offences whose definitions involve delivery or retention of property. However, consent obtained through legally relevant deception or fear may not have the effect the accused claims.

Application

In a problem question, identify who possessed the property, whether the accused had permission, whether property was moved or delivered, what the accused intended, and whether the statutory elements of the specific offence are met.

Conclusion

Dishonest intention and consent often distinguish a criminal property offence from a lawful transaction or civil dispute. They must be assessed using the exact definition in the BNS.
