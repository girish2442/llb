PART 4 — General Defences

CORE IDEAGeneral defences are recognised grounds on which a defendant may escape liability (or, for contributory negligence, have damages apportioned) even after the plaintiff proves the tort. Order to learn: Volenti → Contributory Negligence → Act of God → Private Defence → Necessity → Statutory Authority → Inevitable Accident → Mistake.

1. Volenti non fit injuria (Consent)

"To one who is willing, no injury is done." Plaintiff who knows the risk and freely/voluntarily accepts it cannot recover for harm from that risk.
Conditions — VKF-C
tap to flip
Knowledge of risk + Free & voluntary acceptance + genuine Consent (no fraud/compulsion) + injury within scope of risk accepted. Rule: knowledge ≠ consent.
Case: Hall v Brooklands Auto Racing Club (1933)
Facts: Spectator injured when racing cars entered the spectator area.
Principle: Attendance = consent to the ordinary risks of the event.
Case: Smith v Baker (1891)
Facts: Employee kept working despite knowing of a danger.
Principle: Mere knowledge ≠ voluntary consent, especially under economic compulsion.
Case: Haynes v Harwood (1935)
Facts: Rescuer injured responding to danger the defendant created.
Principle: Volenti doesn't defeat a rescue claim just because the rescuer knowingly faced danger.
📌 Volenti fails when: knowledge without real choice · economic/other compulsion · rescue cases · fraud/misrepresentation · harm beyond consented scope.

2. Contributory Negligence

Plaintiff's own failure to take reasonable care contributes to the harm. Modern law does NOT fully bar recovery — damages may be apportioned.
Case: Davies v Mann (1842) — Last Opportunity Rule
Facts: Plaintiff left a donkey tied on the road; defendant drove excessively fast and killed it.
Principle: Though plaintiff was initially negligent, defendant had the last opportunity to avoid the accident and failed — liable.

3. Act of God (Vis Major)

3 Essentials — NUH
tap to flip
Natural cause (no human agency) + Unforeseeable/extraordinary event + Humans cannot guard against it.
Case: Nichols v Marsland (1876)
Facts: Extraordinary rainfall burst dams/lakes, flooding plaintiff's land.
Principle: Rainfall was so extraordinary → Act of God succeeded.
Case: Ramalinga Nadar v Narayana Reddiar (1971)
Facts: Goods lost during communal riots/mob violence.
Principle: Riots involve human agency → NOT an Act of God.

4. Private Defence

Permits reasonable force to protect self/others/property from unlawful harm — must be imminent threat + necessary + proportionate; never revenge for a past wrong; no deliberate harm to innocent third parties.
Case: Bird v Holbrook (1828)
Facts: Spring gun set to protect tulips injured a trespasser.
Principle: Force disproportionate → private defence failed. Exam line: imminent threat + necessary/proportionate force + Bird v Holbrook.

5. Necessity

A lesser harm is caused to prevent a greater, imminent harm. Conditions: real & imminent danger + proportionate lesser harm + reasonable belief in necessity.
Case: Leigh v Gladstone (1909)
Facts: Hunger-striking prisoner force-fed by prison authorities.
Principle: Justified by necessity to preserve life/health.
Public NecessityPrivate Necessity
Protects the community (e.g. demolishing property to stop spreading fire) — generally a complete defenceProtects private interests — compensation for loss may still arise

6. Statutory Authority

Defence where act is authorised/required by statute and done within that authority, without negligence.
Absolute/MandatoryConditional/Permissive
Statute commands the act, no real choice — inevitable consequences protectedStatute permits but doesn't compel — reasonable care still required
Case: Vaughan v Taff Vale Railway Co. (1860)
Facts: Sparks from a statutorily authorised railway engine caused fire to plaintiff's woods.
Principle: Railway protected — authorised operation done without negligence; damage was an inevitable consequence. Limit: statutory authority never excuses negligence.

7. Inevitable Accident

Event that could not be foreseen/prevented by ordinary reasonable care. Unlike Act of God, need not be purely natural.
Case: Stanley v Powell (1891)
Facts: A shot glanced off a tree and accidentally injured another person.
Principle: Reasonable care could not have prevented it → inevitable accident.

Act of God vs Inevitable Accident

8. Mistake

Generally NOT a defence in tort. Interfering with another's legal right remains actionable even from an honest mistake of fact.

PART 5 — Vicarious Liability

CORE IDEALiability imposed on one person for another's tort due to a special legal relationship — chiefly master–servant/employer–employee. VCB = Valid relationship + Course of employment + Connection/benefit to master's business.
Respondeat Superior
tap to flip
"Let the master answer." Employer bears responsibility for the acts of the employee.
Qui facit per alium facit per se
tap to flip
"He who acts through another, acts himself." Basis for principal-agent liability.
Justifications: employer benefits from the work → bears the risk; employer controls the employee; employer usually has more resources to compensate; encourages responsible hiring/supervision.

Control Test — Master & Servant

Controls WHAT + HOWControls only result (WHAT)
Employee/servant relationshipIndependent contractor — generally outside vicarious liability

CaseLimpusJoel v Morrison
Case: Limpus v London General Omnibus Co. (1862)
Facts: Bus driver, forbidden to race, deliberately obstructed a rival bus causing an accident.
Principle: Employer liable — driver was still doing the authorised job (driving the bus), just in an unauthorised way.
Case: Joel v Morrison (1834)
Facts: Servant deviated from route to visit a friend, caused an accident.
Principle: Frolic of his own — master not liable.
Case: Beard v London General Omnibus Co. (1900)
Facts: Bus conductor (not authorised to drive) drove the bus on his own initiative and caused an accident.
Principle: Entirely outside his employment — employer not liable.
Case: State of Rajasthan v Mst. Vidhyawati (1962)
Facts: Government driver negligently knocked down a pedestrian while returning an official vehicle to the garage.
Principle: Supreme Court held the State vicariously liable — driver acting in course of official employment. Exam anchor: Vidhyawati = Indian State vicarious liability.
Case: Mersey Docks v Coggins & Griffith (1947) — Loaned Servant
Facts: Crane driver lent by the harbour board to another firm.
Principle: Original employer remained liable — it retained control over how the driver operated the crane. Rule: general employer stays liable unless the borrowing employer took full control.

Principal & Agent / Partners

Agent acting within scope of authority commits a tort → principal may be vicariously liable (qui facit per alium facit per se). E.g. sales agent fraudulently misrepresents goods while conducting principal's business. Partners may be jointly & severally liable for a torts committed by one partner in the ordinary course of firm business.

Independent Contractor

General rule: employer not vicariously liable for a contractor's torts (contractor controls the method, only the result is specified).
Exceptions where employer IS liable
  • Extra-hazardous / inherently dangerous work
  • Non-delegable duties imposed by law
  • Negligent selection of an incompetent/unqualified contractor (employer's own negligence)
  • Work affecting a public highway creating danger/obstruction
  • Strict liability situations — can't escape by appointing a contractor
Case: Honeywill & Stein v Larkin Bros (1934)
Facts: Contractor hired for flash photography in a cinema negligently caused a fire.
Principle: Example of extra-hazardous work — an exception to the general independent-contractor rule.

MOTOR VEHICLES ACT NOTEUnder the MV Act 1988, a vehicle owner may be vicariously liable for negligent driving by a person driving with the owner's consent even if not technically an employee (detailed in Unit V).
📌 VICARIOUS LIABILITY MAP: Master–Servant + course of employment → liable (Limpus) · frolic of own → not liable (Joel) · Principal–Agent within authority → liable · Partners, ordinary firm business → liable · State employee official course → liable (Vidhyawati) · Loaned servant → control decides (Mersey Docks) · Independent contractor → generally not liable, exceptions apply (Honeywill).

Common Exam Traps

❌ Volenti: never equate knowledge with consent.
❌ Act of God: human agency (e.g. riots) is not an Act of God.
❌ Private defence: proportionality is essential — don't skip it.
❌ Contributory negligence: don't write "no compensation" — modern law apportions damages.
❌ Course of employment: disobedience alone doesn't end liability if still doing the authorised job.
❌ Frolic of own: look for abandonment of employer's business for a personal purpose.
❌ Independent contractor: learn the exceptions — never write "employer is never liable".
❌ Statutory authority: statutory permission never excuses negligence.
