PART 10 — Torts Against the Person

Assault

Act causing reasonable apprehension of imminent battery — actual contact NOT required. Elements: act by defendant + reasonable apprehension + apprehension of imminent contact + present/apparent ability to carry it out.
Case: Tubervell v Savage (1669)
Facts: Hand placed on sword, but accompanying words showed no immediate intention to strike.
Principle: Words can negate an apparent assault.
Case: Stephens v Myers (1830)
Facts: Defendant advanced threateningly at a parish meeting but was stopped before reaching plaintiff.
Principle: Assault established — reasonable apprehension + apparent ability to carry it out.

Battery

Intentional + direct + unlawful application of force, without consent/justification. Actual injury not necessary — slight hostile touching suffices; ordinary incidental contact is not battery (implied consent).
Case: Cole v Turner (1704)
Principle: The least touching of another in anger may be battery; everyday incidental contact isn't, due to implied consent.
BasisAssaultBattery
MeaningReasonable apprehensionActual application of force
ContactNot requiredRequired
ExampleThreateningly raising a fistUnlawfully punching

Mayhem

Permanent disabling, disfiguring or dismembering injury — historically about reducing ability to defend oneself; modern sense = permanent physical disfigurement/disabling injury. Exam point: define + permanent/disabling character + distinguish from ordinary battery.

False Imprisonment

TOTAL + UNLAWFUL restraint of liberty. Actionable per se (no actual damage needed). Elements: total restraint + no lawful justification + against plaintiff's will + no minimum duration.
Case: Bird v Jones (1845)
Facts: Plaintiff stopped going one way on a footway but could return the way he came.
Principle: Restraint was NOT total → key authority for the total-restraint requirement.
Case: Meering v Grahame-White Aviation Co. (1919)
Principle: Awareness of restraint is not essential — total restraint existed even though plaintiff didn't know guards were posted outside; awareness may affect damages only.
Case: Bhim Singh v State of J&K (1985)
Principle: Wrongful arrest/detention by the State is both a tort and a constitutional wrong involving personal liberty (Art. 21).
📌 PART 10: Assault = apprehension, no contact needed (Stephens). Battery = intentional hostile touching (Cole). Mayhem = permanent disfigurement. False Imprisonment = TOTAL restraint (Bird v Jones); awareness not essential (Meering); constitutional angle (Bhim Singh).

PART 11 — Torts Against Reputation: Defamation

MEMORYDEFAMATION = Defamatory statement + Reference to plaintiff + Publication. Winfield: tends to lower a person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society, or make them shun/avoid that person.

3 Elements

1. Defamatory Statement — Sim v Stretch (1936)
Principle: Objective test — must tend to lower plaintiff's reputation among right-thinking ordinary people.
2. Reference to Plaintiff — Hulton v Jones (1910) / Knupffer v London Express (1944)
Hulton v Jones: Fictional character shared a real person's name — liability arose because reasonable readers understood it as referring to the real plaintiff.
Knupffer: In group defamation, the individual must be sufficiently identifiable — mere membership of a large group isn't enough.
3. Publication — Theaker v Richardson (1962)
Principle: Must be communicated to at least one person other than the plaintiff; publication established where a letter was read by another in reasonably foreseeable circumstances.

Innuendo

Case: Cassidy v Daily Mirror (1929)
Principle: Words innocent on their face may be defamatory by innuendo where people aware of surrounding facts understand a defamatory meaning referring to the plaintiff.

Defences: Justification, Fair Comment, Absolute Privilege (malice-proof), Qualified Privilege (malice defeats — Adam v Ward), Consent.

PART 12 — Torts Affecting Freedom

Malicious Prosecution — PRMFDA (6 elements)

Prosecution by defendant + absence of Reasonable/probable cause + Malice + Favourable termination + Damage + plaintiff was the Accused.
Case: Abrath v North Eastern Railway (1886)
Principle: Plaintiff bears the burden of proving absence of reasonable and probable cause.
Case: Brown v Hawkes (1891)
Principle: Honest belief in guilt, based on reasonable grounds, = reasonable & probable cause even if plaintiff is later acquitted.
Case: D.N. Banerjee v P.R. Mukherjee (1953)
Principle: Indian illustration — false complaint from personal enmity led to prosecution, damage, and establishment of all required elements.
Note: malice ≠ same as absence of reasonable cause (absence of cause is evidence of malice, not conclusive). Favourable termination = acquittal/discharge/discontinuance; conviction generally shows sufficient basis existed.

Malicious Civil Action

BasisMalicious ProsecutionMalicious Civil Action
ProceedingsCriminalCivil

Abuse of Legal Process

Using legal proceedings/machinery for an improper purpose other than what it exists for (e.g. arrest-warrant for collateral purpose, oppressive execution of judgment, extortion/harassment). 3 requirements: collateral/improper purpose + resulting damage + knowledge of the improper purpose.
📌 PART 12: PRMFDA for Malicious Prosecution. Malicious Civil Action = same idea, civil proceedings, stricter damage proof. Abuse of Process = improper purpose + damage + knowledge.

PART 13 — Domestic and Other Rights

Marital Rights

Enticement of spouse — wrongfully inducing a spouse to leave/desert (historically recognised, now abolished/limited in most jurisdictions). Harbouring a spouse — wrongfully sheltering a spouse away from the other, knowingly. Criminal conversation — historical husband's action against a third party for adultery; abolished in England/most jurisdictions (Indian position treated separately).

Parental Rights

Enticement of a child — inducing a child to leave parental custody (defendant persuaded child + child left custody + defendant's knowledge + loss of service/damage). Harbouring a child — sheltering a child known to belong in parental custody. Seduction of a child — historical action based on loss of services.

Rights to Service & Contractual Rights

Employer could sue a third party who wrongfully enticed/injured a servant, depriving the employer of services — Per Quod Servitium Amisit ("whereby he lost the service").
Case: Lumley v Gye (1853) — Inducing Breach of Contract
Principle: Leading case — a third party who, with knowledge and improper conduct, intentionally induces another to breach an existing contract, incurs liability.

Intimidation & Conspiracy

Case: Rookes v Barnard (1964) — Intimidation
Principle: Unlawful threat/coercive pressure causing another to act or refrain, resulting in recognised damage.
Case: Quinn v Leathem (1901) — Conspiracy
Principle: Predominant purpose test — if the predominant purpose of a combination is to injure the plaintiff, it's actionable; if predominantly to advance legitimate interests (injury incidental), it's not.
BasisIntimidationConspiracy
PartiesThreat may be by one personRequires 2+ persons combining
MethodUnlawful threatAgreement/combination
CaseRookes v BarnardQuinn v Leathem
📌 PART 13: Marital/Parental rights = enticement + harbouring (mostly historical/limited today). Lumley v Gye = inducing breach of contract. Rookes v Barnard = intimidation. Quinn v Leathem = conspiracy, predominant purpose test.

PART 14 — Torts Against Property

Trespass to Land

Direct + unjustifiable interference with land in another's possession (ownership not essential) — actionable per se, even with no actual damage. Elements: direct interference + plaintiff in possession + no lawful justification + intentional entry.
Forms: unauthorised entry, placing objects on land, remaining after licence revoked, abusing a licence, trespass below/above surface. Trespass ab initio: lawful entry that's later abused may be treated as trespass from the start.
Case: Entick v Carrington (1765)
Facts: Officials entered and searched plaintiff's home under a general warrant, seizing papers.
Principle: Even a very small unauthorised invasion of private property is trespass.
BasisTrespass to LandNuisance
InterferenceDirect physical entryGenerally indirect
ActionabilityPer seDepends on type/requirements
DurationMay be single actOften continuing/recurrent

Trespass to Goods, Conversion & Detinue — T-C-D

Trespass to Goods: direct & intentional interference with goods in another's possession (scratching a vehicle, moving/using goods without permission) — actionable per se.
Conversion: intentional dealing inconsistent with owner's rights, treating goods as one's own (stealing, selling, destroying, wrongful refusal to return) — plaintiff may claim value of goods.
Detinue: wrongful detention — refusal to return goods after a demand.
BasisConversionDetinue
Core wrongDealing inconsistently with ownershipWrongful detention/refusal to return
NatureMore active interferenceMore passive refusal
RemedyValue of goodsReturn of goods or value + damages
📌 PART 14: Trespass to Land = direct + possession + per se (Entick v Carrington). T-C-D → Trespass to Goods (direct interference) → Conversion (treating as own) → Detinue (refusal after demand).
