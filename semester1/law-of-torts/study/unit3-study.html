PART 6 — Negligence

CORENegligence = breach of a legal duty to take care, resulting in damage. Winfield: breach of legal duty to take care → damage to plaintiff. Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks (1856): omitting what a reasonable person would do, or doing what one would not.

3 Essential Elements — proof flow

Duty of care→Breach→Factual causation→Remoteness→Extent (thin skull)
Case: Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) — Duty / Neighbour Principle
Facts: Mrs. Donoghue drank ginger beer from an opaque bottle allegedly containing a decomposed snail, fell ill.
Principle: Established the neighbour principle — take reasonable care to avoid acts/omissions likely to injure persons closely & directly affected.
Case: Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co. (1856) — Breach / Reasonable Person
Facts: Damage occurred during an exceptionally severe frost.
Principle: Negligence = failing the reasonable-person standard in the circumstances; an accident alone doesn't prove negligence.
Case: Barnett v Chelsea Hospital (1969) — But-For Causation
Facts: Patient who'd ingested arsenic died; hospital allegedly failed to treat him promptly.
Principle: Not liable — patient would have died even with proper treatment ("but for" test not satisfied).
Case: Wagon Mound No. 1 (1961) — Remoteness
Facts: Oil escaped into Sydney Harbour, later caught fire and caused damage.
Principle: Modern test — liable only for damage of a reasonably foreseeable type; too-remote damage is not recoverable. (Re Polemis 1921 = older direct-consequence approach, now superseded.)
Case: Smith v Leech Brain (1962) — Thin Skull Rule
Facts: A burn led to a far more serious consequence due to the victim's unusual condition.
Principle: "Take the victim as you find him" — liable for full extent if the type of harm was foreseeable, even if its severity wasn't.

Medical Negligence

Case: Bolam v Friern Hospital (1957) — Bolam Test
Principle: A doctor is not negligent if acting in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion.
Case: IMA v V.P. Shantha (1995) — Medical Services & Consumer Law
Principle: Medical services can fall within "services" under the Consumer Protection Act, 1986 — deficiency claims possible alongside tort remedies.

Contributory Negligence (recap)

Plaintiff's own lack of reasonable care contributing to the damage. Differs from volenti (voluntary risk acceptance); modern law apportions damages rather than a complete bar.
📌 NEGLIGENCE = D+B+D → Duty (Donoghue) + Breach (Blyth) + Damage, via Causation (Barnett) + Remoteness (Wagon Mound) + Thin Skull (Smith v Leech Brain). Medical → Bolam; consumer context → IMA v Shantha.

PART 7 — Nuisance

COREUnlawful interference with rights connected with land — Public Nuisance (affects community) vs Private Nuisance (unreasonable interference with one person's land use/enjoyment).

Public Nuisance

Affects the community/class of public (health, safety, comfort, public rights). A private individual generally can't sue as "just one of the public" — needs special damage beyond general inconvenience.
Case: Winterbottom v Lord Derby (1867)
Facts: Defendant obstructed a public footpath; an individual sued personally.
Principle: Could not sue as a mere member of the public without special damage.
Case: Rose v Miles (1815)
Facts: A barge blocked a public navigable waterway, forcing plaintiff to incur extra expense.
Principle: Plaintiff could sue — he suffered special damage beyond general public inconvenience.

Private Nuisance

Forms: encroachment (roots/branches), physical damage (flooding), interference with enjoyment (noise, smell, smoke, vibration, heat). Features: unreasonable interference + with use/enjoyment of land + usually continuous/recurring + substantial.

Reasonableness Factors — flip cards

Locality / character
tap
Sturges v Bridgman (1879): doctor set up near a confectioner's noisy machinery. Coming to an existing nuisance is NOT itself a defence; reasonableness depends on locality/circumstances.
Abnormal sensitivity
tap
Robinson v Kilvert (1889): heat affected unusually sensitive brown paper. Claim failed — abnormally sensitive use isn't protected if ordinary use wouldn't be substantially affected.
Malice
tap
Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett (1936): defendant deliberately disturbed a fox farm to harm business. Malice made interference unreasonable — liability followed.
Other factors
tap
Duration & frequency, severity/intensity, social utility of the defendant's activity — all weighed together.

PART 8 — Strict & Absolute Liability

Rylands v Fletcher — Strict Liability

One who brings/keeps a dangerous thing on land, making non-natural use, keeps it at his peril; if it escapes and causes damage, liable — subject to exceptions.
Case: Rylands v Fletcher (1868)
Facts: Defendant built a reservoir; water escaped and flooded plaintiff's mine.
Principle: Liability imposed despite no ordinary negligence — non-natural use + escape of a dangerous thing.

Non-Natural Use & Escape

Non-natural use = beyond ordinary use, creating special/increased risk (e.g. large reservoirs, explosives, high-voltage electricity). Escape = the thing must move from defendant's land/control to outside it and cause damage — a key traditional requirement contrasted with absolute liability.

Exceptions to Strict Liability

📌 Memory: God + Stranger + Consent + Statutory Authority + Plaintiff's fault — Act of God, Act of Stranger, Plaintiff's own fault, Consent/common benefit, Statutory authority.

Absolute Liability — M.C. Mehta

Indian doctrine for hazardous/inherently dangerous industries — a higher standard than Rylands.
Case: M.C. Mehta v Union of India (1987)
Facts: Shriram Foods & Fertilizers' hazardous chemical plant in Delhi leaked oleum gas, causing harm.
Principle: SC developed absolute liability — hazardous enterprise owes an absolute, non-delegable duty to the community; if harm results, absolutely liable to compensate, no Rylands-type exceptions. Compensation should be proportionate to the enterprise's size, capacity and profitability.

PART 9 — Legal Remedies, Damages & Remoteness

PURPOSERestore the plaintiff, so far as possible, to the position before the wrong. Judicial remedies: Damages · Injunction · Specific Restitution.

Types of Damages — C.N.O.A.E.

TypeMeaning
ContemptuousSmallest possible award — court disapproves of the claim/conduct though plaintiff technically succeeds
NominalSmall award for right violated without substantial actual loss
Ordinary/CompensatoryMain monetary remedy — compensates and restores
AggravatedExtra compensatory damages where conduct worsened injury to dignity/feelings/reputation
Exemplary/PunitiveBeyond compensation — punishes & deters
Special damages = specifically quantifiable/proved (medical bills, lost earnings, repairs). General damages = not precisely calculable (pain & suffering, loss of amenity, mental anguish).

Injunction

Temporary / Permanent
tap
Temporary (interlocutory) — during the suit. Permanent (perpetual) — final order.
Prohibitory / Mandatory
tap
Prohibitory — stop doing something. Mandatory — do something (e.g. remove an obstruction).
Case: Radhey Shyam v Gur Prasad (1978)
Facts: Continuing interference from a flour mill in a residential area.
Principle: Permanent injunction granted — continuing nuisance may justify injunction where damages alone are inadequate.

Specific Restitution & Extra-Judicial Remedies

Specific restitution: court orders return of wrongfully taken land/goods, not just their money value.
Extra-Judicial RemedyMeaning
Expulsion of trespasserReasonable force to remove a trespasser
Re-entry on landPeaceably retaking possession, within legal limits
Abatement of nuisanceRemoving the source (e.g. overhanging branches) after appropriate notice
Distress damage feasantRetaining goods/animals causing damage until compensated, subject to legal limits
CautionExtra-judicial remedies must be exercised without breach of peace and with reasonable force — excess force creates liability.

Remoteness — Final Revision

Factual: But-for (Barnett)→Legal: foreseeable TYPE (Wagon Mound)→Thin skull (Smith v Leech Brain)
📌 DAMAGES = C.N.O.A.E. Injunction = Temporary/Permanent + Prohibitory/Mandatory. Remoteness: But-for → Wagon Mound (type) → Thin Skull (extent). Re Polemis = old direct-consequence rule, superseded by Wagon Mound.
