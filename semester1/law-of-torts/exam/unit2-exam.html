MQ1. Enumerate and examine the general defences available in an action for Tort.
📌 PYQ: 2010, 2011, 2014–2016, 2018–2020, 2021, 2023, 2024, 2026🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark

Introduction

General defences are circumstances recognised by law in which conduct that would otherwise attract tortious liability is excused or justified, so the defendant escapes liability (wholly, or — for contributory negligence — partly, through apportionment) even though the plaintiff has technically proved the tort.

Essential Points — Enumerate All 9, Explain the Major Ones

  • Volenti non fit injuria — plaintiff freely and knowingly accepted the risk. Hall v Brooklands Auto Racing Club (1933) — spectator injured when a car entered the enclosure; held to have accepted the ordinary risks of a motor race.
  • Contributory Negligence — plaintiff's own carelessness contributed to the damage; modern law apportions damages rather than barring the claim entirely. Davies v Mann (1842) — Last Opportunity Rule: whoever had the last clear chance to avoid the accident bears liability.
  • Inevitable Accident — unforeseeable despite reasonable care. Stanley v Powell (1891).
  • Act of God — extraordinary natural force. Nichols v Marsland (1876).
  • Private Defence — reasonable/proportionate force to protect person or property. Bird v Holbrook (1828).
  • Necessity — lesser harm to prevent a greater one. Cope v Sharpe (1912).
  • Statutory Authority — act authorised by statute, done without negligence. Vaughan v Taff Vale Railway Co. (1860).
  • Mistake — generally NOT a defence in tort.

Case Law

Use one case per defence as listed above — do not attempt full facts for all 7 in a single answer; name them and give one line each, then reserve full Facts–Decision–Principle treatment for whichever specific defence the question separately asks about (see MQ2, MQ6, MQ7 below).

Example / Application

If asked to "enumerate," a bullet list with one case-name per defence is sufficient; if asked to "enumerate and examine," add the one-line principle after each case as shown above.

Conclusion

Whether a particular defence succeeds always depends on whether its specific legal requirements are met on the facts — a defence only partially satisfied, or exceeded in scope, will fail, as Bird v Holbrook itself illustrates.

Answer Plan (16 mark)

  1. Introduction — meaning of general defences
  2. List all defences
  3. One paragraph + one case per major defence
  4. Conclusion on fact-specific application

MQ2. Explain Volenti non fit injuria with its exceptions and decided cases. (Also covers: Rescue cases / liability arising from rescue situations.)
📌 PYQ: 2012, 2015, 2017, 2019, 2021, 2022, 2025, 2026 (+ Rescue: 2011, 2017, 2026)🎯 Type: 10 / 15 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Volenti non fit injuria — explain with exceptions/decided cases." · "Rescue cases / liability arising from rescue situations" — rescue is one of volenti's own recognised exceptions, not a separate doctrine.

Introduction

Volenti non fit injuria means "to one who is willing, no injury is done." A person who freely and knowingly accepts a risk cannot later complain in tort of injury from that risk.

Definition / Meaning

Where a plaintiff, with full knowledge of a risk, freely and voluntarily agrees to accept it, he cannot recover for harm arising from that risk. It is a complete defence where established.

Essential Points

  • Knowledge of the nature and extent of the risk.
  • Free and voluntary consent — not obtained by fraud, compulsion or economic pressure.
  • Scope — the injury must fall within the risk actually consented to.
  • Knowledge ≠ Consent — the most important exam point: mere knowledge of danger is not, by itself, sufficient; the defendant must show genuine voluntary acceptance.

Exceptions

  • Consent obtained by fraud, compulsion or coercion is not free consent.
  • Rescue cases — the defence does not normally apply where the plaintiff is injured rescuing someone endangered by the defendant's own wrongdoing.
  • If the defendant's conduct exceeds the scope of what was consented to, the defence doesn't extend to the additional risk.
  • Knowledge without genuine freedom of choice (e.g. economic compulsion) may defeat the defence even where knowledge is proved.

Case Law

Hall v Brooklands Auto Racing Club (1933)Facts: A spectator at a motor race was injured when two cars collided and one was flung into the enclosure.
Decision: Defendant not liable.
Principle: Attending a race means voluntarily accepting its ordinary inherent risks.
Smith v Baker (1891)Facts: A quarry worker, aware a crane regularly swung rocks overhead and had complained about it, continued working and was injured by a falling stone.
Decision: Employer held liable; volenti failed.
Principle: Mere knowledge of danger is not the same as free, voluntary acceptance of the risk — the leading authority for "knowledge is not consent."
Haynes v Harwood (1935)Facts: The defendant's servant left horses unattended in a crowded street; they bolted, and a police constable was injured stopping them to protect bystanders.
Decision: Defendant liable; volenti did not apply.
Principle: A rescuer who knowingly faces danger to save others endangered by the defendant's negligence has not "voluntarily accepted" that negligence — the leading rescue-exception case.

Example / Application

A passenger who merely knows his driver has been drinking has not automatically consented to the risk of the driver's subsequent negligence — knowledge of intoxication is a factor, but volenti still requires proof of genuine voluntary acceptance (see Problem 1 below).

Conclusion

Volenti is a complete defence only where genuine, free, informed consent to the specific risk is proved — knowledge alone (Smith v Baker) and rescue situations (Haynes v Harwood) are the two most-tested limits on the defence.
🔴 MUST STUDY
MQ3. What is Vicarious Liability? Explain its meaning and basis, including the principle of Respondeat Superior.
📌 PYQ: 2011–2026, recurring🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark

Introduction

Vicarious liability means liability imposed by law on one person for a tort actually committed by another, arising out of a recognised legal relationship between them — most commonly, the liability of a master/employer for a servant's/employee's tort.

Definition / Meaning

Respondeat superior — "let the master answer" — the master bears responsibility for the servant's torts. Qui facit per alium facit per se — "he who acts through another, acts himself" — the conceptual basis extending liability to the person on whose behalf the act was done.

Essential Points — Basis / Justification

  • The employer benefits economically from the employee's work and should bear the corresponding risk.
  • The employer exercises control over how the work is performed.
  • The employer is generally better placed, financially and through insurance, to bear and spread the cost.
  • The doctrine encourages careful selection, training and supervision of staff.

Case Law

No separate case is needed purely for "meaning and basis" — use Limpus v London General Omnibus Co. (full facts under MQ4) if an illustration is required, since that case demonstrates the doctrine in action.

Example / Application

If a delivery company's driver negligently injures a pedestrian while making deliveries, the company (not just the driver) is liable — because it benefits from and controls the delivery work.

Conclusion

Vicarious liability rests on the twin justifications of control and enterprise risk-bearing, expressed through respondeat superior and qui facit per alium facit per se — but liability only attaches once the specific conditions in MQ4 are satisfied.

MQ4. Explain the liability of a Master for torts committed by his Servant, including the doctrine of Course of Employment. (Also covers: circumstances in which an employer is liable for an employee's acts.)
📌 PYQ: 2011–2026 recurring (Master's liability) + 2021 and later (circumstances of employer liability) + Course/Scope of Employment: recurring🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Explain liability of Master for torts committed by Servant." · "Explain circumstances in which an employer is liable for an employee's acts." · "Explain Course/Scope of Employment." All three ask for the same three-condition test, applied through the same three leading cases.

Introduction

A master is not automatically liable for every tort committed by his servant — liability depends on three specific conditions being satisfied, the most heavily tested of which is "course of employment."

Essential Points — 3 Conditions for Master's Liability

  • A master–servant (employer–employee) relationship must exist.
  • The servant must have committed a tort.
  • The tort must have been committed in the course of employment.

Explanation — Course of Employment

An employer remains liable where the servant performs an authorised task in a wrongful, negligent or even expressly prohibited manner — the test is whether the servant was still, in substance, doing the employer's business, or had wholly abandoned it for a purely personal purpose (a "frolic of his own," where the employer is not liable).

Case Law

Limpus v London General Omnibus Co. (1862)Facts: A bus driver, expressly forbidden to race rival buses, deliberately raced and obstructed one, causing a collision.
Decision: Employer held liable.
Principle: The driver was still doing his authorised job (driving the bus), merely in an unauthorised way — this remains within the course of employment.
Joel v Morrison (1834)Facts: A carter deviated substantially from his route to visit a friend for personal reasons, and negligently caused an accident during the detour.
Decision: Master not liable.
Principle: The servant had abandoned the master's business entirely for a personal purpose — a "frolic of his own," outside the course of employment.
Beard v London General Omnibus Co. (1900)Facts: A conductor, with no authority whatsoever to drive, drove the bus himself at the end of a journey and caused an accident.
Decision: Employer not liable.
Principle: Driving was wholly outside the conductor's job — not merely an unauthorised way of doing his own job (contrast with Limpus).

Example / Application

A shop security guard who assaults a customer while trying (wrongly) to prevent a suspected theft is still doing his job in an improper manner (Limpus-type); a guard who leaves his post to settle a personal score in the parking lot has abandoned his job (Joel-type).

Conclusion

The master is liable wherever the servant, even while disobeying instructions, remains engaged in the employer's business (Limpus) — but not where the servant has wholly abandoned it for a personal purpose (Joel v Morrison) or acted entirely outside his job's scope (Beard).

MQ5. How is the Master–Servant relationship determined? Distinguish a Servant/Employee from an Independent Contractor, and state the exceptions where the employer may still be liable for a Contractor's tort.
📌 PYQ: 2012, 2014, 2019 and later (tests for relationship) + 2012 and later (servant vs independent contractor)🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Explain Master–Servant relationship and the tests for determining it." · "Distinguish Servant/Employee from Independent Contractor." One control-test explanation naturally leads into the distinction and its consequences.

Introduction

Not every person who works for another is a "servant" for vicarious-liability purposes — the law distinguishes a servant from an independent contractor primarily through the control test.

Definition / Meaning — The Control Test

A servant is subject to the employer's control not only as to what work is done but also as to how it is done. An independent contractor undertakes to produce a specified result, while retaining control over the manner and method of achieving it.

Essential Points

  • Supporting indicators (where control alone is inconclusive): degree of integration into the organisation; who supplies tools/equipment; method of payment (wages vs lump sum); working independently or as part of the business.
  • Why it matters: an employer is generally vicariously liable for a servant's tort in the course of employment, but generally not liable for an independent contractor's tort, since the contractor controls the manner of work.
  • Exceptions — the employer IS liable for a contractor's tort where: the work is extra-hazardous/inherently dangerous; a non-delegable duty is imposed by law; the contractor was negligently selected; the work creates danger/obstruction on a public highway; or a strict-liability situation applies.

Case Law

Honeywill and Stein Ltd v Larkin Bros Ltd (1934)Facts: A contractor engaged to take flash photographs in a cinema negligently caused a fire.
Decision: The employer who engaged the contractor was held liable.
Principle: Flash photography using magnesium powder was an extra-hazardous activity — where work is extra-hazardous, the employer cannot escape liability merely by delegating it to a contractor.

Example / Application

A company that hires a driver and directs both his delivery route AND exactly how to drive is an employer of a servant; a company that hires a plumber to "fix the leak" (result specified, method left to the plumber) has engaged an independent contractor.

Conclusion

The control test remains the primary tool for distinguishing servant from independent contractor, but the general rule of no liability for a contractor's torts yields to well-defined exceptions — most importantly, extra-hazardous work, as Honeywill & Stein shows.

MQ6. Distinguish Act of God from Inevitable Accident, with decided cases.
📌 PYQ: Act of God: 2012,13,17,19,22 · Inevitable Accident: 2012,13,18,22🎯 Type: 10 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Explain Inevitable Accident with decided cases." · "Explain Act of God/Vis Major with decided cases." · "Distinguish Act of God from Inevitable Accident." These two defences share overlapping PYQ years (2012,13,22) and are most often tested as a comparison — one answer, structured as below, covers all three question forms.

Introduction

Both defences concern accidents that could not reasonably have been prevented, but they differ sharply in the nature of the cause.

Definition / Meaning

Act of God (Vis Major): an extraordinary natural event which no human foresight could reasonably anticipate and no human care could guard against — the cause must be purely natural, with no human agency involved.
Inevitable Accident: an event that, despite the defendant's reasonable care, could not have been foreseen or avoided — the cause need not be purely natural; it may involve human or mechanical elements.

Case Law

Nichols v Marsland (1876)Facts: Exceptionally heavy, unprecedented rainfall burst the defendant's artificial lakes, flooding the plaintiff's land and sweeping away bridges.
Decision: Defendant not liable.
Principle: The rainfall was so extraordinary it amounted to an Act of God.
Stanley v Powell (1891)Facts: During a pheasant shoot, the defendant fired properly, but the shot glanced off a tree at an unusual angle and injured the plaintiff.
Decision: Defendant not liable.
Principle: The injury was an inevitable accident — reasonable care was taken and the deflection was wholly unforeseeable.

Essential Points — Key Distinction

BasisAct of GodInevitable Accident
CausePurely natural, extraordinaryMay involve human/mechanical elements
Human agencyNoneMay be partly involved
TestExtraordinary + unforeseeable + unavoidable by any human careReasonable care could not have prevented it

Conclusion

Act of God requires the cause to be exclusively natural and extraordinary (Nichols v Marsland); Inevitable Accident is the broader category, where the defining feature is simply that reasonable care could not have prevented the harm (Stanley v Powell).
🟠 HIGH PRIORITY
MQ7. Explain Private Defence, Necessity and Statutory Authority as general defences.
📌 PYQ: Private Defence: 2013,18,26 · Statutory Authority: 2013,21,26 · Necessity: syllabus/question-bank theme🎯 Type: 10 mark (or as separate 5/6-mark short notes if asked individually)
💡 These three share overlapping PYQ years (2013, 2026) and are frequently asked together as one combined question — but each sub-answer below is also written to stand alone if only one defence is asked.

1. Private Defence

Meaning: reasonable and proportionate force may be used to protect person or property from unlawful harm; force must not be excessive, cannot be retaliation for a past wrong, and cannot deliberately harm an innocent third party.

Bird v Holbrook (1828)Facts: The defendant set a hidden spring-gun without warning to protect tulips in his garden; a trespasser retrieving a stray bird was seriously injured.
Decision: Defendant liable.
Principle: The force used was excessive and disproportionate to a mere trespass — private defence does not justify unwarned, indiscriminate force.

2. Necessity

Meaning: an otherwise wrongful act may be justified where reasonably necessary to prevent a greater harm; the danger must be real/imminent and the harm caused proportionate to the harm avoided.

Cope v Sharpe (1912)Facts: A gamekeeper entered the plaintiff's land without permission and beat out a fire to stop it spreading and destroying nesting game birds.
Decision: Defendant not liable for trespass.
Principle: His entry was justified by necessity — real, imminent danger of greater harm.

3. Statutory Authority

Meaning: where an act is authorised, expressly or impliedly, by statute, liability is excluded for harm that is a necessarily incidental consequence of properly carrying out that authority.

Vaughan v Taff Vale Railway Co. (1860)Facts: Sparks from a properly maintained, statutorily authorised railway engine set fire to the plaintiff's adjoining woods.
Decision: Defendant not liable.
Principle: The fire was an inevitable incidental consequence of a properly conducted authorised activity — but this defence never excuses negligence.

Example / Application

A shopkeeper who uses reasonable force to stop a shoplifter has a private-defence justification; a person who breaks a neighbour's window to escape a fire has a necessity justification; a properly run factory authorised by statute to emit some smoke is protected by statutory authority provided it operates without negligence.

Conclusion

All three defences share the same underlying logic — the defendant's conduct is excused only to the extent it was reasonable, proportionate, and within the scope of what the law actually permits; exceeding that scope (Bird v Holbrook) defeats the defence.

MQ8. Is Mistake a defence in tort? Explain.
📌 PYQ: Question-bank theme (no specific years listed)🎯 Type: 5 / 6-mark short note

Definition / Meaning

Mistake — whether of law or of fact — is generally not a defence in tort. A person who interferes with another's legal rights remains liable even if the interference resulted from an honest and reasonable mistake of fact.

Example / Application

If a person honestly but mistakenly believes goods are his own and sells them, he is still liable in conversion to the true owner — his honest mistake does not excuse the interference.

Conclusion

Mistake affords no general defence to tortious liability, though it may occasionally be relevant to specific torts, such as establishing "reasonable and probable cause" in malicious prosecution (a Unit IV topic).
🟡 SUPPORTING
MQ9. Discuss the liability of the State for torts committed by its servants.
📌 PYQ: 2017, 2018🎯 Type: 10 mark
💡 Uses the same 3-condition test as MQ4 — don't re-derive it, just apply it to the State as employer.

Introduction

State liability asks whether the government can be held vicariously liable, like any private employer, for a tort committed by its servant in the course of employment.

Essential Points

Historically, Indian law distinguished sovereign functions (defence, law and order, administration of justice — traditionally immune) from non-sovereign/commercial functions (State liable exactly like a private employer). This distinction has been progressively narrowed by later case law.

Case Law

State of Rajasthan v Mst. Vidhyawati (1962)Facts: A government driver, returning a serviced jeep to the collector's residence, negligently knocked down and killed a pedestrian.
Decision: Supreme Court held the State vicariously liable.
Principle: The State should be treated like any other employer for torts committed by servants during ordinary (non-sovereign) employment activities.

Conclusion

Where a government servant commits a tort in the course of employment while performing a non-sovereign function, the State is generally vicariously liable on the same principles as any private employer, as Vidhyawati establishes.
🟡 SUPPORTING
MQ10. Explain the doctrine of Common Employment.
📌 PYQ: 2011🎯 Type: 5 / 6-mark short note

Definition / Meaning

The doctrine of common employment was an old common-law rule under which an employer was not liable to a servant for injuries caused by the negligence of a fellow-servant engaged in the same ("common") employment — the injured servant was left to sue the negligent fellow-servant personally, who was usually unable to pay meaningful compensation.

Essential Points

  • Originated in English case law (traced to Priestley v Fowler, 1837) as a limitation on an employer's vicarious liability toward his own workforce.
  • The doctrine was widely criticised as unfair to injured workers and was progressively abolished or heavily restricted by later statutory reform and workmen's-compensation-style legislation.

Conclusion

Common employment is now largely a historical doctrine rather than live law — know its meaning and rationale for a short note, but do not expect to apply it as current law.

Case / Problem Application Questions (5)

Limited set, matched to the highest-yield principles above.
Problem 1 — Volenti, drunk driver: A passenger knowingly accepts a ride from a driver who has been drinking; the driver later causes an accident. Does volenti apply?
Principle: Knowledge ≠ consent (Smith v Baker).
Answer: Not automatically — mere knowledge that the driver had been drinking doesn't by itself prove free, voluntary acceptance of the risk of his subsequent negligence; much depends on how obvious/extreme the intoxication was.
Problem 2 — Rescue: A person sees another endangered by the defendant's negligence and is injured attempting a rescue.
Principle: Rescue exception to volenti (Haynes v Harwood).
Answer: The defendant remains liable — a rescuer's knowledge of danger does not amount to voluntary acceptance of the defendant's negligence.
Problem 3 — Servant acting wrongly: A bus driver, forbidden to race, races a rival bus during his normal duties and causes an accident.
Principle: Course of employment (Limpus v London General Omnibus Co.).
Answer: Employer remains liable — the driver was still doing his authorised job, merely in an unauthorised way.
Problem 4 — Frolic of own: A servant abandons his employer's business for a personal detour and causes an accident during it.
Principle: Frolic of his own (Joel v Morrison).
Answer: Employer not liable — the servant had wholly abandoned the employer's business for a personal purpose.
Problem 5 — Independent contractor, extra-hazardous work: An employer hires a contractor for unusually dangerous work; the contractor's negligence causes injury.
Principle: Extra-hazardous work exception (Honeywill & Stein v Larkin Bros).
Answer: The employer remains liable — the general "no liability for a contractor's torts" rule doesn't apply to extra-hazardous activities.

🎯 Common Exam Traps

⚠️ Volenti vs Contributory Negligence — volenti requires actual consent to the specific risk (complete defence); contributory negligence is just a failure to take reasonable care for oneself (apportionment, not a complete bar).
⚠️ Act of God vs Inevitable Accident — Act of God demands a purely natural, extraordinary cause; Inevitable Accident is broader and can involve human/mechanical elements.
⚠️ Employee vs Independent Contractor — the test is control over the MANNER of work (WHAT + HOW = employee; result only = contractor), not just who pays whom.
⚠️ Course of Employment vs Frolic of Own — doing the authorised job badly/against instructions (Limpus) is still within employment; abandoning the job entirely for a personal purpose (Joel v Morrison) is not.
⚠️ General Rule vs Exceptions — don't write "no liability for independent contractors" and stop; the exceptions (extra-hazardous work, non-delegable duties, negligent selection, highway work, strict liability) are exactly what examiners test.
