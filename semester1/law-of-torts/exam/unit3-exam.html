MQ1. Define Negligence and explain its essential ingredients, with reference to Duty of Care / Donoghue v Stevenson.
📌 PYQ: 2013–2025 recurring (essentials) + 2013, 2018 and later (Duty of Care/Donoghue)🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Define Negligence and explain its essential ingredients/nature." · "Explain Duty of Care with reference to Donoghue v Stevenson." Duty of care is the first essential element — going deep on Donoghue answers both question forms.

Introduction

Negligence is the breach of a legal duty to take reasonable care, resulting in damage to the plaintiff — one of the central and most frequently examined torts.

Definition / Meaning

Winfield: Breach of a legal duty to take care, resulting in damage to the plaintiff. Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks (1856): Negligence is omitting to do what a reasonable person would do, or doing what a reasonable and prudent person would not do.

Essential Points — 3 Elements

  • Duty of care — the defendant must owe the plaintiff a legal duty to take reasonable care.
  • Breach of duty — the defendant's conduct falls below the standard of a reasonable, prudent person.
  • Damage — the breach must actually cause legally recoverable damage, not too remote (see MQ2).

Case Law

Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) — Duty / Neighbour PrincipleFacts: Mrs. Donoghue drank ginger beer from an opaque bottle allegedly containing a decomposed snail and fell ill.
Decision: Established the modern neighbour principle.
Principle: A person must take reasonable care to avoid acts/omissions likely to injure persons so closely and directly affected that they ought reasonably to be in contemplation.
Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co. (1856) — BreachFacts: Damage occurred to waterworks equipment during an exceptionally severe frost.
Decision: Explained negligence through the reasonable-person standard.
Principle: Negligence is failing the standard of care a reasonable person would exercise in the circumstances — an accident alone does not prove negligence.

Example / Application

A shopkeeper who leaves a wet floor unmarked owes a duty of care to customers (Donoghue's neighbour principle); if he fails to put up a warning sign that a reasonable shopkeeper would use, that is the breach (Blyth).

Conclusion

A strong negligence answer connects duty (Donoghue) → breach (Blyth) → causation and remoteness (MQ2) — each stage should be supported by its leading case.

  2. Duty of care + Donoghue in full
  3. Breach + Blyth in full
  4. Damage — brief link to MQ2
  5. Conclusion
🔴 MUST STUDY
MQ2. Explain Remoteness of Damage and reasonable foreseeability, with special reference to Re Polemis and Wagon Mound.
📌 PYQ: 2012–2017, 2019, 2022, 2025, 2026 (remoteness) + Recurring (Re Polemis/Wagon Mound)🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark

Introduction

Remoteness of damage determines which consequences of a tortious act are legally recoverable — not every consequence, however directly caused, is compensable.

Definition / Meaning

The defendant is generally liable only for damage of a reasonably foreseeable type; damage that is too remote is not recoverable.

Essential Points

  • Re Polemis (1921) — the older approach: the defendant was liable for all direct consequences of the negligent act, even if the precise kind of damage was not foreseeable.
  • Wagon Mound No. 1 (1961) — the modern approach: liability only for damage of a reasonably foreseeable type.
  • Thin skull rule — once the type of injury is foreseeable, the defendant must take the victim as found and is liable for the full extent, even if unusually severe.

Case Law

The Wagon Mound No. 1 (1961)Facts: Oil escaped from the defendant's ship into Sydney Harbour and later caught fire, causing damage to the plaintiff's wharf.
Decision: Liability limited by reasonable foreseeability of the type of damage.
Principle: A defendant is liable only for damage of a reasonably foreseeable type — superseded the Re Polemis direct-consequence approach.
Smith v Leech Brain & Co. (1962) — Thin Skull RuleFacts: A burn from the defendant's negligence led to a far more serious consequence due to the claimant's unusual pre-existing condition.
Decision: Defendant remained liable for the full extent.
Principle: Foreseeability concerns the type of harm, not its precise extent — "take your victim as you find him."

Example / Application

If a defendant's negligence causes a minor foreseeable burn, but the victim's rare medical condition turns it into a fatal injury, the defendant is liable for the full outcome under the thin skull rule, even though the severity itself wasn't foreseeable.

Conclusion

Re Polemis represents the older direct-consequence approach, now superseded by Wagon Mound's foreseeability test; the thin skull rule deals separately with the unforeseen severity of an otherwise foreseeable type of harm.

MQ3. Explain the doctrine of Res Ipsa Loquitur with decided cases.
📌 PYQ: 2012, 2013, 2018, 2019🎯 Type: 10 mark, or 5/6-mark short note
➕ ADDED — this topic was not present in your uploaded Unit III material at all, despite recurring PYQ evidence across 4 years. The case law below (Byrne v Boadle, Scott v London & St Katherine Docks) is standard, well-established doctrine for this exact principle, not invented for this answer.

Introduction

Res ipsa loquitur ("the thing speaks for itself") is an evidentiary principle in negligence that allows a court to infer negligence from the very nature of the accident, where the plaintiff cannot otherwise prove exactly how the defendant was careless.

Definition / Meaning

Where an accident occurs that would not ordinarily happen without negligence, and the thing causing it was under the defendant's exclusive control, the burden shifts to the defendant to explain how it occurred without negligence on his part.

Essential Points — 3 Conditions

  • The thing/instrumentality causing the damage was under the exclusive control of the defendant (or his servants).
  • The accident is of a kind that would not normally happen without negligence.
  • There is no other explanation available for how the accident occurred.

Case Law

Byrne v Boadle (1863)Facts: A barrel of flour fell from an upper window of the defendant's warehouse and struck a passer-by on the street below; the defendant offered no explanation.
Decision: Held liable — negligence was inferred.
Principle: Barrels do not ordinarily fall out of warehouse windows without negligence; since the warehouse was in the defendant's exclusive control, the burden shifted to him to explain the absence of negligence.
Scott v London & St Katherine Docks Co. (1865)Facts: Bags of sugar fell from a crane operated by the defendant's employees and injured the plaintiff, a customs officer passing below.
Decision: Held liable.
Principle: Confirmed the three-part test — exclusive control, accident not ordinarily occurring without negligence, and no other explanation available.

Example / Application

A surgical instrument left inside a patient after an operation is a classic res ipsa loquitur scenario — operating theatres are under the hospital's exclusive control, and instruments are not ordinarily left inside patients without someone's negligence.

Conclusion

Res ipsa loquitur does not create strict liability — it merely permits an inference of negligence, shifting the evidential burden to the defendant to show he was not negligent, as Byrne v Boadle and Scott v London & St Katherine Docks Co. both illustrate.

MQ4. Explain the Rule in Rylands v Fletcher, its essential elements, and exceptions.
📌 PYQ: Core recurring (strict liability) + Core recurring (exceptions)🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Explain Strict Liability under Rylands v Fletcher." · "Explain exceptions to the Rule in Rylands v Fletcher." · "State the rule in Rylands v Fletcher." · "What is non-natural use of land?"

Introduction

The rule in Rylands v Fletcher introduced strict liability in English tort law for damage caused by the escape of dangerous things accumulated on land, independent of proof of negligence.

Definition / Meaning

A person who, for his own purposes, brings onto his land and keeps there anything likely to do mischief if it escapes, must keep it at his peril; if it escapes and causes damage, he is liable, subject to recognised exceptions.

Essential Points — D-E-N-D

  • Dangerous thing — something likely to cause mischief if it escapes.
  • Escape — the thing must escape from the defendant's land/control to outside it.
  • Non-natural use — a special or exceptional use of land creating increased risk (e.g. large reservoirs, explosives, high-voltage electricity) — ordinary domestic use does not qualify.
  • Damage — actual damage must result from the escape.

Exceptions

  • Act of God
  • Act of a Stranger
  • Plaintiff's own fault
  • Consent / common benefit
  • Statutory authority

Case Law

Rylands v Fletcher (1868)Facts: The defendant built a reservoir on his land; water escaped through disused mine shafts and flooded the plaintiff's neighbouring mine, without any personal negligence by the defendant.
Decision: Held liable under the new strict-liability rule.
Principle: A person making a non-natural use of land, keeping a dangerous thing there, is liable if it escapes and causes damage — irrespective of negligence.
Nichols v Marsland (1876) — Act of God exceptionFacts: Exceptionally heavy, unprecedented rainfall burst the defendant's artificial lakes, flooding the plaintiff's land.
Decision: Defendant not liable.
Principle: The rainfall was so extraordinary it amounted to an Act of God, excusing what would otherwise be strict liability.

Example / Application

A factory storing large quantities of industrial chemicals (non-natural use) that leak and damage a neighbouring farm (escape + damage) is strictly liable under Rylands v Fletcher unless a recognised exception — such as an act of an unforeseeable stranger sabotaging the tank — applies.

Conclusion

Rylands v Fletcher imposes strict liability once dangerous thing, escape, non-natural use and damage are shown — but the five recognised exceptions mean the rule is not absolute (contrast with MQ5's absolute liability, which has none).

MQ5. Explain Absolute Liability under M.C. Mehta v Union of India, and distinguish it from Strict Liability.
📌 PYQ: Core recurring (Absolute Liability) + Recurring (Strict vs Absolute)🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Explain Absolute Liability with reference to M.C. Mehta." · "Distinguish Strict Liability and Absolute Liability."

Introduction

Absolute liability is the distinctly Indian doctrine developed by the Supreme Court for enterprises engaged in hazardous or inherently dangerous activities — a stricter standard than the traditional Rylands rule.

Definition / Meaning

A hazardous or inherently dangerous enterprise owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community; if harm results from the hazardous activity, the enterprise is absolutely liable to compensate, with no Rylands-type exceptions available.

Case Law

M.C. Mehta v Union of India (1987)Facts: Oleum gas leaked from Shriram Food and Fertilizers Ltd.'s hazardous chemical plant in Delhi, causing harm to nearby residents.
Decision: The Supreme Court developed the doctrine of absolute liability.
Principle: A hazardous/inherently dangerous enterprise owes an absolute, non-delegable duty to the community; compensation should be proportionate to the size, capacity and profitability of the enterprise.

Essential Points — Strict vs Absolute

BasisStrict LiabilityAbsolute Liability
CaseRylands v FletcherM.C. Mehta v Union of India
ExceptionsRecognised (God, Stranger, Plaintiff's fault, Consent, Statutory authority)No Rylands-type exceptions
ApplicationDangerous thing + non-natural use + escapeHazardous/inherently dangerous enterprise
EscapeTraditionally requiredNot necessarily required in the same way
DutyQualifiedAbsolute and non-delegable

Example / Application

A chemical plant that leaks toxic gas and injures nearby residents cannot escape liability by showing the leak was due to a third party's sabotage (which might excuse it under Rylands) — under absolute liability, the enterprise remains liable regardless, since no Rylands-type exception applies.

Conclusion

Absolute liability is a higher, exception-free standard developed specifically for hazardous Indian industries, going beyond the qualified strict liability of Rylands v Fletcher.

MQ6. Explain Contributory Negligence and the doctrine of Last Opportunity/Last Clear Chance.
📌 PYQ: 2012, 2017, 2026 (contributory negligence) + 2018 and related papers (Last Opportunity)🎯 Type: 5 / 6-mark short note, or 10 mark if both parts are asked
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Explain Contributory Negligence." · "Explain the Doctrine of Last Opportunity/Last Clear Chance." The Last Opportunity Rule is the historical mechanism by which contributory negligence operated before modern apportionment law — one answer covers both.

Definition / Meaning

Contributory negligence occurs where the plaintiff also fails to take reasonable care for his own safety, and that failure contributes to his own damage. It differs from volenti (voluntary acceptance of risk): contributory negligence is simply a failure to take reasonable care, with no element of consent. Modern law treats it as a ground for apportioning/reducing damages, not a complete bar to the claim.

Essential Points — Last Opportunity Rule

Historically, any contributory negligence by the plaintiff completely barred recovery. The Last Opportunity Rule developed to soften this: where both parties were negligent, the court asked which party had the last clear opportunity to avoid the accident — that party bore full liability.

Case Law

Davies v Mann (1842)Facts: The plaintiff negligently left his donkey, forelegs tied, on a public highway; the defendant, driving too fast, failed to notice it in time and ran it down.
Decision: Defendant held liable.
Principle: Although the plaintiff was initially negligent, the defendant had the last clear opportunity to avoid the accident and failed to take it — this is the leading case for the Last Opportunity Rule.

Conclusion

Contributory negligence is about the plaintiff's own lack of care contributing to his injury; under the Last Opportunity Rule (Davies v Mann), whoever had the final realistic chance to avoid the accident bears the liability.

MQ7. Explain Nervous Shock as a form of actionable damage in Negligence.
📌 PYQ: 2014, 2016, 2018🎯 Type: 10 mark, or 5/6-mark short note
➕ ADDED — this topic was not present in your uploaded Unit III material, despite appearing across 3 exam years. Dulieu v White and Bourhill v Young are standard, foundational cases for this exact doctrine.

Introduction

Nervous shock refers to a recognised psychiatric injury (not mere fear, grief, or distress) caused by witnessing or experiencing a shocking event brought about by the defendant's negligence.

Definition / Meaning

Courts were historically reluctant to compensate psychiatric injury without accompanying physical impact, but gradually recognised that genuine nervous shock — a diagnosable psychiatric condition — is actionable in negligence, subject to the plaintiff being a reasonably foreseeable victim.

Essential Points

  • The shock must result in a recognised psychiatric injury, not merely ordinary grief or fright.
  • The plaintiff must be a person whom the defendant could reasonably foresee as being affected — the "foreseeable claimant" requirement.
  • Proximity — in time, place, and relationship to the event or victim — is relevant to foreseeability.

Case Law

Dulieu v White & Sons (1901)Facts: A pregnant woman inside a pub was seriously frightened when the defendant's servant negligently drove a horse-drawn van through the wall; she suffered nervous shock and a subsequent miscarriage.
Decision: Held liable.
Principle: Recovery for nervous shock is possible where it arises from a reasonable fear of immediate personal injury to oneself — removing the older requirement of actual physical impact.
Bourhill v Young (1943)Facts: A pregnant woman heard, but did not see, a motorcycle accident caused by the defendant's negligent riding some distance away, later saw blood on the road, and suffered nervous shock leading to a miscarriage.
Decision: Defendant not liable.
Principle: No duty was owed because the plaintiff was not a reasonably foreseeable victim — she was outside the area of foreseeable physical or psychiatric injury.

Example / Application

A mother who witnesses her child being struck by a negligently driven car right in front of her has a strong nervous-shock claim (foreseeable, proximate); a stranger who merely hears about the accident secondhand and suffers distress would not (Bourhill v Young).

Conclusion

Nervous shock is actionable where it amounts to a genuine psychiatric injury suffered by a reasonably foreseeable claimant — Dulieu v White established the possibility of recovery, while Bourhill v Young shows its foreseeability limits.
🟠 HIGH PRIORITY
MQ8. Define Nuisance and distinguish between Public and Private Nuisance, including essentials, defences and remedies.
📌 PYQ: Recurring (define/distinguish) + Recurring (private nuisance essentials/defences) + Recurring (public nuisance essentials/remedies)🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Define Nuisance and distinguish Public and Private Nuisance." · "Explain Private Nuisance — essentials, defences/remedies." · "Explain Public Nuisance — essentials and remedies." All three master-list lines are sub-parts of the single composite answer below.

Introduction

Nuisance involves unlawful interference with rights connected with land, and is broadly divided into public nuisance and private nuisance.

A. Public Nuisance

Meaning: affects the community or a class of the public — public health, safety, comfort, or public rights. A private individual generally needs special damage beyond that suffered by the public to sue personally.

Rose v Miles (1815)Facts: A barge blocked a public navigable waterway, forcing the plaintiff to incur extra expense to transport his goods by land.
Decision: Plaintiff could sue.
Principle: Special damage beyond general public inconvenience supports an individual civil action.
Winterbottom v Lord Derby (1867)Facts: The defendant obstructed a public footpath; an individual sued personally.
Decision: Could not sue merely as a member of the public.
Principle: Without special damage, an individual cannot maintain a personal action for public nuisance.

B. Private Nuisance

Meaning: unlawful and unreasonable interference with a person's use or enjoyment of land, or a right connected with land. Reasonableness depends on locality, duration, severity, social utility, sensitivity, and malice. Examples: excessive noise, smoke, smell, vibration, heat, flooding, encroachment.

Sturges v Bridgman (1879)Facts: A doctor set up consulting rooms next to a confectioner whose machinery had long caused noise/vibration.
Decision: Locality and nature of use were decisive.
Principle: Coming to an existing nuisance is not, by itself, a defence.
Robinson v Kilvert (1889)Facts: Heat from the defendant's premises damaged unusually sensitive brown paper stored by the plaintiff.
Decision: Claim failed.
Principle: An abnormally sensitive use is not protected where an ordinary use would not have been substantially affected.
Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett (1936)Facts: The defendant deliberately caused disturbance intending to harm the plaintiff's fox-breeding business.
Decision: Liability followed.
Principle: Malicious conduct can make otherwise borderline interference unreasonable.

Defences to Nuisance

  • Prescriptive right — a private nuisance carried on openly, peaceably, and without objection for the statutory period may become a legally protected right.
  • Statutory authority — where the activity causing the interference is authorised by statute and carried out without negligence.
  • Plaintiff's own sensitivity or fault — an abnormally sensitive use is not protected (Robinson v Kilvert), and a plaintiff cannot complain of a nuisance he substantially contributed to.

Not a defence: that the plaintiff "came to the nuisance" (Sturges v Bridgman) — locality having changed over time does not excuse a genuinely unreasonable interference.

Essential Points — Distinction

BasisPublic NuisancePrivate Nuisance
AffectedCommunity/classIndividual/land user
RightPublic right/comfort/health/safetyUse/enjoyment of land
Individual actionSpecial damage requiredAffected person with a land interest
RemediesCriminal proceedings; civil action only with special damageDamages, injunction

Conclusion

Public nuisance protects the community and requires special damage for an individual to sue personally (Rose v Miles); private nuisance protects an individual's use/enjoyment of land, judged by reasonableness in context (Sturges v Bridgman), with sensitivity (Robinson v Kilvert) and malice (Hollywood Silver Fox Farm) both relevant to that assessment.

MQ9. Explain the legal remedies available for Tortious liability, including Damages and Injunction.
📌 PYQ: Recurring (remedies overview) + Recurring (damages) + Recurring (injunction)🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Explain Remedies available for Tortious liability." · "Explain Damages as a remedy in Tort." · "Explain Injunction as a remedy in Tort."

Introduction

Tort law provides remedies to redress a civil wrong, broadly divided into judicial remedies (through the court) and extra-judicial/self-help remedies.

Essential Points — A. Damages (C.N.O.A.E.)

TypeMeaning
ContemptuousVery small award — court disapproves of the claim/conduct though plaintiff technically succeeds
NominalSmall award for violation of a legal right without substantial actual loss
Ordinary/CompensatoryMain remedy — compensates the plaintiff for loss/injury
AggravatedAdditional compensation where conduct worsened injury to dignity/feelings/reputation
Exemplary/PunitiveBeyond compensation — punishes and deters

Special damages — specifically quantifiable/proved (medical bills, lost earnings, repairs). General damages — not precisely calculable (pain and suffering, loss of amenity).

Essential Points — B. Injunction

A court order directing a person to stop (prohibitory) or perform (mandatory) an act; may be temporary/interlocutory (during the suit) or permanent/perpetual (final order).

Radhey Shyam v Gur Prasad (1978)Facts: Continuing interference from a flour mill in a residential area.
Decision: Permanent injunction granted.
Principle: Continuing nuisance may justify an injunction where damages alone would be an inadequate remedy.

Essential Points — C. Other Remedies

Specific restitution: the court orders return of wrongfully taken land/goods rather than just their money value. Extra-judicial remedies: expulsion of trespasser, re-entry on land, abatement of nuisance, distress damage feasant — all self-help remedies that must be exercised within legal limits, without breach of peace and with reasonable force.

Conclusion

For a full-marks answer, classify remedies first (judicial vs extra-judicial), then explain damages, injunction, specific restitution and extra-judicial remedies in turn, using Radhey Shyam v Gur Prasad to anchor the injunction discussion.

Case / Problem Application Questions (3)

Limited set, matched to the highest-yield principles above.
Problem 1 — Negligence and causation: A patient visits hospital after taking poison; the doctor negligently fails to treat him, but medical evidence shows he would have died even with proper treatment. Discuss liability.
Principle: But-for causation.
Answer: Identify duty → possible breach → apply the but-for test → Barnett v Chelsea Hospital (1969) — since death would have occurred anyway, factual causation fails and the hospital is not liable.
Problem 2 — Rylands v Fletcher: A company stores a dangerous chemical in large quantities for a special industrial use; it escapes and damages neighbouring property; no recognised exception applies. Discuss liability.
Principle: Strict liability.
Answer: Dangerous thing (chemical) + non-natural use (large-scale industrial storage) + escape + damage — with no exception applicable, strict liability under Rylands v Fletcher applies. If the activity is itself hazardous/inherently dangerous, also raise M.C. Mehta absolute liability as an alternative/additional ground.
Problem 3 — Res Ipsa Loquitur (Added): A patient wakes up after surgery with a surgical instrument left inside her body; she cannot show exactly how or why it happened. Discuss liability.
Principle: Res ipsa loquitur.
Answer: The operating theatre was under the hospital's exclusive control, and instruments are not ordinarily left inside patients without negligence — negligence is inferred (as in Byrne v Boadle), shifting the burden to the hospital to explain the absence of negligence.

🎯 Common Exam Traps

⚠️ Contributory Negligence vs Volenti — contributory negligence is a failure to take care for oneself (damages apportioned); volenti is voluntary acceptance of a known risk (traditionally a complete defence).
⚠️ Re Polemis vs Wagon Mound — don't present them as equally valid; Wagon Mound's foreseeability test is the modern rule, Re Polemis is the superseded historical approach.
⚠️ Public vs Private Nuisance — an individual needs special damage to sue for public nuisance; for private nuisance, he needs a relevant interest in land, not special damage.
⚠️ Strict vs Absolute Liability — strict liability (Rylands) has 5 recognised exceptions; absolute liability (M.C. Mehta) has none — don't apply Rylands-type exceptions to an absolute liability answer.
⚠️ General Rule vs Exceptions — for Rylands, always state the exceptions; an answer that only states the rule without the exceptions loses easy marks.
⚠️ Res Ipsa Loquitur is not strict liability — it only shifts the evidential burden to the defendant; it does not remove the need for negligence altogether.
