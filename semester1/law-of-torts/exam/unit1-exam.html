MQ1. Define Tort and explain its essential elements. (Also covers: nature and scope of Law of Torts.)
📌 PYQ: 2012, 2014, 2017, 2019, 2021, 2022, 2024, 2026🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Define Tort and explain its essential elements." · "What are the essential elements of a Tort?" · "Explain the nature and scope of Law of Torts."

Introduction

*The word "Tort" comes from the Latin tortum, meaning "twisted" or "crooked" — conduct that deviates from straight, lawful conduct. A tort is a civil wrong, independent of contract, for which the ordinary remedy is unliquidated damages.

Definition

*Winfield: Tortious liability arises from breach of a duty primarily fixed by law; the duty is owed to persons generally, and its breach is redressable by an action for unliquidated damages.

Salmond: A civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract, trust or other equitable obligation.

Essential Points — W + D + R

  • Wrongful act/omission — must be legally, not merely morally, wrongful (e.g. reckless driving; a doctor's failure to warn of a known risk)
  • Legal damage — violation of a legal right (injuria); actual loss is not always necessary
  • Legal remedy — generally unliquidated (court-assessed) damages

*Nature & scope (if the question uses this wording): Tort law is largely uncodified and judge-made; its scope keeps expanding as courts recognise new wrongs (privacy, harassment, constitutional torts) — this is the practical expression of Winfield's "flexible principle" view.

Case Law

Ashby v White (1703)Facts: A qualified voter was wrongfully denied his vote by the returning officer; his candidate still won, so no financial loss resulted.
Decision: Held liable, damages awarded.
Principle: Violation of a legal right is actionable in itself, even without proof of actual loss.

Example / Application

If a factory unlawfully discharges effluent onto a neighbour's land, even a small verifiable intrusion (wrongful act) that damages the neighbour's legal right to enjoy his land (legal damage) entitles him to damages (legal remedy) — all three elements are present.

Conclusion

Tortious liability therefore rests on three pillars — a legally wrongful act, legal injury to the plaintiff, and an available legal remedy — with the focus always on the violation of a legal right rather than the mere fact of loss, as Ashby v White demonstrates.

MQ2. Define Tort and distinguish it from Contract and Crime.
📌 PYQ: 2010–2016, 2021–2025, 2026🎯 Type: 10 / 15 / 16 mark

Introduction

Tort, Contract and Crime are different kinds of legal wrongs. They differ mainly in the source of the duty, the person who brings the action and the remedy available.

Definition of Tort

Tort (Winfield): Tortious liability arises from the breach of a duty primarily fixed by law; this duty is towards persons generally and its breach is redressable by an action for unliquidated damages.

Simple meaning: A Tort is a civil wrong arising from the breach of a duty imposed by law, for which the usual remedy is unliquidated damages.

Meaning of Contract

A Contract is an agreement between two or more parties that creates legal obligations between them.

Example: A agrees to sell a car to B, and B agrees to pay the agreed price. If A fails to deliver the car, it may be a breach of contract.

Meaning of Crime

A Crime is an act or omission which is prohibited by criminal law and punishable by the State.

Example: Theft, assault or murder may constitute crimes.

Difference between Tort, Contract and Crime

Basis Tort Contract Crime
Source of duty Law Agreement Criminal law
Nature Civil wrong Breach of agreement Public wrong / offence
Who brings the case? Injured person Aggrieved party State
*Privity Generally not required Generally required Not applicable
Main remedy Unliquidated damages Damages / other contractual remedies Punishment
Purpose Compensation for the wrong Enforcement of contractual obligation Punishment of the offender
Proof Balance of probabilities Balance of probabilities *Beyond reasonable doubt

Example / Application

If A assaults B (means - A attacks B or threatens B with immediate physical harm):

  • Tort: B can claim damages from A for the injury.
  • Crime: The State can prosecute A for the criminal offence.

Thus, the same act may sometimes create both civil and criminal liability.

Conclusion

The main difference can be remembered as:
Tort → Duty imposed by law
Contract → Duty created by agreement
Crime → Wrong punishable by the State

Thus, although Tort, Contract and Crime are different, one act can sometimes result in more than one type of liability.

MQ3. Define tortious liability and distinguish it from contractual and criminal liability.
📌 PYQ: 2016, 2024🎯 Type: 10 mark
💡 Builds directly on MQ2's table — apply the same source-of-duty logic specifically to "liability" rather than re-deriving the essentials from MQ1.

Introduction

"Tortious liability" refers to the legal responsibility that arises specifically from a tort, as distinct from liability arising from a contract (a voluntary agreement) or a crime (a public wrong punished by the State).

Definition / Meaning

Tortious liability arises from the breach of a legal duty imposed by law. This duty is generally owed to persons, and its breach gives rise to a civil remedy, usually unliquidated damages.

Essential Points — Distinction Table

BasisTortiousContractualCriminal
SourceFixed by law, owed generallyCreated by agreementFixed by penal law
ConsentNot requiredRequired (privity)Not relevant
Enforced byInjured personParty to the contractState
RemedyUnliquidated damagesDamages tied to breachPunishment

Case Law

No separate case is needed beyond what MQ1/MQ2 already establish — Ashby v White (cited there) already illustrates that tortious liability arises from violation of a legal right, independent of any contract between the parties.

Example / Application

A stranger who negligently damages your car owes you no contractual duty at all (no privity, no agreement) — yet tortious liability still arises purely because the law fixes a general duty of care, which is precisely what separates it from contractual liability.

Conclusion

Tortious liability is defined by a duty fixed by law and owed generally, requiring no privity or consent — this single distinguishing feature separates it from both contractual liability (consent-based) and criminal liability (State-enforced, punitive).

MQ4. “All torts are civil wrongs, but all civil wrongs are not torts.” Discuss / Justify with cases.
📌 PYQ: 2012, 2019, 2020, 2022, 2025🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark

Introduction

A tort is a civil wrong, but the term civil wrong is wider than tort. Therefore, every tort is a civil wrong, but every civil wrong does not amount to a tort.

Meaning of Tort

A Tort is a civil wrong arising from the breach of a legal duty imposed by law, for which the usual remedy is unliquidated damages.

Why all Torts are Civil Wrongs

A tort is a wrong against a person for which the injured person can seek a civil remedy, usually damages.

Example: If A negligently hits B and causes injury, B can bring a civil action against A and claim damages.

Therefore: Tort → Civil Wrong

Why all Civil Wrongs are not Torts

The term civil wrong includes different types of wrongs, such as:

  • Tort
  • Breach of Contract
  • Breach of Trust

A breach of contract is a civil wrong, but it is not a tort because the duty arises from the agreement between the parties.

Similarly, breach of trust is governed by the law relating to trusts and is not treated as a tort.

Main Difference

Basis Tort Contract
Source of duty Law Agreement
Relationship Generally no prior contract is required Contractual relationship exists
Nature of wrong Civil wrong Civil wrong
Usual remedy Unliquidated damages Damages / other contractual remedies

Case Laws

1. Ashby v White (1703)

The plaintiff's legal right to vote was wrongfully interfered with. The court recognised a remedy for the violation of the legal right.

Principle: Ubi jus ibi remedium — Where there is a right, there is a remedy.

2. Donoghue v Stevenson (1932)

The case established the modern principle of duty of care in negligence. A legal duty may arise independently of any contract between the parties.

Principle: A tortious duty can arise by operation of law, even without a contract.

Example / Application

A agrees to deliver goods to B on a particular date but fails to deliver them.

B may have a civil remedy for breach of contract. However, this is not a tort, because A's duty to deliver the goods arose from their contract, not independently from law.

Conclusion

Thus, tort is a species of civil wrong. The wider category of civil wrongs also includes breach of contract and breach of trust. The main distinction is the source of the duty:

Tort → Duty imposed by law
Contract → Duty created by agreement
Trust → Duty arising from the trust relationship

Therefore, all torts are civil wrongs, but all civil wrongs are not torts.

MQ5. Explain Injuria Sine Damno and Damnum Sine Injuria with decided cases. (Also covers: "Every injury imports damage but every damage is not injury"; Explain legal damage as an essential element.)
📌 PYQ: 2011, 2012, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2021, 2023, 2026🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Explain Injuria sine damno and Damnum sine injuria with decided cases/illustrations." · "Every injury imports damage but every damage is not injury. Discuss." · "Explain legal damage as an essential element of Tort." · "Distinguish Injuria sine damno and Damnum sine injuria."

Introduction

Tort law protects legal rights. Mere financial or actual loss does not always give rise to a legal action. This is explained by the two maxims Injuria Sine Damno and Damnum Sine Injuria.

Meaning

  • Injuria = Violation of a legal right
  • Damnum = Actual loss or harm
  • Sine = Without

Injuria Sine Damno → Legal injury without actual loss.

Damnum Sine Injuria → Actual loss without violation of a legal right.

Essential Points — Distinction Table

Basis Injuria Sine Damno Damnum Sine Injuria
Meaning Legal injury, no actual loss Actual loss, no legal injury
Actionable? Yes Generally No
Case Ashby v White (1703) Gloucester Grammar School Case (1410)

1. Injuria Sine Damno

It means violation of a legal right without actual loss. It is actionable.

Case: Ashby v White (1703)

Facts: Ashby was a qualified voter, but White, the returning officer, wrongfully refused to allow him to vote. His candidate nevertheless won the election.

Decision: Ashby was entitled to damages because his legal right to vote had been violated.

Principle: A violation of a legal right can be actionable even without actual loss.

2. Damnum Sine Injuria

It means actual loss without violation of a legal right. It is generally not actionable.

Case: Gloucester Grammar School Case (1410)

Facts: The defendant opened a competing school nearby and charged lower fees. The plaintiff consequently lost students and income.

Decision: The plaintiff could not claim damages because no legal right had been violated.

Principle: Mere loss caused by a lawful act is not sufficient to establish a tort.

Key Principle

“Every injury imports damage, but every damage is not injury.”

Here, injury means legal injury, i.e., violation of a legal right.

Conclusion

Thus, legal injury is the important consideration in Tort law. Actual loss alone is not sufficient unless it is accompanied by the violation of a legal right.

Injuria Sine Damno → Legal injury → Actionable
Damnum Sine Injuria → Mere loss → Generally not actionable

MQ6. Explain/trace the development of Ubi Jus Ibi Remedium.
📌 PYQ: 2011, 2012, 2013, 2019, 2021, 2022, 2024🎯 Type: 10 mark, or 5/6-mark short note

Introduction

Ubi jus ibi remedium is a foundational maxim of tort law expressing the idea that a legally recognised right must always be matched by an effective legal remedy.

Definition / Meaning

"Where there is a right, there is a remedy." Wherever a legally recognised right is violated, the law must provide a means of redress — otherwise the right itself would be meaningless.

Essential Points

  • Scope: applies only to legal rights, not to purely moral, social or religious grievances.
  • Importance: gives practical effect to legal rights by guaranteeing an enforceable remedy for their violation; forms the theoretical basis for injuria sine damno.
  • Limitation: the maxim does not create new rights — there must first be a recognised legal right, and then a legally recognised violation of it, before a remedy can be claimed.

Case Law

Ashby v White (1703)Facts: A qualified voter was wrongfully denied his vote; his preferred candidate still won, so no financial loss resulted.
Decision: Held liable.
Principle: Because the plaintiff had a legal right to vote, its violation was actionable in itself — the law provided a remedy despite the absence of loss.

Example / Application

If a statute grants a person a specific legal right (e.g. to a fair hearing) and an authority denies it without justification, the maxim guarantees that person a remedy — even if they cannot point to any measurable financial loss.

Conclusion

Ubi jus ibi remedium remains foundational because it ensures legal rights are not hollow declarations, but are always backed by effective judicial redress whenever genuinely violated.

MQ7. Discuss the importance of mental elements in tortious liability, and distinguish Intention, Motive and Malice.
📌 PYQ: 2011,13,18,23,24,26 (importance) + 2012,13,25 (distinguish)🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Discuss the importance of mental elements in tortious liability." · "Distinguish Intention, Motive and Malice."

Introduction

Unlike criminal law, which places heavy emphasis on mens rea, tort law is primarily concerned with the wrongful act and the legal right violated — liability can rest on intention, negligence, or strict liability. Nonetheless, mental elements remain important for specific torts.

Definition / Meaning

Intention — conscious desire/purpose to bring about an act or its consequence. Motive — the underlying reason "why" behind the act. Malice — either a wrongful act without lawful justification (malice in law) or actual ill-will/spite (malice in fact).

Essential Points — Distinction Table

BasisIntentionMotiveMalice
RelevanceEssential in intentional torts (assault, battery, false imprisonment)Generally irrelevantRelevant in defamation, malicious prosecution
ProofRelates to the act itselfNot usually requiredMalice in law presumed; malice in fact must be proved
RuleNot needed for negligence/strict liabilityGood motive ≠ excuse; bad motive ≠ liability for a lawful actLegal malice ≠ personal hatred by default

Case Law

Bradford Corporation v Pickles (1895)Facts: Pickles deliberately diverted underground water on his own land, motivated by a desire to force Bradford Corporation to buy his land at a high price.
Decision: No liability — he was exercising a lawful right.
Principle: A lawful act does not become tortious merely because it is done with a bad motive.

Example / Application

Two neighbours both build a legal fence. One does it purely for privacy; the other does it out of spite to block his neighbour's view. Both acts remain lawful — motive does not change the legal character of an otherwise lawful act, as Bradford v Pickles confirms.

Conclusion

Intention matters in intentional torts, motive is generally irrelevant except in specific torts (malicious prosecution, conspiracy, deceit, qualified privilege), and malice may be legal (presumed) or actual (proved) depending on the tort in question.

MQ8. Explain Malice in Law and Malice in Fact.
📌 PYQ: 2012, 2017, 2022🎯 Type: 5 / 6-mark short note
💡 If MQ7 (Intention/Motive/Malice) is asked in full, this is already covered — use this condensed version only when the question asks specifically about malice alone.

Introduction

Malice in tort law takes two distinct forms, both important for defamation and malicious prosecution.

Definition / Meaning

Malice in Law (legal/implied malice) — the intentional commission of a wrongful act without lawful justification; it does not require proof of personal hatred and may simply be presumed from the wrongful act itself. Malice in Fact (actual/express malice) — genuine ill-will, spite or improper motive actually held against the plaintiff; it must be proved as a fact.

Essential Points — Distinction Table

BasisMalice in LawMalice in Fact
Also calledLegal/Implied maliceActual/Express malice
ProofPresumed by lawMust be proved
ExampleFalse defamatory publicationMalicious prosecution from personal grudge

Case Law

No additional case beyond Bradford Corporation v Pickles (already covered under MQ7) is required — that case illustrates that motive/malice does not affect an otherwise lawful act, which underlies both forms of malice discussed here.

Conclusion

Malice in law is concerned with the legal character of the wrongful act and is presumed; malice in fact is concerned with the defendant's genuine state of mind and must be proved — neither should be equated with personal hatred by default.

MQ9. Trace the evolution/development of Law of Torts in England and India.
📌 PYQ: 2011, 2012, 2023🎯 Type: 10 mark — keep concise, don't over-prepare

Introduction

The law of torts developed mainly through judicial decisions and precedent rather than through a single comprehensive code, both in England and later in India.

Essential Points

  • England: early writ system tied remedies to recognised "forms of action"; 18th–19th century case law gradually expanded actionable wrongs, especially negligence, driven by the Industrial Revolution.
  • India: colonial courts adopted English common-law principles, applied through justice, equity and good conscience; post-Independence, Indian courts developed State liability and constitutional torts further.

Case Law

Named only, as one-line evolution markers — full facts/decision/principle for these belong to Units II (vicarious/State liability) and III (absolute liability), so do not over-prepare them here: State of Rajasthan v Vidhyawati; Kasturilal v State of U.P.; Rudal Shah v State of Bihar; Bhim Singh v State of J&K; M.C. Mehta v Union of India (absolute liability).

Conclusion

Indian tort law today combines inherited English common-law principles with independent judicial development suited to India's constitutional values.

MQ10. Explain/compare the definitions of Tort given by Winfield and Salmond. (Also covers: Law of Tort vs Law of Torts; brief mention of Fraser.)
📌 PYQ: Definition/theory theme (recurring in question banks; exact years not listed in the supplied master list)🎯 Type: 5 / 10 mark

Introduction

Winfield and Salmond represent the two classic competing views on how tort should be defined — one broad and principle-based, the other narrower and category-based.

Definition

Winfield: Tortious liability arises from breach of a duty primarily fixed by law, owed to persons generally, redressable by unliquidated damages.
Salmond: A civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages, and which is not exclusively breach of contract, trust or other equitable obligation.
Fraser (syllabus item, not separately PYQ-evidenced — brief mention only): Infringement of a right in rem of a private individual, giving a right of compensation.

Essential Points — Distinction Table

BasisWinfieldSalmond
ViewGeneral principle — unjustifiable harm is tortious unless a defence appliesOnly specific recognised torts ("pigeon-hole" system)
New tortsCan develop as society changesMust fit an existing category
Label"Law of Tort""Law of Torts"
NatureFlexible/progressiveRigid/conservative

Case Law

No case is attached to this purely definitional/theoretical comparison in the source material.

Example / Application

The growth of privacy-related and harassment-related claims in modern courts is generally cited as evidence that the broader Winfield approach better describes how tort law actually develops today.

Conclusion

Winfield's flexible "Law of Tort" view and Salmond's narrower "Law of Torts" view represent two ways of understanding the same body of law — modern courts generally lean toward the broader Winfield approach as new torts continue to emerge.

Case / Problem Application Questions (3)

Small set only — genuinely useful application patterns for Unit I.
Problem 1: A qualified voter is wrongfully stopped from voting, but his candidate still wins the election. Can he claim a remedy?
Principle: Injuria sine damno.
Answer: Yes — his legal right to vote was violated; actual financial loss is not required (Ashby v White, 1703).
Problem 2: A person suffers financial loss because another lawfully opens a competing business nearby. Can he claim damages?
Principle: Damnum sine injuria.
Answer: No — loss from lawful competition, without violation of any legal right, is not actionable (Gloucester Grammar School Case, 1410).
Problem 3: A landowner deliberately diverts underground water on his own land purely to force his neighbour to pay a high price for it. Is he liable?
Principle: Motive is generally irrelevant.
Answer: No — exercising a lawful right over one's own property doesn't become tortious merely because the motive was bad (Bradford Corporation v Pickles, 1895).

🎯 Common Exam Traps

⚠️ Injuria ≠ Damnum — legal right violated is not the same as actual financial/physical loss.
⚠️ Intention ≠ Motive — intention is the conscious purpose to act; motive is the underlying "why," and is generally irrelevant.
⚠️ Malice in Law ≠ Malice in Fact — one is presumed from the act, the other must be proved as actual ill-will.
⚠️ Tort ≠ Crime — don't just write "one is civil, one is criminal" and stop; add privity, proof-standard and remedy differences for full marks.
⚠️ Tort ≠ Contract — duty by law vs duty by agreement; no privity needed in tort.
⚠️ Legal damage ≠ actual financial loss — the single most examined confusion in the whole unit; every Injuria/Damnum answer should open by stating this distinction clearly.
