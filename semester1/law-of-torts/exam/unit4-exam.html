MQ1. Define Assault and Battery, distinguish between them, and state their defences.
📌 PYQ: 2011,12,13,15,16,19,21,22,25,26 (core) + 2015 and related (defences)🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Define Assault and Battery and distinguish between them." · "Explain justifications/defences to Assault and Battery."

Introduction

Assault and battery are the two foundational intentional torts against the person, and are almost always tested together as a pair.

Definition / Meaning

Assault — an act causing reasonable apprehension of imminent battery; actual contact is not required. Battery — the intentional, direct, unlawful application of force to another person, without consent or justification.

Essential Points

  • Assault: act by defendant + reasonable apprehension + apprehension of imminent contact + defendant's apparent present ability to carry it out.
  • Battery: intentional + direct + unlawful force; actual injury not necessary — slight hostile touching suffices; ordinary incidental contact is not battery (implied consent).

Case Law

Tubervell v Savage (1669)Facts: Hand placed on sword, but accompanying words showed no immediate intention to strike.
Decision: No assault.
Principle: Words can negate an apparent assault.
Stephens v Myers (1830)Facts: Defendant advanced threateningly at a parish meeting but was stopped before reaching the plaintiff.
Decision: Assault established.
Principle: Reasonable apprehension plus apparent ability to carry out the threat is enough, even without contact.
Cole v Turner (1704)Facts/Principle: The least touching of another in anger may be battery; ordinary everyday incidental contact is not, due to implied consent.

Explanation — Defences

  • Consent — ordinary social/incidental contact is impliedly consented to (Cole v Turner).
  • Private defence — reasonable, proportionate force to protect oneself or property.
  • Lawful authority — e.g. a police officer using reasonable force during a lawful arrest.

Distinction Table

BasisAssaultBattery
MeaningReasonable apprehensionActual application of force
ContactNot requiredRequired
ExampleRaising a fist threateninglyUnlawfully punching
🎯 EXAM TIP: If the question only asks to "distinguish," the table above is your whole answer — don't add the defences section unless it's specifically asked.

Conclusion

Assault protects against the fear of imminent harm; battery punishes the actual unlawful contact — both require intention, and both yield to genuine consent or proportionate private defence.

MQ2. Define False Imprisonment, explain its essentials, defences and remedies.

Introduction

False imprisonment protects personal liberty against total, unlawful restraint, and is actionable per se — without proof of actual damage.

Definition / Meaning

Total and unlawful restraint of a person's liberty, without lawful justification, against that person's will.

Essential Points

  • Total restraint (not merely partial obstruction)
  • No lawful justification
  • Against the plaintiff's will
  • No minimum duration required

Case Law

Bird v Jones (1845)Facts: Plaintiff was stopped from going one way along a footway but could return the way he came.
Decision: Not false imprisonment.
Principle: Restraint must be TOTAL — this was only partial.
Meering v Grahame-White Aviation Co. (1919)Facts: Plaintiff was confined by guards he did not know had been posted.
Decision: False imprisonment established despite his unawareness.
Principle: Awareness of the restraint is not essential to liability; it may affect the amount of damages only.
Bhim Singh v State of J&K (1985)Facts: An MLA was wrongfully arrested and prevented from attending the State Assembly.
Decision: Exemplary compensation awarded.
Principle: Wrongful detention by the State is both a tort and a constitutional wrong (Article 21); illustrates exemplary damages for gross abuse of power.

Explanation — Defences & Remedies

Defences: lawful arrest by a person with proper legal authority; reasonable suspicion held by a police officer acting within statutory power; the plaintiff's own consent.

Remedies: the constitutional remedy of habeas corpus (directing production/release of a person unlawfully detained); ordinary and exemplary damages (Bhim Singh); self-help — a detained person may use reasonable force to escape.

Example / Application

A shopper wrongly detained by store security for suspected shoplifting, with no reasonable grounds and no route of exit offered, has been falsely imprisoned — even a few minutes' restraint is enough, and he need not have known guards were watching the exit (Meering).

Conclusion

False imprisonment is actionable per se once total, unlawful restraint is shown; awareness is irrelevant to liability (Meering), and the State is not exempt from liability or exemplary damages for its abuse (Bhim Singh).

MQ3. Define Defamation and explain its essentials.
📌 PYQ: 2011,12,2016–2019,2022,2024,2026🎯 Type: 10 / 16 mark

Introduction

Defamation protects a person's reputation from false statements that lower them in the estimation of others.

Definition / Meaning

Winfield: A statement which tends to lower a person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally, or which tends to make them shun or avoid that person.

Essential Points — 3 Elements

  • A defamatory statement
  • Referring to the plaintiff
  • Published to at least one person other than the plaintiff

Case Law

Sim v Stretch (1936)Principle: Whether a statement is defamatory is judged objectively — would it tend to lower the plaintiff's reputation among right-thinking ordinary people?
Hulton v Jones (1910)Facts: A fictional character in a published story happened to share a real person's name.
Decision: Liability arose.
Principle: Liability depends on whether reasonable readers understood the statement as referring to the real plaintiff — the author's intention is irrelevant.
Theaker v Richardson (1962)Principle: Publication is established once the statement is communicated to, and read by, at least one person other than the plaintiff, in reasonably foreseeable circumstances.

Example / Application

A letter making a false accusation, addressed to the plaintiff but opened and read by their spouse in the ordinary course of post, satisfies "publication" (Theaker v Richardson).

Conclusion

All three essentials — defamatory content, reference to the plaintiff, and publication — must be independently satisfied; failing any one of them defeats the claim.

MQ4. Distinguish Libel and Slander, and explain Slander actionable per se.
📌 PYQ: 2016,2018–2020,2026 (libel/slander) + 2016,2019,2026 (slander per se)🎯 Type: 10 mark
💡 ONE ANSWER COVERS THESE PYQs: "Distinguish Libel and Slander." · "Explain Slander actionable per se."

Definition / Meaning

Libel — defamation in a permanent/visible form (writing, print, effigy). Slander — defamation in a transient form (spoken words, gestures).

Distinction Table

BasisLibelSlander
FormPermanent/visibleTransient/spoken
DamageActionable per seGenerally needs proof of actual/special damage
ExceptionNone4 exceptions make it actionable per se

Essential Points — Slander Actionable Per Se (CDPU)

  • Crime — imputation of a criminal offence
  • Disease — imputation of a contagious disease causing others to avoid the person
  • Profession — imputation affecting trade/profession/business
  • Unchastity — traditional exception regarding unchastity of a woman
🧠 MEMORY TRICK: CDPU — Crime, Disease, Profession, Unchastity.

Conclusion

Libel is always actionable without proof of damage; slander generally requires proof of actual damage unless it falls within one of the four CDPU exceptions.

MQ5. Explain the defences to an action for Defamation.
📌 PYQ: 2011,2012,2017,2019,2026🎯 Type: 10 mark

Introduction

Even where a statement is defamatory, several recognised defences may still defeat the plaintiff's claim.

Explanation — Essential Points

  • Justification (Truth) — truth is a complete defence.
  • Fair Comment — honest opinion on a matter of public interest.
  • Absolute Privilege — complete protection on recognised occasions (Parliament, judicial proceedings); malice does not defeat it.
  • Qualified Privilege — protection on duty/interest occasions, but conditional; malice CAN defeat it.
  • Consent — the plaintiff consented to the publication.

Case Law

Adam v Ward (1917)Principle: Qualified privilege protects a statement made in the discharge of a duty or common interest, but is lost if the plaintiff proves the defendant acted with malice.
⚠️ EXAM TRAP: Don't write "privilege is always a full defence" — distinguish absolute (malice-proof) from qualified (malice defeats it); this is the examiner's favourite trap in this topic.

Conclusion

The strength of a privilege defence depends entirely on whether it is absolute or qualified — this distinction, not the mere existence of "privilege," determines whether malice can defeat it.

MQ6. Define Malicious Prosecution and state its essentials.
📌 PYQ: Recurring syllabus/question-bank theme — no specific exam years confirmed in the master list🎯 Type: 10 mark

Definition / Meaning

Malicious prosecution is the malicious institution of unsuccessful criminal (or, in certain contexts, civil) proceedings against another, without reasonable and probable cause.

Essential Points — PRMFDA (6 elements)

Prosecution by defendant + absence of Reasonable/probable cause + Malice + Favourable termination + Damage + plaintiff was the Accused.

Case Law

Abrath v North Eastern Railway (1886)Principle: The plaintiff bears the burden of proving absence of reasonable and probable cause.
Brown v Hawkes (1891)Principle: An honest belief in guilt, based on reasonable grounds, amounts to reasonable and probable cause, even if the plaintiff is later acquitted.
🎯 EXAM TIP: Malice and absence of reasonable cause are NOT the same thing — absence of cause is only evidence of malice, not conclusive proof of it. State this explicitly for full marks.

Conclusion

All six PRMFDA elements must be independently proved; the claim fails if the defendant shows an honest, reasonably-grounded belief in guilt, even without an ultimate conviction.

MQ7. Explain Intimidation and Civil Conspiracy.
📌 PYQ: Syllabus/question-bank theme (both) — no specific exam years confirmed in the master list; grouped under one KSLU syllabus heading🎯 Type: 10 mark
💡 One composite answer, two clearly separated sub-parts — extract either half alone if only one is asked.

A. Intimidation

Definition: An unlawful threat or coercive pressure causing another to act, or refrain from acting, resulting in recognised damage.

Rookes v Barnard (1964)Principle: An unlawful threat that coerces another into action, causing loss, is actionable intimidation.

B. Civil Conspiracy

Definition: A combination of two or more persons to do an unlawful act, or a lawful act by unlawful means, causing damage.

Quinn v Leathem (1901)Principle: The "predominant purpose" test — if the predominant purpose of the combination is to injure the plaintiff, it is actionable; if predominantly to advance the defendants' own legitimate interests (injury being incidental), it is not.

Distinction Table

BasisIntimidationConspiracy
PartiesMay be a single personRequires 2+ persons combining
MethodUnlawful threatAgreement/combination
CaseRookes v BarnardQuinn v Leathem

Conclusion

Both are "freedom/economic" torts requiring either an unlawful threat (intimidation) or a combination with a predominantly malicious purpose (conspiracy) — the two tests are distinct and should not be conflated.

MQ8. Explain Trespass to Land — essentials and remedies.
📌 PYQ: Recurring🎯 Type: 10 mark
🔍 GAP FILLED — "remedies" not explicit in your Study Material; added below, applying the general remedies framework to trespass specifically, no new case introduced.

Definition / Meaning

Direct and unjustifiable interference with land in another's possession (ownership not essential), actionable per se even without actual damage.

Essential Points

  • Direct interference
  • Plaintiff in possession
  • No lawful justification
  • Intentional entry

Forms: unauthorised entry, placing objects on land, remaining after licence revoked, abusing a licence. Trespass ab initio — a lawful entry later abused may be treated as trespass from the start.

Case Law

Entick v Carrington (1765)Facts: Officials entered and searched the plaintiff's home under a general warrant, seizing papers.
Decision: Held liable.
Principle: Even a small unauthorised invasion of private property is trespass.

Explanation — Remedies

Damages (actionable per se, so nominal damages are available even without proof of loss); injunction to restrain a continuing trespass; re-entry/self-help to retake possession, using no more force than reasonably necessary.

Conclusion

Trespass to land protects possession, not ownership, and is actionable the moment unauthorised direct entry occurs — remedies range from nominal damages to injunctions for continuing interference.

MQ9. Explain Trespass to Goods, Conversion and Detinue.
📌 PYQ: Recurring (Trespass to Goods) + Property-tort theme (Conversion/Detinue)🎯 Type: 10 mark

Introduction

These three torts protect personal property (goods/chattels), each addressing a distinct form of interference rather than a single continuous wrong.

Definition / Essential Points

  • Trespass to Goods — direct and intentional interference with goods in another's possession (e.g. scratching, moving, or using goods without permission); actionable per se.
  • Conversion — intentionally dealing with goods in a manner inconsistent with the owner's rights, treating them as one's own (e.g. wrongful sale, destruction, or refusal to return); remedy is typically the value of the goods.
  • Detinue — wrongful detention of goods — refusal to return them after a proper demand.

Case Law

No specific leading case is required beyond this definitional distinction, consistent with your source material's own treatment — this is answered conceptually rather than case-by-case.

Distinction Table

BasisConversionDetinue
Core wrongDealing inconsistently with ownershipWrongful detention/refusal to return
RemedyValue of goodsReturn of goods or their value + damages

Conclusion

Trespass to Goods, Conversion and Detinue are distinct property torts, each addressing a different form of interference with goods — direct physical interference, dealing with goods inconsistently with the owner's rights, and wrongful detention after a demand for return — rather than strict escalating degrees of the same wrong.

MQ10. Explain Mayhem.
📌 PYQ: Syllabus/question-bank (low evidence — short note only)🎯 Type: 5-mark short note

Definition / Meaning

A permanent disabling, disfiguring, or dismembering injury; historically concerned with reducing a person's ability to defend themselves, now understood more broadly as permanent physical disfigurement or disabling injury.

Essential Points

Distinguished from ordinary battery solely by the permanent character of the injury. No specific leading case is attached to this topic in your source material — keep this answer strictly to definition + permanence + distinction from battery.

Conclusion

Mayhem is a historically-rooted, narrow category of battery, distinguished only by the permanence of the resulting injury.

⚠️ Common Exam Traps

⚠️ Assault vs Battery — contact is the dividing line; don't confuse a "threat" with an actual "touch."
⚠️ False Imprisonment — "total" restraint is essential; partial obstruction (Bird v Jones) is not enough.
⚠️ Absolute vs Qualified Privilege — malice defeats qualified privilege only, never absolute.
⚠️ Malice ≠ Absence of Reasonable Cause — one is evidence of the other, not proof of it.
⚠️ Intimidation vs Conspiracy — don't apply the "predominant purpose" test to intimidation; it belongs only to conspiracy.
⚠️ Conversion vs Detinue — conversion is treating goods as your own; detinue is refusing to return them after a demand — don't merge the two remedies.
