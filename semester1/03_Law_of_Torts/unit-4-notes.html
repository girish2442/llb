PART 10 — TORTS AGAINST THE PERSON

1. Assault

Assault is an act by the defendant which causes the plaintiff a reasonable apprehension of the infliction of battery upon himself. Actual physical contact is not necessary.

MEMORY: ASSAULT = reasonable apprehension of battery — no actual contact required.

Essential Elements

Act by the defendant.

Reasonable apprehension in the plaintiff.

Apprehension of imminent battery or physical contact.

Present or apparent ability to carry out the threat.

Tubervell v Savage (1669): A hand was placed on a sword, but accompanying words indicated that there was no immediate intention to strike; the words therefore negated the apparent assault.

Stephens v Myers (1830): The defendant advanced threateningly toward the plaintiff during a parish meeting but was stopped before reaching him. Assault was established because there was reasonable apprehension of battery and apparent ability to carry it out.

2. Battery

Battery is the intentional and direct application of force to another person without lawful justification or consent.

MEMORY: BATTERY = intentional + direct + unlawful touching.

Essential Elements

Intentional act.

Direct application of force.

Absence of consent.

Absence of lawful justification.

Actual injury is not necessary. The source emphasizes that slight intentional touching may amount to battery where it is hostile or unlawful; ordinary incidental contact is different.

Cole v Turner (1704): The least touching of another in anger may constitute battery, while ordinary incidental contact in daily life is not treated as battery because of implied consent.

Assault vs Battery

3. Mayhem

Mayhem, also called maim, refers to permanent disabling, disfiguring or dismembering injury to the body. Historically it concerned injury that reduced a person's ability to defend himself; the source gives the modern idea of permanent physical disfigurement or disabling injury.

EXAM POINT: Define mayhem, state its permanent/disabling character, and distinguish it from ordinary battery.

4. False Imprisonment

False imprisonment is the unlawful and total restraint of a person's freedom of movement without lawful justification. It is actionable per se, so proof of actual damage is not necessary.

MEMORY: FALSE IMPRISONMENT = TOTAL + UNLAWFUL RESTRAINT OF LIBERTY.

Essential Elements

Total restraint of movement.

Restraint without lawful justification or legal authority.

Restraint against the plaintiff's will / without consent.

No minimum duration; even momentary total restraint may be sufficient.

Bird v Jones (1845): The plaintiff was prevented from proceeding in one direction on a footway but could return the way he came. The restraint was not total; the case is the key authority for the total-restraint requirement.

Meering v Grahame-White Aviation Co. (1919): The source states that awareness of the restraint is not essential. Total restraint existed even though the plaintiff did not know guards were stationed outside. Awareness may affect damages.

Bhim Singh v State of J&K (1985): The source discusses wrongful arrest and detention by the State as both a tortious wrong and a constitutional wrong involving personal liberty under Article 21.

Methods of restraint may include physical barriers, force, threats or coercive authority exercised without lawful justification.

PART 11 — TORTS AGAINST REPUTATION: DEFAMATION

1. Meaning and Definition

Defamation is the publication of a false statement about a person that tends to lower that person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society. The source gives Winfield's formulation: a statement is defamatory when it tends to lower a person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society generally, or tends to make them shun or avoid that person.

MEMORY: DEFAMATION = DEFAMATORY STATEMENT + REFERENCE + PUBLICATION.

2. Essential Elements

Element 1 — Defamatory Statement

The statement must tend to lower the plaintiff's reputation in the estimation of right-thinking ordinary people. Sim v Stretch (1936) is given as the authority for the objective approach.

Element 2 — Reference to Plaintiff

The statement must refer to the plaintiff and the plaintiff must be identifiable.

Hulton v Jones (1910): A fictional newspaper character had the same name as a real person. The source states that liability could arise because reasonable people understood the publication as referring to the real plaintiff.

Knupffer v London Express (1944): In group defamation, an individual must be sufficiently identifiable from the statement; membership of a large group alone does not automatically establish individual reference.

Element 3 — Publication

The defamatory statement must be communicated to at least one person other than the plaintiff.

Theaker v Richardson (1962): The source uses this case to illustrate publication where a defamatory letter was read by another person in circumstances where such reading was reasonably foreseeable.

3. Libel and Slander

Libel is defamation in permanent or visible form. The source treats it as actionable per se and notes its criminal character in the traditional framework used in the material.

Slander is defamation in transient form, especially spoken words or gestures. The source states that it generally requires proof of actual or special damage, subject to recognised exceptions.

Slander Actionable Per Se — CDPU

4. Innuendo

Innuendo concerns a defamatory meaning that arises from facts or circumstances known to persons receiving the statement, even though the words may appear innocent on their face.

Cassidy v Daily Mirror (1929): The source uses this case to show that a publication may be defamatory by innuendo where people knowing surrounding facts would understand it as referring to the plaintiff and carrying a defamatory meaning.

PART 12 — TORTS AFFECTING FREEDOM

1. Malicious Prosecution

Malicious prosecution is the institution or continuation of legal/criminal proceedings against a person maliciously and without reasonable and probable cause, followed by favourable termination and damage.

MEMORY: PRMFDA = Prosecution + Reasonable cause absent + Malice + Favourable termination + Damage + Accused as Plaintiff.

Six Essential Elements

Prosecution by the defendant.

Absence of reasonable and probable cause.

Malice.

Proceedings terminated in the plaintiff's favour.

Damage to the plaintiff.

The plaintiff was the person proceeded against as the accused.

Abrath v North Eastern Railway (1886): The source states that the plaintiff bears the burden of proving absence of reasonable and probable cause.

Malice means an improper purpose, such as using prosecution for revenge, harassment or an improper advantage. Absence of reasonable cause may be evidence of malice but is not conclusive.

Favourable termination may be acquittal, discharge or discontinuance. The source states that conviction ordinarily indicates sufficient basis for the prosecution.

Damage may be to reputation, person/liberty, or property through expenses of defence.

Brown v Hawkes (1891): Honest belief in the plaintiff's guilt, if based on reasonable grounds, constitutes reasonable and probable cause even if the plaintiff is acquitted.

D.N. Banerjee v P.R. Mukherjee (1953): The source presents this Indian case as an illustration where a false complaint motivated by personal enmity led to prosecution, damage and eventual establishment of the required elements.

2. Malicious Civil Action

Malicious Civil Action, or malicious civil proceedings, means institution of civil proceedings maliciously and without reasonable cause, which terminate in the plaintiff's favour and cause damage.

3. Abuse of Legal Process

Abuse of legal process means using legal proceedings or legal machinery for a purpose other than the proper purpose for which the process exists.

Using an arrest-warrant application for an improper collateral purpose.

Executing a judgment oppressively.

Using legal process to extort money or harass another person.

The source identifies three core requirements: use for a collateral/improper purpose, resulting damage, and knowledge of the improper purpose.

PART 13 — DOMESTIC AND OTHER RIGHTS

1. Marital Rights

Enticement of spouse means wrongfully inducing a spouse to leave the matrimonial home and refuse to return, or persuading a spouse to desert the other spouse. The source notes that this was historically recognised at common law but has been abolished or severely limited in many modern jurisdictions.

Harbouring a spouse means wrongfully sheltering or keeping a spouse away from the other spouse with knowledge of the circumstances.

Criminal conversation historically referred to an action by a husband against a third party for adultery with his wife. The source notes that the action has been abolished in England and most modern jurisdictions and discusses the Indian position separately.

2. Parental Rights

Enticement of a child means wrongfully inducing a child to leave the custody and control of the parent.

The defendant persuaded or induced the child.

The child left parental custody.

The defendant knew the child was under parental custody.

Loss of service or other damage resulted.

Harbouring a child means wrongfully sheltering a child who has left the parent's home while knowing that the child should remain in parental custody.

Seduction of a child is described in the source as the historical action where seduction of a child/daughter still in parental service or custody could support an action based on loss of services.

3. Rights to Service

At common law, an employer or master could sue a third party who wrongfully enticed away, injured or harmed an employee/servant and thereby deprived the employer of the employee's services.

Per Quod Servitium Amisit = whereby he lost the service.

4. Contractual Rights — Inducing Breach

A third party may incur liability where, with the required knowledge and improper conduct, the third party intentionally induces another person to breach an existing contract.

Lumley v Gye (1853): The source identifies this as the leading case for inducing breach of contract.

5. Intimidation

Intimidation is the use of an unlawful threat or coercive pressure to cause another person to act or refrain from acting, resulting in legally recognised damage.

Rookes v Barnard (1964): The source identifies this as the leading case. Core exam point: unlawful threat + coercive pressure + resulting damage.

6. Conspiracy

Conspiracy in tort involves two or more persons combining together and causing damage to another. The source emphasises the predominant purpose test.

Quinn v Leathem (1901): Where the predominant purpose of the combination is to injure the plaintiff, the conduct may be actionable. Where the predominant purpose is to advance legitimate interests and injury is only incidental, the source states that the combination is not actionable on that basis.

PART 14 — TORTS AGAINST PROPERTY

1. Trespass to Land

Trespass to land is direct and unjustifiable interference with land in the possession of another. Every direct unauthorised entry is trespass even when no actual damage is proved; it is actionable per se.

Essential Elements

Direct interference.

Land must be in the possession of the plaintiff; ownership is not essential.

Interference must be without lawful justification or permission.

Entry must be intentional.

Forms of Trespass

Entering another's land without permission.

Placing objects on another's land.

Remaining after a licence is revoked.

Abusing a licence by doing something outside the permitted purpose.

Trespass below the surface, such as unauthorised digging.

Trespass above the surface, subject to statutory limits.

Trespass ab initio: where a person enters under legal authority but abuses that authority by doing something unauthorised, the law may treat the entry as trespass from the beginning.

Entick v Carrington (1765): Government officials entered and searched the plaintiff's home under a general warrant and seized papers. The source treats the entry as unlawful trespass and uses the case for the principle that even a very small unauthorised invasion of private property is trespass.

Trespass to Land vs Nuisance

2. Trespass to Goods

Trespass to goods is direct and intentional interference with goods in another's possession without lawful justification.

Scratching or damaging another person's vehicle.

Moving another person's goods.

Using goods without permission.

Wrongfully taking goods temporarily.

The source treats trespass to goods as actionable per se.

3. Conversion

Conversion is intentional dealing with goods in a manner inconsistent with the owner's right, effectively treating the goods as one's own. It is a more serious interference with the owner's rights than ordinary trespass to goods.

Stealing goods.

Selling another person's goods.

Refusing to return goods where the circumstances amount to denial of the owner's rights.

Destroying another's goods.

Using goods in a manner inconsistent with the owner's rights.

The source states that the plaintiff may claim the value of goods converted.

4. Detinue

Detinue is wrongful detention of another person's goods, especially refusal to return them after a demand for return.

MEMORY: T-C-D = Trespass to Goods (direct interference) → Conversion (treating as own) → Detinue (wrongful detention/refusal to return).
