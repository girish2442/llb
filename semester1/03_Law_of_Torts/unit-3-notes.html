PART 6 — NEGLIGENCE

1. Meaning and Definition

Negligence means carelessness in ordinary language. In tort law it means breach of a legal duty to take care which results in damage to the plaintiff.

Winfield: negligence is the breach of a legal duty to take care which results in damage to the plaintiff.

Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co. (1856): negligence involves omitting what a reasonable person would do, or doing what a reasonable person would not do.

2. Three Essential Elements

Duty of care — the defendant owed the plaintiff a legal duty to take reasonable care.

Breach of duty — the defendant fell below the applicable reasonable standard.

Damage — the breach caused actionable damage and the damage is not too remote.

3. Duty of Care — Neighbour Principle

A duty is owed to persons reasonably foreseeable as likely to be affected by the defendant’s conduct. The source uses Donoghue v Stevenson as the foundation.

Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) — Case Snapshot

Facts: Mrs. Donoghue consumed ginger beer from an opaque bottle and alleged that it contained a decomposed snail, causing illness.

Decision: The case established the modern neighbour principle.

Principle: A person must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions likely to injure persons closely and directly affected by the conduct.

4. Breach — Reasonable Person Test

The standard is objective: what would a reasonable and prudent person have done in the same circumstances?

The court considers the circumstances and the nature of the risk.

An accident alone does not automatically prove negligence.

Blyth v Birmingham Waterworks Co. (1856) — Case Snapshot

Facts: The case involved waterworks and damage occurring during an exceptionally severe frost.

Decision: The court explained negligence by reference to the reasonable person standard.

Principle: Negligence is measured by reasonable care in the circumstances.

5. Causation — But-for Test

Ask: “But for the defendant’s breach, would the plaintiff have suffered the damage?”

Barnett v Chelsea Hospital (1969) — Case Snapshot

Facts: A patient attended after ingesting arsenic and later died.

Decision: The hospital was not liable on causation because the patient would have died even with proper treatment.

Principle: The breach must be a factual cause of the damage.

6. Remoteness of Damage

Even where breach caused the damage factually, the defendant is not liable for every consequence. The source applies reasonable foreseeability of the TYPE of damage.

Wagon Mound No. 1 (1961) — Case Snapshot

Facts: Oil escaped into Sydney Harbour and later caught fire, causing damage.

Decision: The modern remoteness approach focuses on reasonable foreseeability of the type of damage.

Principle: Damage of a reasonably foreseeable type is generally recoverable; damage that is too remote is not.

Re Polemis (1921) represents the earlier direct-consequence approach; the source identifies the Wagon Mound approach as the modern foreseeability rule.

7. Thin Skull / Eggshell Skull Rule

Take the victim as you find him. If the type of injury is foreseeable, the defendant may be liable for the full extent even when the extent is unusually severe because of the plaintiff’s condition.

Smith v Leech Brain (1962) — Case Snapshot

Facts: A burn led to a much more serious consequence because of the victim’s unusual condition.

Decision: The defendant remained liable for the full consequences.

Principle: Foreseeability concerns the type of harm, not necessarily its precise extent.

8. Medical Negligence — Bolam Test

Medical negligence occurs when a medical professional fails to meet the applicable professional standard and that failure causes harm.

Bolam v Friern Hospital (1957) — Case Snapshot

Facts: The case concerned the standard expected from medical professionals.

Decision: The professional standard is assessed by accepted responsible medical practice.

Principle: The Bolam test asks whether the doctor acted in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of medical opinion.

Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha (1995) — Case Snapshot

Facts: The issue was whether medical services fall within “services” under the Consumer Protection Act, 1986.

Decision: The Supreme Court held that relevant medical services could fall within consumer-law coverage.

Principle: Medical treatment can therefore engage consumer-law remedies for deficiency in service, alongside tort principles, as stated in the source.

9. Contributory Negligence

Contributory negligence occurs when the plaintiff also fails to take reasonable care for his or her own safety and that conduct contributes to the damage.

It concerns the plaintiff’s own lack of reasonable care.

It differs from volenti: volenti concerns voluntary acceptance of risk; contributory negligence concerns failure to take reasonable care.

The source treats modern contributory negligence as involving reduction/apportionment rather than the complete defence associated with volenti.

10. Negligence — Proof Flow

Duty of care? → Donoghue / neighbour principle.

Breach? → reasonable person test / Blyth.

Factual causation? → but-for test / Barnett.

Remoteness? → foreseeable type / Wagon Mound.

Unusually severe extent? → thin skull / Smith v Leech Brain.

PART 7 — NUISANCE

Nuisance involves unlawful interference with rights connected with land. The source divides it into Public Nuisance and Private Nuisance.

1. Public Nuisance

Affects the community or a class of the public.

May concern public health, safety, comfort or public rights.

A private individual generally cannot sue merely as one of the public.

A private individual can sue where he proves special damage beyond general public inconvenience.

Winterbottom v Lord Derby (1867) — Case Snapshot

Facts: The defendant obstructed a public footpath and an individual attempted a personal action.

Decision: The individual could not sue merely as a member of the public without special damage.

Principle: Special damage particular to the plaintiff is required for an individual action for public nuisance.

Rose v Miles (1815) — Case Snapshot

Facts: A barge blocked a public navigable waterway, forcing the plaintiff to incur additional expense.

Decision: The plaintiff could sue because he suffered special damage.

Principle: Special damage beyond general public inconvenience supports an individual action.

2. Private Nuisance

Private nuisance is unlawful and unreasonable interference with a person’s use or enjoyment of land, or a right connected with land.

Encroachment such as roots or branches.

Physical damage such as flooding or structural damage.

Interference with enjoyment such as noise, smell, smoke, vibration or heat.

Essential features

Unreasonable interference.

Interference with use or enjoyment of land.

Usually continuous or recurring rather than a purely isolated event, according to the source.

Substantial interference in the circumstances.

3. Reasonableness Factors

Locality / character of the neighbourhood.

Duration and frequency.

Severity or intensity.

Social utility of the defendant’s activity.

Whether the plaintiff’s use is abnormally sensitive.

Malice or deliberate intention to cause harm.

Sturges v Bridgman (1879) — Case Snapshot

Facts: A doctor established consulting rooms near a confectioner whose machinery caused noise.

Decision: Locality and the character of the use were relevant to nuisance.

Principle: Coming to an existing nuisance is not by itself a defence; reasonableness depends on circumstances and locality.

Robinson v Kilvert (1889) — Case Snapshot

Facts: Heat from the defendant’s premises affected unusually sensitive brown paper.

Decision: The claim failed under the source’s abnormal-sensitivity principle.

Principle: Unusually sensitive use is generally not protected where ordinary use would not be substantially affected.

Hollywood Silver Fox Farm v Emmett (1936) — Case Snapshot

Facts: The defendant deliberately caused disturbance near a fox farm intending to interfere with the plaintiff’s business.

Decision: Malice was relevant and liability followed.

Principle: Malicious conduct can make interference unreasonable.

PART 8 — STRICT LIABILITY AND ABSOLUTE LIABILITY

1. Strict Liability — Rylands v Fletcher

Strict liability may arise without proof of ordinary negligence when the requirements of the Rylands v Fletcher rule are satisfied.

Core rule: a person who brings and keeps a dangerous thing on land, making a non-natural use of the land, must keep it at his peril; if it escapes and causes damage, liability may arise subject to recognised exceptions.

Essential elements

Dangerous thing brought onto or accumulated on the defendant’s land.

Non-natural or special use of land.

Escape from the defendant’s land/control.

Damage caused by the escape.

Rylands v Fletcher (1868) — Case Snapshot

Facts: The defendant constructed a reservoir; water escaped and flooded the plaintiff’s mine.

Decision: Liability was imposed under the rule despite absence of ordinary negligence.

Principle: A person making a non-natural use of land and keeping a dangerous thing there may be liable for damage caused by its escape.

2. Non-Natural Use

The source describes this as use going beyond ordinary use and creating a special or increased risk.

Examples in the source include large reservoirs, explosives and high-voltage electricity.

The nature and circumstances of the land use matter.

3. Escape

Escape is a key traditional requirement: the dangerous thing must move from the defendant’s land/control to a place outside it and cause damage. The source contrasts this with absolute liability.

4. Exceptions to Strict Liability

Act of God — extraordinary natural event.

Act of Stranger — escape caused by an independent third party outside the defendant’s control, subject to the rule’s requirements.

Plaintiff’s own fault.

Consent / common benefit.

Statutory authority.

Memory: God + Stranger + Consent + Statutory Authority + Plaintiff’s fault.

5. Absolute Liability — M.C. Mehta

The source presents Absolute Liability as an Indian doctrine for hazardous or inherently dangerous industries, creating a higher standard than Rylands v Fletcher.

M.C. Mehta v Union of India (1987) — Case Snapshot

Facts: Shriram Food and Fertilizers Ltd. operated a hazardous chemical plant in Delhi; oleum gas leaked and caused harm.

Decision: The Supreme Court developed the doctrine of absolute liability for hazardous/inherently dangerous enterprises.

Principle: Such an enterprise owes an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community; if harm results, the enterprise is absolutely liable to compensate.

Key features

No Rylands-type exceptions.

Non-delegable duty.

Hazardous/inherently dangerous industrial activity.

Source states compensation should be proportionate to the size, capacity and profitability of the enterprise.

Escape from land is not necessarily required in the same way as under Rylands.

PART 9 — LEGAL REMEDIES, DAMAGES AND REMOTENESS

1. Purpose

The primary purpose of tort remedies is generally to provide redress and restore the plaintiff, so far as possible, to the position before the wrong.

2. Judicial Remedies

Damages.

Injunction.

Specific restitution of property.

3. Types of Damages

Contemptuous — Smallest possible award where the plaintiff technically succeeds but the court disapproves of the claim/conduct.

Nominal — Small award for violation of a legal right without substantial actual loss.

Ordinary / Compensatory — Main monetary remedy intended to compensate and restore the plaintiff.

Aggravated — Additional compensatory damages where conduct increases injury to dignity, feelings or reputation.

Exemplary / Punitive — Beyond compensation; intended to punish and deter in appropriate cases.

4. Special and General Damages

Special damages = specifically quantifiable and proved losses, e.g. medical expenses, past earnings and repair costs.

General damages = losses not precisely calculable, e.g. pain and suffering, loss of amenity and mental anguish.

5. Compensatory vs Exemplary

6. Injunction

An injunction is a court order directing a person to refrain from an act or to perform an act.

Temporary/interlocutory — during the suit.

Perpetual/permanent — final order.

Prohibitory — stop doing something.

Mandatory — do something, such as remove an obstruction.

The source notes that injunction may be appropriate where damages are inadequate, especially for continuing nuisance.

Radhey Shyam v Gur Prasad (1978) — Case Snapshot

Facts: The case concerned continuing interference from a flour mill in a residential area.

Decision: A permanent injunction was granted in the source’s account.

Principle: Continuing nuisance may justify injunction where damages alone are inadequate.

7. Specific Restitution

Where a person is wrongfully dispossessed of land or goods, the court may order restoration of the property rather than merely its monetary value.

8. Extra-Judicial Remedies

Important: extra-judicial remedies must be exercised without breach of peace and with reasonable force. Excessive force may create liability.

9. Remoteness — Final Revision

Factual causation → But-for test → Barnett.

Legal remoteness → foreseeable TYPE of damage → Wagon Mound No. 1.

Thin skull → take victim as found → Smith v Leech Brain.

Re Polemis → earlier direct-consequence approach; Wagon Mound → modern foreseeability approach in the source.
