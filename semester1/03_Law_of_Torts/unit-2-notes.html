PART 4 — GENERAL DEFENCES

1. Meaning

General defences are recognised grounds by which a defendant may escape liability, or in the case of contributory negligence have damages reduced/apportioned, even after the plaintiff establishes the relevant tort.

2. Volenti non fit injuria — Consent

Volenti non fit injuria means 'to one who is willing, no injury is done.' If the plaintiff knows the risk and freely and voluntarily accepts that risk, the defence may prevent recovery for harm arising from that accepted risk.

Essential conditions

Knowledge of the relevant risk.

Free and voluntary acceptance of that risk.

Genuine consent, not obtained through fraud, compulsion or economic pressure.

The injury must fall within the scope of the risk accepted.

Hall v Brooklands Auto Racing Club (1933) — a spectator at a motor race was injured when cars entered the spectator area.

Principle: Attendance at the race amounted to consent to the ordinary risks of the event.

Smith v Baker (1891) — an employee continued working despite knowing of a danger.

Principle: Mere knowledge of a risk is not the same as voluntary consent, particularly where economic compulsion exists.

Haynes v Harwood (1935) — a rescuer was injured while responding to danger caused by the defendant.

Principle: Volenti does not ordinarily defeat a rescue claim merely because the rescuer knowingly faced danger.

When volenti fails

Knowledge without real choice.

Economic or other compulsion.

Rescue cases.

Fraud or misrepresentation affecting consent.

Harm beyond the scope of the consented risk.

MEMORY BOX — VOLENTI VKF-C = Knowledge + Voluntary acceptance + Free/genuine consent. Key rule: knowledge ≠ consent.

3. Contributory Negligence

Contributory negligence arises where the plaintiff's own negligence contributes to the harm. The source states that modern law does not completely bar recovery merely because of contributory negligence; damages may be apportioned.

Core elements

Plaintiff failed to take reasonable care for his/her own safety.

That failure contributed to the damage.

The consequence is reduction/apportionment of damages under the modern approach described in the source.

Last Opportunity Rule

The Last Opportunity Rule developed to soften the harsh older approach. The question becomes who had the last opportunity to avoid the accident.

Davies v Mann (1842) — the plaintiff left a donkey with its forelegs tied on a public road; the defendant drove at excessive speed and killed it.

Principle: Although the plaintiff was initially negligent, the defendant had the last opportunity to avoid the accident and failed to do so.

MEMORY BOX — CONTRIBUTORY NEGLIGENCE Plaintiff also negligent → contribution to harm → damages may be apportioned. Davies v Mann = Last Opportunity.

4. Act of God (Vis Major)

An Act of God is an extraordinary natural event which human foresight could not reasonably anticipate and human care could not guard against.

Three essentials

Purely natural cause — no human agency.

Extraordinary and unforeseeable event.

Impossible for reasonable human care or skill to guard against it.

Nichols v Marsland (1876) — extraordinary rainfall caused dams/lakes to burst and flood the plaintiff's land.

Principle: The rainfall was so extraordinary that the Act of God defence succeeded.

Ramalinga Nadar v Narayana Reddiar (1971) — goods were lost during communal riots and mob violence.

Principle: Riots involve human agency and therefore were not treated as an Act of God.

MEMORY BOX — ACT OF GOD NUH = Natural cause + Unforeseeable/extraordinary + Humans cannot guard against it.

5. Private Defence

Private defence permits reasonable force to protect oneself, another person or property from unlawful harm.

Conditions

Imminent/present threat.

Force must be necessary.

Force must be reasonable and proportionate to the threat.

It cannot be used as revenge for a past wrong.

The source cautions against deliberate harm to innocent third parties.

Bird v Holbrook (1828) — a spring gun set to protect tulips injured a trespasser.

Principle: The force was disproportionate; private defence failed.

Exam point: always write imminent threat + necessary/proportionate force + Bird v Holbrook.

6. Necessity

Necessity permits a lesser harm to be caused in order to prevent a greater and imminent harm. The source describes it as a practical exception where two interests conflict.

Conditions

Real and imminent danger of greater harm.

Harm caused must be proportionate and lesser than the harm prevented.

Defendant must reasonably believe the act is necessary.

Leigh v Gladstone (1909) — a hunger-striking prisoner was force-fed by prison authorities.

Principle: The act was treated as justified by necessity to preserve life and health.

Public and private necessity

Public necessity: action to protect the community, such as demolishing property to stop a spreading fire; generally treated as a complete defence in the source.

Private necessity: action to protect private interests; compensation for loss may still arise according to the source.

MEMORY BOX — NECESSITY Lesser harm to prevent greater harm = imminent danger + proportionality + reasonable belief.

7. Statutory Authority

Statutory authority is a defence where an act is authorised or required by statute and performed within the authority granted. The source states that the defence operates provided the act is done without negligence and in the authorised manner.

Two types

Absolute/Mandatory authority: the statute commands the act and leaves no real choice; inevitable consequences of the authorised act may be protected.

Conditional/Permissive authority: the statute permits but does not compel the act; reasonable care is still required.

Vaughan v Taff Vale Railway Co. (1860) — sparks from a statutorily authorised railway engine caused fire to the plaintiff's woods.

Principle: The railway was protected because the authorised operation was carried out without negligence and the relevant damage was treated as an inevitable consequence.

Limitation: statutory authority does not excuse negligence. It is not a licence for careless performance.

8. Inevitable Accident

An inevitable accident is an event that could not have been foreseen or prevented by ordinary reasonable care. Unlike Act of God, it need not be caused solely by natural forces.

Stanley v Powell (1891) — a shot glanced off a tree and accidentally injured another person.

Principle: The accident was treated as inevitable because reasonable care could not have prevented it.

9. Mistake

Mistake is generally not a defence in tort law according to the source. A person who interferes with another's legal rights remains liable even if the interference resulted from an honest mistake of fact.

MEMORY BOX — GENERAL DEFENCES Volenti → Contributory Negligence → Act of God → Private Defence → Necessity → Statutory Authority → Inevitable Accident → Mistake.

PART 5 — VICARIOUS LIABILITY

1. Meaning

Vicarious liability is liability imposed on one person for the tortious act of another because of a special legal relationship. The most important relationship is master-servant/employer-employee.

Latin maxims

Respondeat superior — 'Let the master answer.'

Qui facit per alium facit per se — 'He who acts through another, acts himself.'

Justifications stated in the source

Employer benefits from employee's work and should bear associated risks.

Employer exercises control over the employee.

Employer may have greater resources to compensate the victim.

The doctrine encourages responsible hiring and supervision.

MEMORY BOX — VICARIOUS LIABILITY VCB = Valid relationship + Course of employment + connection/benefit to master's business.

2. Master and Servant / Employer and Employee

The source uses the control test. A master controls not only what the servant does but also how the work is performed.

Control Test

Controls WHAT + HOW → employee/servant relationship.

Controls only result/WHAT → independent contractor, generally outside ordinary vicarious liability.

3. Course of Employment

The tort must be committed in the course of employment. An employee can remain within the course of employment even while acting wrongly or contrary to instructions, if the employee is still performing the authorised job.

Limpus v London General Omnibus Co. (1862) — a bus driver was forbidden to race other buses but deliberately obstructed a rival bus and caused an accident.

Principle: The employer was liable because the driver was still doing the authorised work of driving the bus; he used an unauthorised way of doing an authorised act.

4. Frolic of Own

A frolic of own occurs when the servant abandons the employer's business and acts purely for personal purposes. The employer is generally not vicariously liable.

Joel v Morrison (1834) — a servant deviated from the authorised route to visit a friend and caused an accident.

Principle: The servant was on a frolic of his own; the master was not liable.

Beard v London General Omnibus Co. (1900) — a bus conductor, not authorised to drive, drove the bus on his own initiative and caused an accident.

Principle: The act was entirely outside the conductor's employment; the employer was not liable.

5. State Liability

State of Rajasthan v Mst. Vidhyawati (1962) — a government driver negligently knocked down a pedestrian while returning an official vehicle to the garage.

Principle: The Supreme Court held the State vicariously liable because the driver was acting in the course of official employment.

Exam memory: Vidhyawati = Indian State vicarious liability illustration.

6. Principal and Agent

When an agent commits a tort while acting within the scope of authority, the principal may be vicariously liable. The source uses the maxim qui facit per alium facit per se.

Example: a sales agent fraudulently misrepresents goods while conducting the principal's business.

7. Partners

The source states that partners may be jointly and severally liable for torts committed by one partner in the ordinary course of the firm's business.

8. Loaned / Transferred Servant

When a servant is loaned to another employer, the key question is who controls the servant, especially how the work is performed. The source states that the general employer remains liable unless the borrowing employer has taken full control.

Mersey Docks v Coggins & Griffith (1947) — a crane driver was lent by the harbour board to another firm.

Principle: The original employer remained liable because it retained control over how the driver operated the crane.

9. Independent Contractor

An independent contractor is generally engaged to achieve a result and is not subject to the employer's detailed control over the manner of work. The general rule in the source is that an employer is not vicariously liable for the contractor's torts.

Exceptions in the source

Extra-hazardous/inherently dangerous work.

Non-delegable duties imposed by law.

Negligent selection of an incompetent or unqualified contractor — liability arises from the employer's own negligence.

Work affecting a public highway and creating danger/obstruction.

Strict liability situations where the employer cannot escape responsibility merely by appointing a contractor.

Honeywill & Stein v Larkin Bros (1934) — a contractor hired to take flash photographs in a cinema negligently caused a fire.

Principle: The source uses this as an example of extra-hazardous work and an exception to the general independent-contractor rule.

10. Owner of Vehicle — special point

The source notes that under the Motor Vehicles Act, 1988, an owner may be held vicariously liable for negligent driving by a person driving with the owner's consent even where the driver is not technically an employee. Detailed Motor Vehicles Act provisions are dealt with in Unit V.

General Defences

INEVITABLE ACCIDENT → Unavoidable despite reasonable care → Stanley.

MISTAKE → Generally no defence.

Vicarious Liability

Respondeat superior = Let the master answer.

Qui facit per alium facit per se = He who acts through another acts himself.

CONTROL TEST → WHAT + HOW = employee; result/WHAT = independent contractor.

COURSE OF EMPLOYMENT → Limpus = employer liable.

FROLIC OF OWN → Joel = employer generally not liable.

CONDUCTOR DRIVES → Beard = outside employment.

STATE DRIVER → Vidhyawati = State liable.

LOANED SERVANT → Mersey Docks = control decides.

INDEPENDENT CONTRACTOR → generally no liability, but exceptions must be remembered.

Common exam traps

Volenti: never equate knowledge with consent.

Act of God: human agency such as riots is not an Act of God under the source.

Private defence: proportionality is essential.

Contributory negligence: do not automatically write 'no compensation'; the source uses apportionment under modern law.

Course of employment: disobedience does not automatically end the employer's liability if the employee is still doing the authorised job.

Frolic of own: look for abandonment of the employer's business for a personal purpose.

Independent contractor: learn the exceptions; do not write 'employer is never liable'.

Statutory authority: statutory permission does not excuse negligence.
