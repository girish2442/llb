PART 16 — CONSUMER PROTECTION ACT, 1986

1. Learning Objectives

State objectives and salient features of CPA 1986.

Define consumer and identify who qualifies.

Explain defect in goods and deficiency in services.

Describe the three-tier redressal mechanism.

Explain remedies, limitation and penalties.

Write complete exam answers on core CPA topics.

2. Why Consumer Protection Law?

The source explains that before the CPA 1986, an aggrieved consumer generally had to approach civil courts, which could be expensive, time-consuming and procedurally complex. The Act was enacted to provide a simpler, speedy and inexpensive mechanism for redressal of consumer disputes.

3. Objectives of the CPA, 1986

Protect consumers from exploitation.

Provide simple, speedy and inexpensive redressal of consumer disputes.

Promote and protect consumer rights.

Establish consumer councils and dispute redressal agencies.

Deal with matters connected with consumer protection.

4. Six Consumer Rights

5. Who is a Consumer? — Section 2(1)(d)

Under the source’s CPA 1986 framework, a consumer is a person who buys goods for consideration or hires/avails services for consideration. Consideration may be paid, promised, partly paid and partly promised, or under deferred payment. Approved users/beneficiaries are also included in the circumstances stated in the Act.

Core test

BUY/HIRE + CONSIDERATION + permitted purpose = CONSUMER

Who is NOT a Consumer?

A person buying goods for resale.

A person obtaining goods/services primarily for commercial profit, subject to the self-employment livelihood exception described in the source.

Self-Employment Exception

The source states that a person using goods exclusively to earn livelihood through self-employment may still qualify as a consumer, illustrated by Cheema Engineering Services (1997).

6. Defect in Goods

Defect means a fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, purity or standard required by law or claimed by the trader in relation to goods.

7. Deficiency in Services

Deficiency means any fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality, nature or manner of performance of a service required by law or undertaken by contract.

Memory: DEFECT = GOODS; DEFICIENCY = SERVICES.

8. Medical Services under CPA

The source treats medical services as an important exam topic.

Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha (1995)

The Supreme Court held, in the source’s framework, that medical services fall within “services” under the CPA 1986. Patients who pay for medical treatment are consumers and can complain about deficiency in service, including medical negligence.

Spring Meadows Hospital case

The source uses Spring Meadows Hospital to illustrate medical negligence as deficiency in service.

Exam line: “Medical services are covered where they fall within the statutory concept of service; paid treatment can bring the patient within consumer protection.”

9. Government Bodies and CPA

Lucknow Development Authority v M.K. Gupta (1994)

The source states that government bodies providing services can be covered under the CPA. The case is an important authority for treating certain government service activities as subject to consumer protection.

10. Three-Tier Consumer Dispute Redressal Structure

Memory: D → S → N = District → State → National.

Procedure

Complaint in writing.

Notice to opposite party.

Hearing of parties.

Laboratory testing where required for goods.

Order by the forum.

Appeal may lie to the next level as provided by the Act.

The source notes disposal within 90 days, or 150 days where laboratory testing is needed.

11. Remedies under CPA 1986

Removal of defects.

Replacement of goods.

Refund of price.

Compensation for loss or injury.

Removal of deficiency in service.

Discontinuance of unfair trade practices.

Withdrawal of hazardous goods from sale.

Costs and other appropriate reliefs within statutory powers.

12. Limitation

The source states that a consumer complaint should ordinarily be filed within two years from the date on which the cause of action arises. Delay may be condoned where sufficient cause is shown.

Memory: LIMITATION = 2 YEARS.

13. Penalties / Non-Compliance

The source states that failure to comply with a consumer forum’s order may attract imprisonment of one month to three years and/or a fine of Rs. 2,000 to Rs. 10,000 under the 1986 framework.

Exam caution: use the figures as part of the source’s 1986 exam framework.

14. Unfair Trade Practices

For exam preparation, remember this as a separate CPA theme: practices involving false or misleading representations, deceptive selling methods, or other statutory unfair practices may attract consumer remedies.

PART 17 — MOTOR VEHICLES ACT, 1988

1. Learning Objectives

State salient features of MV Act 1988.

Explain liability without fault.

Understand compulsory motor-vehicle insurance.

Explain Motor Accidents Claims Tribunal.

Know major offences, penalties and claim procedure.

2. Introduction

The Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 provides a framework for regulation of motor vehicles and protection of accident victims. The source highlights compulsory third-party insurance, no-fault liability, specialised Claims Tribunals, and offence provisions.

3. Salient Features of MV Act, 1988

Compulsory third-party insurance.

Liability without fault in specified situations.

Motor Accidents Claims Tribunals.

Hit-and-run compensation framework.

Offences and penalties for road-safety violations.

Pay-and-recover principle in the source’s framework.

4. Compulsory Third-Party Insurance — Section 146

The source states that motor vehicles used in public places must have insurance covering third-party risks. The purpose is to ensure a financially responsible mechanism for compensation to third parties injured or killed in accidents.

Memory: SECTION 146 = COMPULSORY THIRD-PARTY INSURANCE.

5. Liability Without Fault — Section 140

This is one of the main Unit V topics. Under the source’s 1988 framework, compensation is payable for death or permanent disablement resulting from a motor-vehicle accident without requiring the claimant to prove negligence.

No need to establish negligence for the statutory no-fault amount.

The source states that contributory negligence is not a defence to the specified no-fault compensation.

The no-fault remedy is presented as additional to other compensation rights, subject to the statutory scheme.

Memory: NO-FAULT = 50K DEATH / 25K PERMANENT DISABLEMENT.

6. Section 163A — Structured Formula

The source also discusses Section 163A as a structured-formula route to compensation without requiring proof of negligence under ordinary fault-based tort principles.

7. Hit-and-Run — Section 161

Where the offending vehicle cannot be identified, the source states that compensation may be claimed from the Solatium Fund.

Memory: HIT & RUN = 25K / 12.5K.

8. Pay and Recover Principle

The source explains that where the insurer has a statutory defence against the insured, the insurer may still be required to pay the third-party victim first and then recover the amount from the insured, protecting the innocent victim from being left without compensation because of disputes between owner and insurer.

9. Motor Accidents Claims Tribunal — Section 165

The Act provides specialised Motor Accidents Claims Tribunals (MACT) for motor-accident compensation claims.

Jurisdiction

Death resulting from a motor accident.

Bodily injury resulting from a motor accident.

Third-party property damage resulting from a motor accident.

The source describes the Tribunal as having exclusive jurisdiction over such compensation claims once constituted.

Who may apply?

Accident victim.

Legal representatives of a deceased victim.

Authorised agent, as applicable.

Procedure — exam flow

Application → Notice to owner/driver/insurer → Insurance policy and defence → Evidence/inquiry → Award → Payment → Appeal.

Compensation

The source explains the multiplier method for death claims: annual dependency × age-based multiplier, with Sarla Verma v Delhi Transport Corporation (2009) identified as a case that standardised the method.

Important case: Sarla Verma v DTC (2009)

Use this case in answers on calculation of compensation and the multiplier method.

Statutory update — use this for the applicable KSLU batch

The earlier notes below preserve the historical Consumer Protection Act, 1986 presentation from the source document. KSLU Board of Studies revised Unit V from academic year 2021–22 to the Consumer Protection Act, 2019. For a student admitted in 2024–25, prepare the 2019 Act as the operative syllabus law. The 1986 Act is retained only for historical comparison; do not reproduce its old District Forum/State Commission/National Commission nomenclature or its old jurisdiction slabs as current law.

Current framework: Consumer Protection Councils; Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA); District, State and National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions; mediation; product liability; offences and penalties. Under the 2021 Jurisdiction Rules, pecuniary jurisdiction is based on consideration paid: District up to ₹50 lakh; State above ₹50 lakh up to ₹2 crore; National above ₹2 crore. Complaint limitation is generally two years under Section 69, subject to condonation.

Medical-services case note: Indian Medical Association v V.P. Shantha (1995) remains the established authority in many syllabi, but the Supreme Court in 2024 referred its correctness for reconsideration by a larger Bench. State the case and the referral qualification accurately; do not claim the precedent has already been overruled.

Motor Vehicles Act, 1988 — present-day distinctions

  • Section 140 is not the current no-fault compensation route; the 2019 amendment inserted Section 164, providing ₹5 lakh for death and ₹2.5 lakh for grievous hurt without requiring proof of wrongful act, neglect or default.
  • Hit-and-run compensation is administered under Section 161 and the 2022 Scheme: ₹2 lakh for death and ₹50,000 for grievous hurt (subject to any later notified change; check the current Scheme before exam use).
  • Section 146: compulsory third-party insurance. Section 149: insurer's duty to satisfy judgments/awards, subject to statutory conditions and defences. Section 165: Claims Tribunals. Section 166: application for compensation. Section 168: award of just compensation.
  • Do not quote the old Section 140 ₹50,000/₹25,000 figures, old hit-and-run ₹25,000/₹12,500 figures, old six-month bar, or pre-2019 offence fines as current law. For exact offences and penalties, consult the amended sections and commencement notifications.
