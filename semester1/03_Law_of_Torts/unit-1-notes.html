PART 1 — INTRODUCTION TO LAW OF TORTS

2. Introduction

Every civilised society needs rules to protect people from harm caused by others. When someone wrongfully harms another person — their body, reputation, property, or rights — the law must provide a remedy. This is the purpose of Law of Torts.

The word "Tort" comes from the Latin word "Tortum" which means "twisted" or "crooked" — something that is not straight, meaning a wrongful act.

In simple terms: If you wrong someone, you must pay for it. That is the essence of tort law.

3. Meaning and Definition

Ordinary Meaning

A tort is a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for damages (money compensation).

Important Definitions

Winfield's Definition:

Tortious liability arises from the breach of a duty primarily fixed by law; this duty is towards persons generally and its breach is redressable by an action for unliquidated damages.

Simple Version of Winfield: A tort is a breach of a legal duty that is fixed by law (not by agreement), owed to everyone, and for which you can claim damages.

Salmond's Definition:

A tort is a civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract or the breach of a trust or other merely equitable obligation.

Fraser's Definition:

Tort is an infringement of a right in rem of a private individual giving a right of compensation at the suit of the injured party.

4. Simple Explanation

Think of Tort as a mathematical formula. If you meet all the conditions, it equals a Tort.

Wrongful Act (You drive carelessly) + Legal Damage (The pedestrian is injured) + Breach of Duty (You failed your legal duty to drive safely)

= TORT LIABILITY (The pedestrian sues you for financial compensation)

In Tort → A private individual sues another private individual for compensation.

How it differs from the others:

Crime = Wrongful Act + State Prosecution = Punishment (Jail/Fines)

Contract = Broken Promise + Prior Agreement = Damages (Enforcing the deal)

5. Essential Elements of a Tort

For a tort to exist, three essential conditions must be satisfied:

A Wrongful Act or Omission (by the defendant)

Legal Damage (Injuria = legal injury)

Legal Remedy (Unliquidated damages)

Element 1: Wrongful Act or Omission

The defendant must have breached a legal duty. This can happen in two ways:

Wrongful Act (Doing something you shouldn't): e.g., Driving recklessly and hitting a pedestrian.

Wrongful Omission (Not doing something you should): e.g., A doctor failing to warn a patient about the severe risks of a surgery.

Element 2: Legal Damage (The Most Important Element)

Element 3: Legal Remedy

The primary remedy in tort law is unliquidated damages.

Note: "Unliquidated" means the amount is not pre-fixed or agreed upon beforehand (unlike a contract). The court decides the compensation amount based on the severity of the injury or loss.

6. Important Principles

The foundation of Tort Law is built upon a single, essential Latin maxim: Ubi Jus Ibi Remedium.

Meaning: "Where there is a right, there is a remedy."

Explanation: If the law guarantees you a right, and someone violates that right, the legal system must provide a remedy. The law dictates that no legal wrong should go unredressed.

Limitation: This maxim strictly applies to legal rights only. The violation of a purely moral, social, or religious right does not guarantee a legal remedy.

Landmark Case Application: > Ashby v White (1703) > The court heavily relied on the principle of Ubi Jus Ibi Remedium to rule in favor of the plaintiff. Even though the voter suffered zero financial loss when he was wrongfully prevented from voting, the court held that because his legal right was violated, the law was obligated to provide him a remedy.

2. Introduction

In criminal law, mens rea (guilty mind) is essential. But in tort law, the position is different. Generally, tort law is concerned more with the act than the mental state. However, mental elements — intention, motive, malice — do play a role in certain torts.

3. The Four Mental Elements

In Tort Law, the mental state of the defendant is categorized into four key concepts:

1. Intention

2. Motive

3. Malice in Law (Legal Malice)

4. Malice in Fact (Actual Malice)

A. INTENTION

Meaning: Intention means the desire to bring about a particular consequence. The defendant acts purposefully to cause the harm.

In Tort Law: Intention is relevant in some torts — such as assault, battery, false imprisonment, deceit, and malicious prosecution. However, unlike criminal law, you can be liable in tort even without intention (e.g., negligence).

Example: A deliberately pushes B into a ditch — intention is present. This is Battery.

Key Point: Intention goes to the act, not just the result. Even if you intend an act but not the consequences, you may still be liable.

B. MOTIVE

Meaning: Motive is the reason or purpose behind an act — why the defendant did what he did.

General Rule: Motive is generally irrelevant in tort law.

This means:

A good motive does NOT excuse a wrongful act

A bad motive does NOT make a lawful act tortious

Case: Bradford Corporation v Pickles (1895)

Exception: Motive becomes relevant in certain torts like malicious prosecution, deceit, and conspiracy.

C. MALICE IN LAW (Legal Malice)

Meaning: Malice in law does NOT mean hatred or spite. It means doing a wrongful act intentionally without lawful justification or excuse, even if there is no personal ill-will.

Simple explanation: The law presumes malice from the intentional doing of a wrongful act. You don't need to prove hatred.

Example: Publishing a false defamatory statement — even without personal hatred, the law will presume malice.

This is also called "implied malice" or "malice in law."

D. MALICE IN FACT (Actual Malice)

Meaning: Malice in fact means actual ill-will, spite, hatred, or an improper motive — the real mental state of the defendant.

Simple explanation: The defendant had genuine personal hatred or bad intention toward the plaintiff.

Where relevant: In torts like:

Malicious Prosecution — must prove actual malice

Defamation (Qualified Privilege) — actual malice defeats the defence

PART 3 — GENERAL THEORY: THEORIES OF TORT LIABILITY

1. Is Tort Law a Law of Torts or the Law of Tort?

This is a famous debate between two great jurists:

Winfield's View — "Law of Tort" (Wider View)

Winfield argued that there is a general principle underlying all of tort law: any unjustifiable harm is tortious unless there is a defence. New torts can always be created. The law grows.

Salmond's View — "Law of Torts" (Narrower View)

Salmond argued that tort law consists of a fixed number of specific torts. If your situation does not fall within a recognised category (negligence, defamation, nuisance, etc.), you cannot succeed.

Illustration:

Winfield: "Everything is a tort unless justified." (Like: all wrong unless proved right)

Salmond: "Only specific wrongs are torts." (Like: only listed wrongs are actionable)

Current position: English and Indian courts have generally sided with Winfield's view — new torts can and have been recognised (e.g., privacy torts, harassment).

Question: Define Tort. Explain its essential elements and distinguish it from Crime and Contract.

Introduction

The word "tort" is derived from the Latin word "tortum" meaning twisted or crooked. In legal parlance, a tort is a civil wrong, distinct from both crime and breach of contract, for which the remedy is an action for unliquidated damages. The law of torts aims to protect individuals from wrongful interference with their rights and to restore them to their original position through monetary compensation.

Definition

Winfield defines a tort as: "Tortious liability arises from the breach of a duty primarily fixed by law; this duty is towards persons generally and its breach is redressable by an action for unliquidated damages."

Salmond defines tort as: "A civil wrong for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages and which is not exclusively the breach of a contract or breach of a trust."

Essential Elements of Tort

Three essential elements must be present for an action in tort:

1. Wrongful Act or Omission The defendant must have committed an act which is legally wrongful, or omitted to do something which the law required him to do. A purely moral wrong is not sufficient.

2. Legal Damage (Injuria) The plaintiff must have suffered legal damage — a violation of a legal right. Two important maxims govern this element:

Injuria sine damno — Legal injury without actual damage is still actionable. In Ashby v White (1703), the plaintiff's right to vote was wrongfully denied, but his candidate won. The court held that violation of a legal right alone is sufficient — no actual loss is needed.

Damnum sine injuria — Actual loss without violation of a legal right is not actionable. In the Gloucester Grammar School case, the plaintiff suffered financial loss due to competition, but no legal right was violated, so no action lay.

3. Legal Remedy — Unliquidated Damages The remedy in tort is unliquidated damages — the court assesses and awards the amount of compensation. This distinguishes it from contract where damages may be liquidated (pre-agreed).

Conclusion

The law of torts thus occupies a unique place in civil law — protecting individuals against wrongful acts by providing compensation through court action. It is neither criminal law nor contract law, but a distinct area governed by the principle: ubi jus ibi remedium — where there is a right, there is a remedy.

Question: Explain Injuria sine damno and Damnum sine injuria with cases.

Introduction

In the law of torts, legal damage — not mere actual loss — is the foundation of an action. Two important Latin maxims deal with this: Injuria sine damno and Damnum sine injuria.

1. Injuria sine Damno

This maxim means "legal injury without actual damage." Where a plaintiff's legal right has been violated, he can sue even if he has suffered no actual financial loss. The violation of a right is itself sufficient.

In Ashby v White (1703), the plaintiff, a qualified voter, was wrongfully denied his right to vote by the defendant returning officer. Though the plaintiff's candidate won and no financial loss was suffered, the court awarded damages. Holt C.J. held that violation of a legal right must be remedied.

2. Damnum sine Injuria

This maxim means "actual damage without legal injury." Where a person suffers loss but no legal right of his has been violated, no action lies in tort.

In the Gloucester Grammar School case (1410), the defendant opened a rival school, causing financial loss to the plaintiff's school. But since the defendant had every right to open a school, no legal right of the plaintiff was violated and no action lay.

Conclusion

These two maxims together establish that in tort law, legal injury (injuria) is essential, not mere actual damage. Without violation of a legal right, no tort is committed, however great the actual loss.

Question: Explain the maxim Ubi Jus Ibi Remedium with a case.

Introduction

Ubi jus ibi remedium is a fundamental Latin maxim of tort law. It means "where there is a right, there is a remedy." This maxim forms the very foundation of the law of torts — it declares that no legal right can exist without a corresponding remedy for its violation.

Meaning and Explanation

The maxim has two parts:

Ubi jus = where there is a legal right

Ibi remedium = there is a remedy

Whenever a person's legal right is violated, the law must provide him a means of redress. If the law recognises a right but provides no remedy, the right is meaningless in practice. This maxim prevents such a situation.

Application

The maxim applies to violations of legal rights — not merely moral rights. If a person suffers a moral wrong but no legal right is violated, the maxim does not apply.

Important Case: Ashby v White (1703)

In this case, the plaintiff, a qualified voter, was wrongfully prevented from casting his vote by the defendant returning officer. The plaintiff's candidate actually won, so no financial loss occurred. The court, applying ubi jus ibi remedium, held that since the plaintiff's legal right to vote was violated, a remedy must be available. Damages were awarded even without actual financial loss.

Holt C.J. famously observed: if a man has a right, he must have a means to vindicate it.

Limitation

The maxim operates only where there is a recognised legal right. It does not apply to purely moral rights or social obligations that the law does not enforce.

Conclusion

Ubi jus ibi remedium is the bedrock of tort law. It ensures that every legal right is backed by an enforceable remedy, making the law of torts a practical and effective tool for protecting individuals against wrongful acts.

Question: Write a note on Motive in the Law of Torts.

Introduction

Motive refers to the reason or purpose behind a person's act — the "why" behind what was done. In criminal law, motive may be relevant to sentencing. However, in the law of torts, the general rule is that motive is irrelevant.

General Rule — Motive is Irrelevant

In tort law, what matters is whether a legal duty was breached and whether legal damage resulted — not why the defendant acted. This means:

A good motive cannot excuse a wrongful act

A bad motive cannot make a lawful act tortious

Leading Case: Bradford Corporation v Pickles (1895)

The defendant Pickles deliberately diverted underground water flowing through his land, with the clear malicious purpose of forcing the Bradford Corporation to buy his land at an inflated price. The Corporation sued, arguing his malicious motive made the act tortious.

The House of Lords held that since Pickles had a legal right over water on his own land, no tort was committed regardless of his bad motive. A lawful act cannot be made unlawful by a malicious motive.

Exceptions — Where Motive is Relevant

Motive becomes legally relevant in certain specific torts:

Malicious Prosecution — actual malice (bad motive) must be proved

Conspiracy — malicious purpose of the combination is relevant

Deceit — fraudulent intent (dishonest motive) is essential

Qualified Privilege in Defamation — actual malice defeats the defence

Conclusion

As a general principle, motive is irrelevant in tort law — the focus is on the act and its consequences. However, in specific torts where mental state is an element of the wrong, motive becomes directly relevant and must be proved.

Question: What is meant by Malice in tort law? Distinguish Malice in Law from Malice in Fact.

Introduction

The term "malice" in tort law does not always carry its ordinary meaning of hatred or spite. The law of torts recognises two distinct types of malice — Malice in Law and Malice in Fact — each with different meanings and legal consequences.

Malice in Law (Legal Malice)

Malice in law means doing a wrongful act intentionally without lawful justification or excuse, even if there is no personal ill-will toward the plaintiff. It is also called implied malice because the law presumes it from the very fact that a wrongful act was deliberately committed.

Example: Publishing a false defamatory statement about someone — even without personal hatred — attracts malice in law. The deliberate publication of a falsehood is itself sufficient.

Malice in Fact (Actual Malice)

Malice in fact means genuine personal ill-will, spite, hatred, or improper motive directed against the plaintiff. It is also called express malice or actual malice. Unlike malice in law, it must be actually proved — it is not presumed.

Example: Prosecuting someone for a crime knowing he is innocent, out of personal grudge — this involves malice in fact.

Question: Discuss the Mental Elements in the Law of Torts — Intention, Motive, and Malice.

Introduction

In criminal law, mens rea (guilty mind) is a central requirement. In the law of torts, however, the position is more nuanced. Tort law is primarily concerned with protecting legal rights and compensating victims — not punishing wrongdoers. Nevertheless, mental elements such as intention, motive, and malice do play a significant role in certain torts. These elements determine the nature of liability and the availability of certain defences.

1. Intention

Intention means the conscious desire to bring about a particular act or its consequences. In tort law, intention is relevant in specific torts such as assault, battery, false imprisonment, deceit, and malicious prosecution.

General Principle: A person who intentionally commits a wrongful act cannot claim ignorance of its consequences as a defence. Even if the exact harm was unintended, intentional commission of the act makes the defendant liable.

Example: A deliberately pushes B into a well intending to cause a fall — this is an intentional tort (battery). A is fully liable.

However, unlike criminal law, tort law does not always require intention. Negligence — one of the most important torts — is committed without any intention to cause harm. Thus, tort liability can arise from intentional acts, negligent acts, and even strict liability (without fault).

2. Motive

Motive is the underlying reason or purpose behind an act — the "why" of the defendant's conduct. The general rule in tort law is:

Motive is irrelevant — a bad motive cannot make a lawful act tortious; a good motive cannot excuse a wrongful act.

Leading Case: Bradford Corporation v Pickles (1895)

The defendant deliberately diverted underground water on his land to force the Bradford Corporation to pay him more money. His motive was clearly malicious. The House of Lords held that since the act (diverting water on his own land) was lawful, the bad motive was irrelevant. No tort was committed.

Exceptions — Where Motive is Relevant:

Motive becomes legally significant in: (i) Malicious Prosecution — improper motive must be proved (ii) Conspiracy — motive of the combination is central (iii) Deceit / Fraud — dishonest intent is an essential element (iv) Qualified Privilege in Defamation — actual malice defeats the defence

3. Malice

Malice in tort law has two distinct meanings:

A. Malice in Law (Legal/Implied Malice)

This is not actual ill-will or hatred. It means doing a wrongful act intentionally without lawful justification. The law presumes malice from the deliberate commission of a wrongful act — no proof of actual hatred is required.

Example: Deliberately publishing a false statement injuring someone's reputation — the law presumes malice from the deliberate publication, even without personal hatred.

B. Malice in Fact (Actual/Express Malice)

This refers to genuine personal ill-will, spite, or improper motive. It must be actually proved and is not presumed by law.

Example: Prosecuting an innocent person with the knowledge that he is innocent, out of personal revenge — this is malice in fact and must be proved to succeed in an action for malicious prosecution.

Significance of Mental Elements — Summary

Conclusion

Mental elements in tort law serve a more limited purpose than in criminal law. The primary focus of tort law remains on protecting rights and compensating victims. Intention is relevant in specific torts; motive is generally irrelevant but has important exceptions; and malice operates in two distinct senses — one presumed by law and one requiring actual proof. A clear understanding of these mental elements is essential for applying tort law correctly in complex situations.

Question: Trace the Evolution of Law of Torts in India.

Introduction

The law of torts in India does not have a comprehensive statutory code like the Indian Penal Code for criminal law. It has developed primarily through judicial decisions based on English common law principles, adapted to Indian conditions. The evolution of tort law in India reflects the growth of the judiciary's role in protecting individual rights and ensuring accountability — including of the State.

1. Colonial Foundation — English Common Law

When the British established courts in India, English common law was applied to disputes between British subjects and, in many cases, to all civil disputes. The Charter Acts and later the Civil Procedure Code allowed courts to apply principles of justice, equity, and good conscience — which meant English common law principles including torts.

Thus, Indian tort law began as a transplant of English law, with Indian courts following decisions of English courts as persuasive precedents.

2. Early Indian Cases — Adoption of English Principles

Indian courts readily adopted English tort law principles. Cases on negligence, nuisance, defamation, and trespass were decided following English precedents. The High Courts — particularly Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras — played a key role in developing early Indian tort jurisprudence.

3. Role of the Supreme Court — Expansion of Tort Law

After Independence, the Supreme Court of India significantly expanded tort law in several directions:

A. State Liability in Tort

Under English law, the Crown could not be sued in tort (sovereign immunity). The Indian Constitution (Article 300) provides that the Union and States may sue and be sued. The Supreme Court progressively reduced the scope of sovereign immunity.

In State of Rajasthan v Mst. Vidhyawati (1962), the Supreme Court held the State vicariously liable for the negligence of a government driver — treating the State like any other employer.

In Kasturilal v State of UP (1965), the court recognised sovereign immunity for acts of the State in exercise of sovereign functions — but later cases have steadily narrowed this immunity.

B. Constitutional Torts

The Supreme Court developed the concept of constitutional torts — where the State pays compensation for violating fundamental rights, even without a specific tort action.

In Rudal Shah v State of Bihar (1983), the Supreme Court awarded compensation under Article 32 for illegal detention — establishing that violation of fundamental rights attracts monetary compensation.

In Bhim Singh v State of J&K (1985), compensation was awarded for wrongful arrest and detention of an MLA.

C. Absolute Liability — Beyond English Law

The most significant Indian contribution to tort law is the doctrine of Absolute Liability developed in M.C. Mehta v Union of India (1987).

In this case, oleum gas leaked from a factory in Delhi causing deaths and injuries. The Supreme Court, speaking through Chief Justice Bhagwati, held that enterprises engaged in hazardous activities owe an absolute and non-delegable duty to the community. Unlike the English rule in Rylands v Fletcher (which allowed exceptions), the Indian doctrine of absolute liability allows no exceptions — and the compensation must be proportionate to the size and capacity of the enterprise.

This was a bold departure from English law and a major indigenous contribution to Indian tort jurisprudence.

4. Consumer Protection and Modern Developments

With industrialisation, individual tort actions became inadequate to protect consumers against large corporations. The Consumer Protection Act, 1986 (now replaced by the 2019 Act) created a parallel system of consumer redressal — influenced by tort principles but with simpler procedures.

5. Current Position

Today, Indian tort law is a blend of:

English common law principles (adopted through courts)

Indigenous Indian developments (absolute liability, constitutional torts)

Statutory modifications (Motor Vehicles Act, Consumer Protection Act)

Constitutional protections under Articles 21, 32, and 226

Conclusion

The evolution of tort law in India demonstrates a progressive journey — from a transplanted English common law system to an independent, indigenous jurisprudence. Indian courts, especially the Supreme Court, have not merely followed English law but have creatively developed it to meet Indian social and constitutional realities. The doctrine of absolute liability stands as the finest example of Indian tort law surpassing its English origins.

Question: Distinguish Winfield's view from Salmond's view on the Law of Torts.

Introduction

Two of the greatest jurists — Winfield and Salmond — hold fundamentally different views on the nature of tort law. Their debate centres on whether tort law is a general principle (Winfield) or a collection of specific wrongs (Salmond).

Winfield's View — "The Law of Tort" (Wider View)

Winfield argued that all unjustifiable harms are tortious unless there is a lawful excuse or justification. According to him, tort law is governed by a single general principle — any wrongful act causing damage is actionable unless specifically justified. New torts can therefore always be created by courts as society evolves.

Winfield's illustration: Like a tree that grows — tort law is a living, expanding body of law.

Salmond's View — "The Law of Torts" (Narrower View)

Salmond argued that tort law consists of a fixed list of specific, recognised torts — negligence, nuisance, trespass, defamation, etc. If your situation does not fit within a recognised category, you have no remedy. The law does not create new torts freely.

Salmond's illustration: Like a pigeon-hole system — your facts must fit into one of the existing holes.

Distinction Table

Current Position

Modern courts — both in England and India — have generally favoured Winfield's view. New torts (privacy, harassment, constitutional torts) have been recognised over time, showing that tort law is indeed a growing and expanding area.

Conclusion

While both views have merit, Winfield's broader approach better reflects the actual development of tort law in modern times — as courts continue to recognise new rights and new remedies in response to changing social needs.
